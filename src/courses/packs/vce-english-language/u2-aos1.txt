@ f7da46eb U2.1.1.1 | The history of English through key events
**In a sentence**
  English grew from the dialects of Germanic settlers into a global language, and each great event — Viking raids, the Norman Conquest, Christianity, plague, printing, dictionaries, typewriters and the internet — left its mark on its sounds, words and grammar.
**Key terms**
  **Old English (OE)**: English from about 450 to 1100 — highly inflected, with Germanic vocabulary.
  **Middle English (ME)**: about 1100 to 1500 — after the Norman Conquest, with heavy French influence and fewer inflections.
  **Early Modern English (EModE)**: about 1500 to 1800 — the age of printing, the Renaissance and Shakespeare.
  **Modern English (ModE)**: about 1800 to the present.
  **Incursion**: an invasion or influx of people bringing another language.
  **Language contact**: speakers of different languages interacting, leading to borrowing and change.
**The periods**
  **Old English**: Angles, Saxons and Jutes from northern Germany and Denmark settled Britain after the Romans left (from about 450). Inflected nouns and verbs, freer word order, runes then the Latin alphabet with extra letters such as þ (thorn) and æ (ash). Key text: "Beowulf".
  **Middle English**: after 1066 French was the language of the court, law and church; English survived among ordinary people and lost most of its inflections. Key text: Chaucer's "Canterbury Tales" (late 1300s).
  **Early Modern English**: printing, the Renaissance and exploration; spelling became more fixed; thousands of Latin and Greek borrowings; the Great Vowel Shift. Key texts: Shakespeare, the King James Bible (1611).
  **Modern English**: empire, industry and science expand the vocabulary; standard spelling and grammar codified; English spreads worldwide; recently the internet.
**Incursions (one elective option)**
  **The Vikings** (raids from 793; the Danelaw from the late 800s): Old Norse was close to Old English, so speakers mixed easily. Norse gave everyday words — "sky", "egg", "skin", "knife", "window" ("wind-eye"), "husband", "law" — and even the pronouns "they", "them", "their". Mixed communities simplified word endings, speeding the loss of inflections. Place names in "-by" (Whitby, Derby) and "-thorpe".
  **The Norman Conquest** (1066): William of Normandy's victory at Hastings made Norman French the language of power for about 300 years. About 10 000 French words entered English: government ("parliament", "tax"), law ("judge", "jury"), church, fashion and food. English kept the farm animals ("cow", "pig", "sheep") while French gave the meat on the table ("beef", "pork", "mutton"). By the late 1300s English was used again in law courts and Parliament (1362).
  **Religion**: Christianity (Augustine's mission in 597) brought Latin church and learning words — "altar", "candle", "school", "verse", "monk". The King James Bible (1611) spread phrases still used: "the salt of the earth", "a labour of love", "a law unto themselves".
  **Plagues**: the Black Death (1348–50) killed perhaps a third or more of England's people; labour shortages raised the status of ordinary English speakers, and English replaced French in schools soon after. COVID-19 is a modern example: "iso", "lockdown" and "social distancing" spread rapidly; "covidiot" was coined.
**Inventions (the other elective option)**
  **Gutenberg and the printing press**: Gutenberg's press (Mainz, about 1440); William Caxton set up the first press in England at Westminster in 1476. Printers chose London-based spellings, spreading one standard; some Flemish typesetters added quirks ("ghost" with an h). Spelling began to fix while pronunciation was still changing.
  **Dictionaries**: Robert Cawdrey's "Table Alphabeticall" (1604), the first English dictionary; Samuel Johnson's "Dictionary" (1755) fixed many spellings; Noah Webster's American dictionary (1828) created American spellings ("color", "center"); the Oxford English Dictionary (completed 1928) records historical use; the Macquarie Dictionary (1981) records Australian English.
  **Typewriters and keyboards**: the Sholes and Glidden typewriter (1870s) gave the QWERTY layout; typing standardised business letters; "cc" (carbon copy) survives in email; keyboards and phone keypads shaped abbreviations and predictive text.
  **The internet**: email, texting and social media created new genres and conventions — initialisms ("lol", "omg"), emoji, speech-like writing — and spread neologisms worldwide in days ("selfie", first recorded in Australia in 2002; "doomscrolling"; "to google").
**Worked example**
  Explain how the Norman Conquest changed English vocabulary, using an example.
    After 1066 Norman French speakers held power, so French words entered domains they controlled — law, government, the church and fine dining.
    The contrast "cow/beef", "pig/pork", "sheep/mutton" shows it: English-speaking farmers tended the animals (Old English words), while French-speaking nobles ate the meat (French words).
    Many English/French pairs survive with different levels of formality: "ask"/"enquire", "kingly"/"royal".
**Going deeper**
  English often has three words for one idea from three layers: Old English "kingly", French "royal", Latin "regal"; "ask", "question", "interrogate". The Germanic word is usually the most everyday.
  Dates for the periods are approximate — language changes gradually, and scholars choose slightly different boundaries.
**Worked example 2**
  Why do "they", "them" and "their" come from Old Norse?
    Old English "hīe", "him" and "heora" sounded very similar to other pronouns ("he", "him", "her").
    In the Danelaw, where speakers of English and Norse lived side by side, the clearer Norse forms "þeir" etc. were adopted and gradually spread.
    It's rare to borrow pronouns — a sign of how close the contact was.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling Shakespeare's English "Old English". It is Early Modern English.
  Giving exact dates for periods as if change happened overnight.
  Listing borrowings without linking them to the social context that caused them.
**Exam tips**
  For any event: who came into contact, what social change occurred, and which subsystems changed — with examples.
  You must know all the events, and your elective (Incursions or Inventions) in depth.
**Connections**
  English and the Indo-European languages: where Old English came from.
  Codification and Standard English: printing and dictionaries.
  The Great Vowel Shift; lexical, morphological, syntactic and semantic change.
**Practice questions**
  Explain the impact of the Vikings on English.
    Old Norse speakers settled the Danelaw and lived alongside English speakers. English borrowed everyday words ("sky", "egg", "knife") and pronouns ("they", "them", "their"), and communication between the two groups helped simplify inflections.
  How did the printing press affect English spelling?
    Printing spread uniform London-based spellings to many readers, helping standardise spelling, but it fixed spellings while pronunciation was still changing (e.g. during the Great Vowel Shift), creating mismatches like "knight".
  Give two examples of language change linked to the internet.
    New initialisms such as "lol", and conversion of nouns to verbs such as "to google"; also emoji and new words such as "selfie".
  How did the Black Death help English regain status?
    It killed a large part of the population, creating labour shortages that improved the position of English-speaking workers; English soon replaced French in schools and later in law and Parliament.
**Flashcards**
  Approximate dates of Old English? :: About 450 to 1100.
  Approximate dates of Middle English? :: About 1100 to 1500.
  Norman Conquest date? :: 1066.
  Who set up the first printing press in England, and when? :: William Caxton, Westminster, 1476.
  Name three Old Norse borrowings. :: "sky", "egg", "they" (also "skin", "knife", "window", "law").
  Why does English have "beef" and "cow"? :: French-speaking Normans ate the meat (beef); English-speaking farmers raised the animal (cow).
  Samuel Johnson's dictionary was published in {{1755}}.
  Name two words spread by COVID-19. :: "iso", "lockdown" (also "social distancing", "covidiot").

@ ee2fd113 U2.1.1.2 | English and the Indo-European languages
**In a sentence**
  English belongs to the Germanic branch of the Indo-European family, so it shares ancestor words — cognates — with languages from German and Dutch to Latin, Greek and Sanskrit.
**Key terms**
  **Language family**: languages descended from a common ancestor.
  **Indo-European (IE)**: the family including most languages of Europe and many of South Asia and Iran.
  **Proto-Indo-European (PIE)**: the reconstructed ancestor language, spoken thousands of years ago with no written records.
  **Germanic**: the IE branch including English, German, Dutch, Frisian and the Scandinavian languages.
  **Cognate**: a word descended from the same ancestor word as a word in a related language.
**The family tree**
  PIE (roughly 6000 years ago, reconstructed by comparing its daughter languages).
  Branches include: Germanic (English, German, Dutch, Swedish), Italic (Latin → French, Spanish, Italian), Hellenic (Greek), Indo-Iranian (Sanskrit → Hindi; Persian), Celtic (Irish, Welsh), Balto-Slavic (Russian, Polish, Lithuanian).
  English: PIE → Germanic → West Germanic → Anglo-Frisian → Old English. Its closest living relative is Frisian (spoken in the Netherlands and Germany).
**Cognates**
  English "father", German "Vater", Latin "pater", Greek "patēr", Sanskrit "pitṛ".
  English "night", German "Nacht", Latin "nox/noctis", Spanish "noche".
  English "three", German "drei", Latin "tres", Greek "treis".
  Cognates are not borrowings: they were inherited separately from a common ancestor.
**Borrowing on top of inheritance**
  English's core — "mother", "water", "house", "eat", "the", "and" — is Germanic.
  But so many French and Latin words were borrowed that roughly a quarter or more of English vocabulary is Germanic, with similar shares from French and Latin (plus Greek and others). The most frequent words, though, are overwhelmingly Germanic.
**Worked example**
  Show how cognates reveal that English and Latin are related.
    English "father" and Latin "pater", English "fish" and Latin "piscis", English "foot" and Latin "pes/pedis" match in meaning.
    Where Latin has p, English regularly has f — a systematic sound correspondence, not coincidence.
    This regular pattern shows both descend from one ancestor, PIE.
**Going deeper**
  Sir William Jones (1786) noticed that Sanskrit, Latin and Greek were too similar to be coincidence and must share a source.
  Grimm's Law (1822) describes the sound changes that set Germanic apart: PIE p → f ("pater"/"father"), t → th ("tres"/"three"), k → h ("centum"/"hundred").
**Common mistakes**
  Saying English "comes from Latin". English is Germanic; it borrowed heavily from Latin and French.
  Treating borrowings as cognates ("beef" in English and "bœuf" in French is a borrowing, not a cognate).
**Exam tips**
  Name the branch (Germanic) precisely and use cognate examples showing regular sound correspondences.
**Connections**
  The history of English: Old English as a West Germanic language.
  Lexical change: borrowings added to the inherited core.
  Tracing etymologies (key skill).
**Practice questions**
  Which branch of Indo-European does English belong to, and what are its closest relatives?
    Germanic (West Germanic); its closest relatives are Frisian, then Dutch and German.
  Distinguish a cognate from a borrowing, with examples.
    A cognate is inherited from a common ancestor ("father"/"Vater"); a borrowing is taken directly from another language ("beef" from French "bœuf").
  Why is English vocabulary so mixed when its grammar is Germanic?
    Contact with Norse, Norman French, and Latin through the church and learning added huge numbers of borrowings, but the core vocabulary and grammar stayed Germanic.
**Flashcards**
  What is Proto-Indo-European? :: The reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European languages.
  English belongs to which branch of Indo-European? :: Germanic (West Germanic).
  What is a cognate? :: A word descended from the same ancestor word as a word in a related language.
  English's closest living relative is {{Frisian}}.
  Give a cognate set for "father". :: Vater (German), pater (Latin), pitṛ (Sanskrit).
  Who first proposed that Sanskrit, Latin and Greek are related? :: Sir William Jones (1786).
  According to Grimm's Law, PIE p became {{f}} in Germanic.

@ 79cb2a20 U2.1.1.3 | Codification and Standard English
**In a sentence**
  Standard English wasn't handed down — one London-based variety was selected, spread by printing and then codified in dictionaries and grammar books, which is why English spelling is so irregular and some "rules" were invented in the 1700s.
**Key terms**
  **Standard English**: the variety used in formal writing, education and official contexts; in Australia, Standard Australian English (SAE).
  **Standardisation**: the process by which one variety becomes the accepted norm.
  **Codification**: setting down the norms of a language in dictionaries, grammars and style guides.
  **Chancery Standard**: the written English of London government clerks in the 1400s, an early basis for standard spelling.
  **Prescriptive grammar**: rules telling people how they should use language.
**How a standard develops**
  Selection: one variety is chosen — the London and East Midlands dialect, used by the court, government and printers.
  Elaboration: it's extended to all functions (law, science, literature).
  Codification: spelling and grammar written down.
  Acceptance: it's taught in schools and seen as "correct".
**Origins of English spelling**
  Old English spelling was fairly phonetic, but French scribes after 1066 introduced conventions ("qu" for "cw": "queen"; "ou" for /uː/: "house").
  Chancery clerks (1400s) and printers (Caxton, from 1476) spread consistent London spellings.
  The Great Vowel Shift changed pronunciation after many spellings had fixed — so "name", "bite" and "house" are spelt for old pronunciations.
  Renaissance scholars added letters to show Latin origins: "debt" (Latin "debitum"), "doubt" ("dubitare"); "island" got an "s" by false analogy with "isle".
  Samuel Johnson's "Dictionary" (1755) fixed many spellings; Noah Webster (1828) simplified American spellings ("color", "theater").
**Origins of English grammar rules**
  In the 1700s, writers worried English was decaying. Jonathan Swift (1712) proposed an academy to "fix" it.
  Grammarians such as Robert Lowth ("A Short Introduction to English Grammar", 1762) wrote rules, some modelled on Latin: don't split an infinitive ("to boldly go"), don't end a sentence with a preposition, avoid double negatives.
  These rules describe a preferred style, not how English actually worked — Shakespeare used double negatives freely.
**Standard Australian English**
  SAE follows international standard grammar but has its own vocabulary and spelling choices (British "-our" spelling in most contexts, "program" in computing), recorded in the Macquarie Dictionary and the Australian Government Style Manual.
**Worked example**
  Explain why "knight" is spelt with a "k" and "gh".
    In Middle English both letters were pronounced: something like [kniçt].
    Spelling was being fixed by printers and scribes in the 1400s–1500s.
    Afterwards, /k/ before /n/ and the "gh" sound were lost, but the spelling stayed — a fossil of the older pronunciation.
**Going deeper**
  No official body regulates English (unlike the Académie française), so dictionaries mostly describe current use — but many people treat them as authorities that prescribe.
  Every standard is a social choice: it's the variety of those with power, not a linguistically superior one.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying Standard English is the "original" or "purest" English. It's one dialect that became the norm.
  Thinking grammar rules like "never split an infinitive" were always part of English.
**Exam tips**
  Link each spelling or grammar feature to a cause: printing, the Great Vowel Shift, Latin-based respellings, dictionary makers.
**Connections**
  The history of English: printing and dictionaries.
  The Great Vowel Shift: why spelling and sound don't match.
  Prescriptivism and descriptivism.
**Practice questions**
  What is codification? Give two examples.
    Setting down a language's norms in writing — for example Johnson's "Dictionary" (1755) and Lowth's grammar (1762).
  Explain why "debt" has a silent "b".
    Renaissance scholars added the "b" to show the word's origin in Latin "debitum", although it came into English from French "dette" and had never been pronounced with /b/.
  Why is the "don't split infinitives" rule considered artificial?
    It was invented by 18th-century grammarians modelling English on Latin, where the infinitive is a single word and can't be split; English speakers have always split infinitives.
**Flashcards**
  Define codification. :: Setting down a language's norms in dictionaries, grammars and style guides.
  Which variety became the basis of Standard English? :: The London/East Midlands dialect (Chancery Standard).
  Who wrote an influential prescriptive grammar in 1762? :: Robert Lowth.
  The silent "b" in "debt" was added to show its {{Latin}} origin.
  Why does English spelling often not match pronunciation? :: Spellings fixed by printing before sound changes like the Great Vowel Shift finished.
  Who created American spellings like "color"? :: Noah Webster (1828).
  What does SAE stand for? :: Standard Australian English.

@ cb892226 U2.1.1.4 | The Great Vowel Shift
**In a sentence**
  Between about 1400 and 1700, English long vowels moved up in the mouth and the two highest became diphthongs — so "bite" once sounded like "beet" and "house" like "hoose" — while spelling stayed put.
**Key terms**
  **Great Vowel Shift (GVS)**: a series of changes to the long vowels of English, roughly 1400–1700.
  **Long vowel**: a vowel held for longer (in IPA marked with ː).
  **Raising**: a vowel moving to a higher tongue position.
  **Diphthongisation**: a monophthong becoming a diphthong (a glide between two vowel qualities).
**What happened**
  Each long vowel moved up (raised) one step; the two highest vowels, which couldn't rise further, became diphthongs.
    /iː/ → /aɪ/ (via intermediate stages): "bite" [biːt] → [baɪt] (HCE today /bɑet/).
    /uː/ → /aʊ/: "house" [huːs] → [haʊs] (HCE /hæɔs/).
    /eː/ → /iː/: "meet" [meːt] → [miːt].
    /oː/ → /uː/: "goose" [ɡoːs] → [ɡuːs].
    /aː/ → /eː/ → /eɪ/: "name" [naːm] → [neɪm].
  The changes happened over generations and varied by region — northern dialects shifted differently.
**Why it matters**
  Printing and scribal practice were fixing spellings during the same period, so English spellings record pre-shift pronunciations: the letter "i" in "bite", "ee" in "meet", "oo" in "goose".
  It explains why English vowel letters have different values from the same letters in most European languages (Italian or Spanish "i" is still /i/).
  Shakespeare's pronunciation was mid-shift; some of his rhymes that fail today worked then.
**Worked example**
  Explain why "bite" and "beet" sound different today but might once have sounded alike to modern ears.
    Before the shift, "bite" had /iː/ (like modern "beet").
    During the GVS, /iː/ diphthongised to /aɪ/, while "beet" (then with /eː/) raised to /iː/.
    The spellings "i" and "ee" preserve the older vowels.
**Going deeper**
  Nobody knows exactly why it happened. Suggested causes include social mixing after the Black Death, migration to London, and speakers imitating or avoiding French-influenced prestige pronunciations.
  The GVS is a "chain shift": when one vowel moved, others moved to keep words distinct.
**Common mistakes**
  Describing it as a sudden event rather than a gradual change over about 300 years.
  Saying it affected short vowels. It affected long vowels.
  Mixing up the direction: vowels moved up, not down.
**Exam tips**
  Give at least two examples with before-and-after IPA, and link the GVS to the mismatch between spelling and sound.
**Connections**
  Codification and Standard English: spelling fixed during the shift.
  Phonetics and the IPA (Unit 1): vowel height and diphthongs.
  The history of English: Middle to Early Modern English.
**Practice questions**
  Describe the Great Vowel Shift.
    Between about 1400 and 1700 English long vowels were raised; the highest, /iː/ and /uː/, became diphthongs (/aɪ/, /aʊ/). For example "house" changed from [huːs] to [haʊs].
  Why does the Great Vowel Shift help explain English spelling?
    Spellings were being standardised by printers during the shift, so they reflect the older pronunciations: "meet" keeps "ee" from when it was [meːt].
  What happened to the vowel in "goose"?
    It was raised from /oː/ to /uː/.
**Flashcards**
  When did the Great Vowel Shift happen? :: About 1400 to 1700.
  Which vowels did the Great Vowel Shift affect? :: The long vowels.
  In the GVS, long vowels were {{raised}}; the highest became {{diphthongs}}.
  "house" before the GVS? :: [huːs] ("hoose").
  "bite" before the GVS? :: [biːt] (like modern "beet").
  Why is the GVS important for spelling? :: Spelling was fixed before it finished, so spellings show older pronunciations.
  What is a chain shift? :: A series of linked sound changes where one vowel's move triggers others.

@ 46f29ca3 U2.1.1.5 | Morphological change: word formation
**In a sentence**
  New words are constantly built from old material — by adding affixes, compounding, blending, shortening, making acronyms and initialisms, and back-formation — and English itself lost most of its inflections over time.
**Key terms**
  **Affixation**: adding a prefix or suffix to make a new word ("un-" + "friend").
  **Compounding**: joining two free morphemes ("blackboard", "website").
  **Blending**: combining parts of two words ("brunch" = breakfast + lunch).
  **Shortening (clipping)**: cutting part of a longer word ("uni" from "university", "phone" from "telephone").
  **Abbreviation**: a shortened form of a word or phrase; includes acronyms and initialisms.
  **Acronym**: initial letters pronounced as a word ("scuba", "Qantas").
  **Initialism**: initial letters pronounced one by one ("ABC", "lol").
  **Backformation**: removing a supposed affix to create a new word ("edit" from "editor").
**The processes, with examples**
  Affixation: "unfriend", "ungoogleable", "re-tweet", "-ish" ("fiveish").
  Compounding: "blackboard", "smartphone", "sunscreen", "carpark".
  Blending: "smog" (smoke + fog), "motel" (motor + hotel), "Brexit" (Britain + exit).
  Shortening: "bus" (omnibus), "fridge" (refrigerator), "uni", "ad".
  Acronyms: "scuba" (self-contained underwater breathing apparatus), "laser", "Qantas" (Queensland and Northern Territory Aerial Services), "NATO".
  Initialisms: "ABC", "ATM", "lol", "omg".
  Backformation: "edit" (from "editor"), "burgle" (from "burglar"), "babysit" (from "babysitter"), "donate" (from "donation").
  Going further (Unit 2 metalanguage): conversion of word class ("to google", "a big ask") and contraction ("can't").
**Loss of inflections**
  Old English marked grammatical case with endings: "se cyning" (the king, subject), "þone cyning" (object), "þæs cyninges" (of the king).
  Through contact with Norse and the Norman period, most endings were levelled; by Middle English, word order and prepositions did their job.
  Survivors: plural "-s", possessive "-'s", pronoun case ("he/him"), a few old plurals ("oxen", "children", "feet").
**Worked example**
  Identify the process in each: "sitcom", "RSVP", "vax", "televise".
    "sitcom" — blend of "situation" + "comedy" (arguably a clipped compound).
    "RSVP" — initialism (from French "répondez s'il vous plaît").
    "vax" — shortening of "vaccine/vaccination".
    "televise" — backformation from "television".
**Going deeper**
  Australian English loves shortening with a suffix — "arvo", "servo", "brekkie", "Chrissie" — called hypocoristics (studied in Unit 4).
  Word-formation processes combine: "Brexiteer" is a blend plus affixation.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling "ABC" an acronym. It's an initialism; acronyms are said as words.
  Confusing a blend (parts of words) with a compound (whole words).
  Treating back-formation as ordinary shortening — back-formation removes what looks like an affix and creates a new word class.
**Exam tips**
  Name the process precisely and show how the word was formed (component words or the removed affix).
**Connections**
  Morphology (Unit 1): free and bound morphemes, affixes.
  Lexical change: neologisms often come from these processes.
  Syntactic change: loss of inflections led to fixed word order.
**Practice questions**
  Distinguish an acronym from an initialism, with examples.
    An acronym is pronounced as a word ("scuba", "Qantas"); an initialism is pronounced letter by letter ("ABC", "ATM").
  Explain backformation using "burgle".
    "burglar" looked like verb + "-ar" (like "beggar"), so speakers removed the "suffix" to create the new verb "burgle".
  How did the loss of inflections change English?
    Case endings that showed a word's grammatical role disappeared, so English came to depend on word order (SVO) and prepositions to show who did what to whom.
**Flashcards**
  "brunch" is formed by {{blending}}.
  "uni" from "university" is an example of {{shortening}} (clipping).
  What is backformation? :: Creating a new word by removing a supposed affix, e.g. "edit" from "editor".
  Acronym or initialism: "Qantas"? :: Acronym — pronounced as a word.
  Acronym or initialism: "lol"? :: Usually an initialism (said letter by letter), though some say it as a word.
  Give an example of compounding. :: "blackboard" (also "smartphone", "website").
  What replaced lost inflections in English? :: Fixed word order and prepositions.

@ 37f77d4f U2.1.1.6 | Lexical change: words in and out
**In a sentence**
  The English lexicon keeps changing: it borrows words from other languages, turns brand names into ordinary words, coins new words, keeps some old ones for special effect, and lets others fade away.
**Key terms**
  **Borrowing (loanword)**: a word taken from another language.
  **Commonisation**: a brand name becoming a common word for the product type.
  **Neologism**: a newly coined word or new meaning.
  **Archaism**: an old word or form still used in special contexts for effect.
  **Obsolescence**: a word falling out of use.
  **Nominalisation**: turning a verb or adjective into a noun ("decide" → "decision").
**Borrowings**
  English has borrowed from more than 350 languages.
  French: "government", "restaurant", "ballet"; Latin: "data", "agenda"; Greek: "telephone", "democracy"; Norse: "sky"; Italian: "piano", "pizza"; Japanese: "tsunami", "emoji"; Hindi: "shampoo", "bungalow"; Arabic: "algebra", "coffee" (via Turkish).
  Australian borrowings from Aboriginal languages: "kangaroo" (Guugu Yimithirr, recorded by Cook's expedition in 1770), "koala" and "boomerang" (Dharug), "yakka" (hard work, from Yagara).
**Commonisation**
  "esky", "biro", "hoover", "band-aid", "google" (to search), "zoom" (a video call). Companies often fight this because it can cost them trademark protection.
**Neologisms**
  "selfie" (first recorded in Australia, 2002), "doomscrolling", "mansplain", "iso", "rizz". Many come from word-formation processes; some are new meanings for old words ("cloud", "viral").
**Archaisms and obsolescence**
  Archaisms survive in law, religion, ceremony and fantasy: "whereas", "heretofore", "thou", "whilst", "betwixt".
  Obsolescence: "wireless" (for radio), "gramophone", "groovy"; words vanish when the thing disappears or a fashion passes. Some return ("wireless" for Wi-Fi).
**Worked example**
  Explain how "esky" became an ordinary Australian word.
    "Esky" was a brand name for a portable cooler (from the 1950s).
    It became so strongly linked to the product that Australians used it for any portable cooler, whatever the brand.
    This is commonisation: a proper noun (brand) becoming a common noun.
**Going deeper**
  Borrowings reveal contact and power: food, fashion and art words from French and Italian; trade words from Dutch ("yacht", "cruise"); colonial words from Indian and Aboriginal languages.
  Dictionaries decide when to add neologisms by tracking use over time — the Macquarie Dictionary's Word of the Year highlights new Australian words.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling any new-sounding word a borrowing — check its origin.
  Confusing archaism (still used for effect) with obsolescence (no longer used).
**Exam tips**
  For each change, give the example and the social cause: contact, technology, marketing, fashion.
**Connections**
  Morphological change: how neologisms are formed.
  Tracing etymologies (key skill).

**Common mistakes**
  Confusing broadening/narrowing (scope of meaning) with elevation/deterioration (positive or negative value).
  Giving the modern meaning without the earlier one — the change needs both.
**Exam tips**

**Connections**

**Key terms**

  **Descriptivism**: the view that linguists should describe how language is actually used, without judging it.
  **Complaint tradition**: the long history of people complaining that language is declining.
  **Standard**: the variety taken as the norm in formal contexts.
  **Appropriateness**: whether language suits its context, as opposed to whether it's "correct".
**Prescriptivism**
  Judges usage as right or wrong: "It's 'fewer items', not 'less items'", "Don't use 'literally' for emphasis", "'Could of' is wrong".
  Historically: Swift (1712), Lowth (1762), style guides, newspaper letters complaining about text-speak or Americanisms.
  Strengths: consistency and clarity in formal writing; a shared standard helps education and access.
  Criticisms: many "rules" are arbitrary; it can stigmatise speakers of non-standard varieties.
**Descriptivism**
  Describes and explains: "'Less' with countable nouns is common in informal speech"; "'Literally' is developing an intensifier meaning, like 'really' did."
  The approach of linguistics and of modern dictionaries like the Macquarie, which record words once they're widely used.

  Criticisms: some see it as "anything goes" — though descriptivists do recognise that some forms suit some contexts better.

**Going deeper**
  Prescriptive attitudes often target the speech of young people, women, migrants or lower-status groups — the complaint is often really about the speakers.

**Common mistakes**
  Presenting descriptivism as "there are no rules". Descriptivists study the rules speakers actually follow.
  Taking one side in an analysis question when you're asked to explain both.
**Exam tips**
  Use specific examples of each attitude, and discuss both views before reaching a judgement.
