@ 18e1e303 U1.2.1.1 | Stages of language development
**In a sentence**
  Children move through predictable stages — crying, cooing, babbling, one word, two words, telegraphic speech, then full sentences — while additional-language learners build a developing "interlanguage" shaped by the language they already know.
**Key terms**
  **Pre-linguistic stage**: the period before a child's first words (crying, cooing, babbling).
  **Cooing**: vowel-like sounds made from about 6–8 weeks.
  **Babbling**: consonant–vowel syllables ("ba", "da") from about 6 months, reduplicated ("ba-ba") then variegated ("ba-da").
  **Holophrastic (one-word) stage**: single words that do the work of whole sentences.
  **Two-word stage**: combining two words to express relationships ("more milk").
  **Telegraphic stage**: short utterances of content words with function words and inflections left out ("me want biscuit").
  **Interlanguage**: an additional-language learner's developing system, with its own rules, between the first language and the target language.
**First-language stages (ages vary widely)**
  **Crying** (from birth): the first vocal communication — different cries for hunger, pain, discomfort.
  **Cooing** (about 6–8 weeks): vowel-like sounds such as "ooo", "aaa", often in response to a carer.
  **Babbling** (about 6–12 months): reduplicated babble ("mamama") then variegated babble ("badagu"). Babbling gradually narrows to the sounds of the home language; deaf infants exposed to sign "babble" with their hands.
  **Holophrastic** (about 12–18 months): first words, mostly nouns for people, food, animals and toys, plus social words ("bye", "no"). "Juice!" can mean "I want juice" or "That's juice".
  **Two-word** (about 18–24 months): "Daddy gone", "big dog", "my ball" — expressing relations such as agent–action, possession and description. Vocabulary grows rapidly (the "vocabulary spurt").
  **Telegraphic** (about 24–36 months): "Mummy go shop", "Me want that" — word order is mostly right, but function words (articles, auxiliaries) and inflections are missing.
  **Post-telegraphic** (3 years+): function words and inflections appear; questions, negatives and complex sentences develop; over-generalisations ("goed", "mouses") show rules being learned; by about 5 a child has a large vocabulary and most adult grammar.
**Additional-language learners**
  A **silent period**, often in young children, when the learner listens more than speaks.
  **Formulaic chunks** learned whole ("How are you?", "I don't know").
  **Interlanguage**: rule-governed but different from the target ("He go yesterday") — it develops as the learner's rules change.
  **Transfer** from the first language: accent, word order, missing articles (e.g. speakers of languages without articles such as Mandarin or Vietnamese).
  **Fossilisation**: some features stop changing, even after years.
**Worked example**
  A child says "Doggie run!" and "Mummy shoe". Identify the stage and explain.
    Two-word stage (about 18–24 months).
    Each utterance combines two content words expressing a relationship: agent–action ("Doggie run") and possession ("Mummy shoe" = Mummy's shoe).
    Function words and inflections are absent — "the", "is", "-ing", possessive "-'s".
**Going deeper**
  Stages are a description of typical development, not strict boxes: children overlap stages, and the ages vary a lot between children without any problem.
  Comprehension runs ahead of production — toddlers understand far more words than they say.
**Worked example 2**
  An adult learner of English says, "Yesterday I go to shop and buy the rice." Explain two features using the idea of interlanguage.
    The missing past-tense inflections ("go", "buy") show a rule still developing — the learner marks time with "Yesterday" instead.
    The article is missing before "shop" and an unneeded "the" appears before "rice" — typical of transfer from a first language without English-style articles.
    These aren't random errors: they follow the learner's current rules.
**Common mistakes**
  Giving exact ages as if every child is the same. Always say "about" and allow for variation.
  Calling babbling "meaningless noise" — it practises the sounds of the home language.
  Calling a learner's patterned errors "random mistakes".
**Exam tips**
  Name the stage, give the approximate age, and point to features in the sample (word count, missing function words, over-generalisation) as evidence.
**Connections**
  Development across the subsystems: what changes at each stage in phonology, morphology, lexis, syntax and semantics.
  Universal grammar and usage-based theories: how each explains the stages.
  Child and adult learning compared.
**Practice questions**
  Describe the telegraphic stage and give an example.
    At about 2–3 years, children produce short utterances of mainly content words in the correct order but without function words and inflections, like a telegram: "Daddy go work", "Me want that".
  What is the holophrastic stage and why is it given that name?
    At about 12–18 months, children use single words to express whole ideas; "holophrase" means a whole phrase in one word ("Up!" = "Pick me up").
  Explain what interlanguage is.
    The developing language system of an additional-language learner. It has its own rules — influenced by the first language and by the learner's hypotheses about the target — and changes as they learn.
**Flashcards**
  Order the first-language stages. :: Crying, cooing, babbling, holophrastic, two-word, telegraphic, post-telegraphic.
  When does babbling typically begin? :: About 6 months.
  "Me want biscuit" is typical of the {{telegraphic}} stage.
  What is a holophrase? :: A single word used to express a whole idea.
  Reduplicated vs variegated babbling? :: Reduplicated repeats a syllable ("bababa"); variegated mixes syllables ("badagu").
  What is the silent period? :: A time when an additional-language learner listens more than speaks.
  What is fossilisation? :: Features of a learner's interlanguage that stop changing.
  Why do comprehension and production differ? :: Children understand many more words than they can say.

@ ccdd24f6 U1.2.1.2 | Universal grammar and usage-based theories
**In a sentence**
  Universal grammar says children are born with an innate grammar that input simply switches on; usage-based accounts say children build language from what they hear, using general skills for reading intentions and finding patterns.
**Key terms**
  **Universal grammar (UG)**: Noam Chomsky's theory that humans are born with knowledge of the principles shared by all languages.
  **Language Acquisition Device (LAD)**: the innate mental faculty Chomsky proposed for acquiring language.
  **Poverty of the stimulus**: the argument that the language children hear is too limited and messy to explain the grammar they acquire.
  **Principles and parameters**: universal principles, plus parameters (options) set by the language a child hears — e.g. verb before or after its object.
  **Usage-based accounts**: theories (associated with Michael Tomasello) that language is learned from use through general cognitive skills.
  **Intention-reading**: understanding what others mean and are attending to (joint attention).
  **Pattern-finding**: noticing regularities in what's heard and generalising from them.
**Universal grammar (nativist)**
  Children are born "wired" for language; the environment triggers it.
  Evidence claimed:
    Poverty of the stimulus — children learn rules nobody teaches them, and hear ungrammatical adult speech yet still acquire the grammar.
    Speed and uniformity — all normally developing children acquire their language in a few years, through similar stages, whatever the culture.
    Creative errors — "goed", "mouses": children apply rules they haven't heard, rather than copying.
    Children resist correction ("Nobody don't like me" repeated after correction).
    Creoles and new sign languages (Nicaraguan Sign Language) developed grammar when children were the learners.
    A critical period.
  Criticisms: UG is hard to test; its content has kept changing; it plays down the rich interaction children get; languages vary more than expected.
**Usage-based (social-cognitive)**
  Language is learned, like other skills, from **usage** — the language children hear in interaction.
  **Intention-reading**: joint attention (looking at the same thing), pointing, following gaze — infants learn words by working out what the speaker means.
  **Pattern-finding**: children track how often things occur and build categories: first item-based phrases ("more juice", "more bikkie" → "more X"), then broader constructions.
  Evidence: early grammar is tied to specific words; children's language mirrors the frequency of what they hear; joint attention at 12–18 months predicts vocabulary; children with more child-directed talk develop faster.
  Criticisms: struggles to explain how children avoid over-generalising wrongly, and how they learn structures that are rare in input.
**Using samples as evidence**
  Over-generalisations ("I runned") → support UG (rule-application) but usage-based accounts explain them as pattern-finding from frequent regular forms.
  Item-based phrases tied to particular words ("Where's Daddy?", "Where's ball?") → support usage-based accounts.
  Rapid, uniform development despite little explicit teaching → cited for UG.
**Worked example**
  A 2½-year-old says "Two foots!" after hearing "two cats", "two dogs". Explain how each theory accounts for this.
    UG: the child has an innate capacity for grammatical rules and has applied a plural rule (-s) productively — it can't be imitation, because adults don't say "foots".
    Usage-based: the child has found the frequent pattern "noun + -s = more than one" from the input and generalised it before learning the irregular exception.
**Going deeper**
  Chomsky's ideas date from the late 1950s and 1960s, partly as a reply to B. F. Skinner's behaviourist view that language is learned by imitation and reinforcement. Tomasello's usage-based theory was set out in "Constructing a Language" (2003).
  The theories aren't always opposites: many researchers accept some innate readiness for language and a large role for input and interaction.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying UG means children need no input. They need input to set parameters and learn vocabulary.
  Saying usage-based means "imitation". It involves active pattern-finding and intention-reading, not copying.
  Claiming a sample "proves" a theory. Samples provide evidence that supports or challenges a theory.
**Exam tips**
  Name the theory and the key idea, then link a specific feature of the sample to it: "The over-generalised 'goed' supports UG because…".
  In a discussion, weigh both theories and reach a judgement.
**Connections**
  Stages of language development: each theory explains the stages differently.
  The critical period: part of the case for UG.
  Development across the subsystems: over-generalisation in morphology and semantics.
**Practice questions**
  Outline the poverty of the stimulus argument.
    The language children hear is incomplete, full of errors and false starts, and rarely includes explicit correction of grammar, yet children still acquire a complex, rule-governed grammar. Chomsky argued this means some grammatical knowledge must be innate.
  Explain the role of intention-reading in usage-based accounts.
    Children learn words by understanding what others mean: through joint attention, pointing and following gaze they work out which object or action a speaker is referring to, and link the word to it.
  How might each theory explain the rapid growth of vocabulary around 18 months?
    UG: the innate language faculty is maturing and the child is ready to map words onto concepts quickly. Usage-based: improving joint attention and pattern-finding, combined with lots of input, let the child learn words faster.
  "Where's ball? Where's Daddy? Where's cup?" Which theory does this pattern support? Explain.
    Usage-based accounts: the child uses a fixed frame ("Where's ___?") learned from input and fills the slot with different words — an item-based construction built from use.
**Flashcards**
  Who proposed universal grammar? :: Noam Chomsky.
  What is the LAD? :: The Language Acquisition Device — an innate faculty for acquiring language, proposed by Chomsky.
  Whose theory is the usage-based account? :: Michael Tomasello (and others).
  The two key skills in usage-based accounts are {{intention-reading}} and {{pattern-finding}}.
  What is poverty of the stimulus? :: The argument that input is too limited to explain the grammar children acquire, so some must be innate.
  How does UG explain "goed"? :: The child applies an innate capacity for rules — the past-tense rule — to an irregular verb.
  What is joint attention? :: A child and adult attending to the same thing — vital for learning words in usage-based accounts.
  What are item-based constructions? :: Early frames tied to specific words, like "more ___".

@ 85f60740 U1.2.1.3 | Child and adult language learning
**In a sentence**
  Young children pick up languages naturally within a critical period, while adults usually learn them more deliberately and rarely reach native-like pronunciation — and growing up with several languages is normal, not confusing.
**Key terms**
  **Critical period hypothesis**: the idea that there is a window (roughly birth to puberty) during which language can be acquired naturally and fully.
  **First language (L1)**: a language acquired from birth.
  **Additional language (L2)**: a language learned after the first.
  **Simultaneous bilingualism**: acquiring two languages from birth.
  **Sequential bilingualism**: learning a second language after the first is established.
  **Multilingualism**: using more than two languages.
**The critical period**
  Eric Lenneberg (1967) proposed that the brain is especially ready for language until about puberty.
  Evidence:
    **Genie** — found in Los Angeles in 1970, aged 13, after extreme isolation; she learned many words but never mastered grammar.
    Deaf children who get access to sign language late show lasting grammatical difficulties compared with those exposed early.
    Migrants who arrive as young children usually sound native; those who arrive as adults usually keep an accent (Johnson and Newport, 1989, found grammar skills declined with age of arrival).
  Cautions: cases like Genie involve abuse and trauma, so language can't be separated from other harm; "sensitive period" may be more accurate than a sharp cut-off.
**Children vs adults**
  Children: acquire implicitly through immersion and interaction; native-like pronunciation; slower at first with vocabulary but eventually complete.
  Adults: learn explicitly (rules, study), use their first language and literacy, may progress faster at first, often keep an accent and some fossilised errors; motivation and exposure matter greatly.
**Bilingual and multilingual children**
  Simultaneous bilinguals reach milestones in the normal range across their two languages combined; mixing languages is normal and systematic.
  Sequential bilinguals often go through a silent period, then transfer skills from their first language.
  Myths: bilingualism doesn't cause language delay or confusion; keeping the home language supports learning English.
  In Australia, over a fifth of people use a language other than English at home (2021 Census: about 22%), the most common being Mandarin, Arabic and Vietnamese.
**Worked example**
  Anh moved from Vietnam to Melbourne aged 4, her father aged 35. Twenty years later, she sounds like any Melbourne speaker, while he still has a strong accent. Explain using the critical period hypothesis.
    Anh began learning English within the critical period, when phonology is acquired naturally from immersion, so she developed native-like pronunciation.
    Her father began after puberty; learning was more explicit and shaped by his Vietnamese phonology, so features of his first language persist (transfer and fossilisation).
**Going deeper**
  The critical period seems strongest for phonology (accent) and grammar, and weakest for vocabulary — adults keep learning new words all their lives.
  "Feral children" such as Victor of Aveyron (France, c. 1800) are often cited, but their cases are poorly documented.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying adults "can't" learn languages. They can learn them well; native-like accent and some grammar are what's hard.
  Presenting Genie's case as clear proof. Her severe deprivation affected far more than language.
  Treating bilingual code mixing as confusion.
**Exam tips**
  Compare on specific points (pronunciation, grammar, method of learning, speed, outcome) rather than general statements.
**Connections**
  Stages of language development: for first- and additional-language learners.
  Universal grammar: the critical period supports an innate, time-limited capacity.
  Code switching in language learning.
**Practice questions**
  Explain the critical period hypothesis and give one piece of evidence.
    It proposes a window up to about puberty when language can be acquired naturally and fully. Genie, isolated until 13, learned vocabulary but never full grammar, suggesting the window had closed.
  Give two differences between child and adult additional-language learning.
    Children acquire implicitly through immersion and usually reach native-like pronunciation; adults learn more explicitly, draw on their first language and literacy, and usually keep an accent.
  A parent worries that speaking Arabic at home will delay their child's English. What does research suggest?
    Bilingualism doesn't cause delay or confusion; children develop both languages normally, and a strong home language supports learning English.
**Flashcards**
  Who proposed the critical period hypothesis? :: Eric Lenneberg (1967).
  Roughly when does the critical period end? :: Around puberty.
  Acquiring two languages from birth is {{simultaneous}} bilingualism.
  What does the case of Genie suggest? :: Grammar may not be fully acquired if exposure begins after the critical period.
  Why is Genie's case limited evidence? :: Her abuse and deprivation affected more than language.
  Which aspect of language is most affected by the critical period? :: Pronunciation (phonology), then grammar.
  About what share of Australians use a language other than English at home? :: About 22% (2021 Census).

@ aad23e97 U1.2.1.4 | Code switching in language learning
**In a sentence**
  Code switching is moving between languages in one conversation, and for learners it's a normal, skilled strategy for filling gaps, matching the listener and showing what they know — not a sign of confusion.
**Key terms**
  **Code switching**: alternating between two or more languages (or varieties) within a conversation.
  **Code**: a language or variety of a language.
  **Intra-sentential switching**: switching within a sentence.
  **Inter-sentential switching**: switching between sentences.
  **Lexical gap**: a word the speaker doesn't know (or that doesn't exist) in the language they're using.
**Why learners code switch**
  To fill a lexical gap: "Can I have the cuchara? The spoon!"
  To match the listener: speaking Italian to Nonna and English to a friend, sometimes in the same breath.
  Because a word fits the context better in one language (a food, a family term, a school word).
  For emphasis or quotation: repeating a command in the home language to make it stronger.
  To show identity and belonging to a bilingual group (studied more in Units 3–4).
**What it shows about acquisition**
  Switches follow rules — bilingual children switch at points where both languages' grammars allow it, showing knowledge of both systems.
  Young bilinguals adjust their language to the person they're talking to by about age 2.
  It is part of normal bilingual development, not a failure to separate languages.
**Worked example**
  A 5-year-old says, "Mum, can I have more pho? It's so ngon!" Explain the code switching.
    "pho" — a culturally specific food word used in its Vietnamese form; it has no English equivalent in everyday use.
    "ngon" (Vietnamese "delicious") — an intra-sentential switch, perhaps because it's the word used for food at home, or for emphasis.
    The switch fits both grammars (an adjective after "so"), showing the child knows the structure of both languages.
**Going deeper**
  Code switching and "code mixing" are sometimes distinguished (mixing within a sentence or word), but VCE uses code switching for both.
  In the study design's metalanguage table, code switching appears in Unit 1 as language learning and again in Unit 4 as a marker of group membership and belonging.
**Common mistakes**
  Treating code switching as a deficit or confusion.
  Assuming only learners code switch — fluent bilinguals do it constantly.
  Not explaining the reason for the particular switch.
**Exam tips**
  For each switch, identify where it happens (within or between sentences) and suggest a reason (gap, listener, emphasis, identity).
**Connections**
  Child and adult learners: bilingual development.
  Stages of development for additional-language learners.
  Units 3–4: code switching as a marker of identity.
**Practice questions**
  Define code switching and give an example.
    Alternating between languages within a conversation, e.g. "I'll see you at the festa, OK?" using Italian "festa" (party) in an English sentence.
  Give two reasons a language learner might code switch.
    To fill a lexical gap when they don't know the word in the language they're using, and to match their listener (using the language the listener speaks).
  Why do linguists argue code switching shows competence rather than confusion?
    Switches follow the grammatical rules of both languages and are adjusted to the listener, which shows the speaker knows both systems and when to use them.
**Flashcards**
  Define code switching. :: Alternating between two or more languages or varieties within a conversation.
  Switching within one sentence is {{intra-sentential}} switching.
  What is a lexical gap? :: A word the speaker doesn't have in the language they're using.
  Is code switching a sign of confusion? :: No — it follows rules and shows knowledge of both languages.
  By what age do bilingual children adjust language to their listener? :: About 2.
  Give a reason for code switching besides a lexical gap. :: To match the listener, for emphasis, or to show identity.
  Inter-sentential switching happens {{between}} sentences.

@ 1777f3d4 U1.2.1.5 | Phonetics, phonology and the IPA
**In a sentence**
  The IPA gives each speech sound one symbol, and consonants are described by voicing, place and manner of articulation, vowels by height, backness and lip rounding — using the Harrington, Cox and Evans system for Australian English.
**Key terms**
  **International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)**: a set of symbols with one symbol for each speech sound.
  **Voicing**: whether the vocal folds vibrate (voiced, e.g. /b/) or not (voiceless, e.g. /p/).
  **Place of articulation**: where in the vocal tract a consonant is made.
  **Manner of articulation**: how the airflow is shaped or stopped.
  **Monophthong**: a vowel with one steady quality.
  **Diphthong**: a vowel that glides from one quality to another.
  **Phonemic transcription**: broad transcription of the sounds that change meaning, in /slashes/.
  **Phonetic transcription**: narrow transcription of the actual sounds produced, in [square brackets].
**Why the IPA?**
  English spelling is inconsistent: "ough" in "tough", "though", "through", "cough"; the sound /f/ in "fish", "phone", "laugh".
  One symbol per sound lets us record exactly what someone said — essential for child language.
  VCE uses the Harrington, Cox and Evans (1997) system for Australian English (HCE).
**Consonants — voicing, place, manner**
  Place (from the lips back):
    Bilabial (both lips): /p b m w/.
    Labiodental (lip and teeth): /f v/.
    Dental (tongue and teeth): /θ/ "thin", /ð/ "then".
    Alveolar (tongue and the ridge behind the teeth): /t d s z n l ɹ/.
    Post-alveolar (just behind the ridge): /ʃ/ "ship", /ʒ/ "beige", /tʃ/ "chip", /dʒ/ "judge".
    Palatal (hard palate): /j/ "yes".
    Velar (soft palate): /k ɡ ŋ/ ("sing" ends in /ŋ/).
    Glottal (vocal folds): /h/, and the glottal stop [ʔ] ("bu'er" for "butter").
  Manner:
    Stops (plosives): air completely blocked then released — /p b t d k ɡ/.
    Fricatives: air forced through a narrow gap — /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/.
    Affricates: a stop released into a fricative — /tʃ dʒ/.
    Nasals: air through the nose — /m n ŋ/.
    Approximants: articulators close but not enough for friction — /w j ɹ/ and the lateral /l/.
  Voicing pairs: /p–b/, /t–d/, /k–ɡ/, /f–v/, /θ–ð/, /s–z/, /ʃ–ʒ/, /tʃ–dʒ/. Nasals and approximants are voiced.
  Full description order: voicing + place + manner. /b/ = voiced bilabial stop; /s/ = voiceless alveolar fricative; /ŋ/ = voiced velar nasal.
**Vowels — height, backness, rounding (HCE)**
  Long monophthongs: /iː/ heed, /ɐː/ hard, /oː/ hoard, /ʉː/ who'd, /ɜː/ herd, /eː/ hair.
  Short monophthongs: /ɪ/ hid, /e/ head, /æ/ had, /ɐ/ mud, /ɔ/ pod, /ʊ/ hood, /ə/ the (schwa, in unstressed syllables).
  Diphthongs: /æɪ/ say, /ɑe/ high, /oɪ/ toy, /əʉ/ so, /æɔ/ how, /ɪə/ here, /ʊə/ cure.
  Height: high (/iː/) to low (/æ/, /ɐː/). Backness: front (/iː/, /e/) to back (/oː/, /ɔ/). Rounding: lips rounded (/oː/, /ʉː/) or spread (/iː/).
  /iː/ = high front unrounded vowel; /oː/ = mid-high back rounded.
**Connected speech processes (Unit 1)**
  Assimilation: a sound becomes more like its neighbour — "ten bikes" /tem bɑeks/.
  Vowel reduction: unstressed vowels become schwa — "to" in "going to go" /tə/.
  Elision: a sound is left out — "camera" /kæmɹə/, "family" /fæmli/.
  Insertion: a sound is added — linking r in "law and order" /loːɹ ənd oːdə/.
**Worked example**
  Transcribe "fish and chips" phonemically (HCE) and describe the first consonant.
    /fɪʃ ən tʃɪps/ — "and" reduced to /ən/ in connected speech (vowel reduction and elision of /d/).
    /f/ is a voiceless labiodental fricative.
**Going deeper**
  HCE uses /ʉː/ for "who'd" because the Australian vowel is central rather than back, and /ɐː/ for "hard", reflecting how Australians actually pronounce these vowels rather than British spelling-based symbols.
  Australian /t/ between vowels is often a quick tap, which is why "water" and "wader" can sound alike.
**Worked example 2**
  A child says [tɐn] for "sun". Describe the change.
    Target /s/ is a voiceless alveolar fricative; the child produced [t], a voiceless alveolar stop.
    Voicing and place are unchanged; only the manner changed (fricative → stop). This is called stopping.
**Common mistakes**
  Transcribing from spelling ("knee" as /kniː/ instead of /niː/).
  Forgetting that "th" is two different sounds, /θ/ (thin) and /ð/ (then).
  Using slashes and brackets interchangeably.
  Describing a consonant without all three parts (voicing, place, manner).
**Exam tips**
  Learn the consonant chart by place and manner; practise describing sounds in the order voicing–place–manner.
  When comparing a child's sound with the target, say which of the three features changed.
**Connections**
  Introducing phonology and discourse: phonology as a subsystem.
  Development across the subsystems: speech sound development.
  Interpreting samples and IPA transcriptions (key skill).
**Practice questions**
  Describe /d/ and /ŋ/ using voicing, place and manner.
    /d/: voiced alveolar stop. /ŋ/: voiced velar nasal.
  Transcribe "cat" and "sheep" phonemically in HCE.
    /kæt/ and /ʃiːp/.
  Explain why linguists use the IPA rather than English spelling.
    English spelling represents the same sound in different ways and different sounds with the same letters; the IPA uses one symbol per sound, so speech can be recorded exactly.
  Name and explain the connected speech process in "handbag" pronounced /hæmbæɡ/.
    Assimilation (and elision): the /d/ is dropped and /n/ becomes bilabial /m/ to match the following bilabial /b/.
**Flashcards**
  Three features used to describe a consonant? :: Voicing, place of articulation, manner of articulation.
  Describe /p/. :: Voiceless bilabial stop.
  Describe /v/. :: Voiced labiodental fricative.
  Three features used to describe a vowel? :: Height, backness and lip rounding.
  HCE symbol for the vowel in "hard"? :: /ɐː/
  The unstressed vowel /ə/ is called {{schwa}}.
  Phonemic vs phonetic transcription brackets? :: Phonemic /slashes/; phonetic [square brackets].
  Name the connected speech process: "ten bikes" /tem bɑeks/. :: Assimilation.

@ 0167f735 U1.2.1.6 | Development across the subsystems
**In a sentence**
  As children grow, their language develops in every subsystem at once — they simplify sounds they can't yet make, over-generalise grammatical rules, stretch and shrink word meanings, and build longer, more complex sentences.
**Key terms**
  **Phonological processes**: patterned simplifications children make to adult pronunciations.
  **Over-generalisation (morphological)**: applying a regular rule to an irregular form ("goed", "mouses").
  **Over-extension**: using a word for a wider range of things than adults do ("doggie" for all four-legged animals).
  **Under-extension**: using a word for a narrower range ("dog" only for the family dog).
  **Semantic over-generalisation**: another term for over-extension of meaning.
  **Mean length of utterance (MLU)**: the average number of morphemes per utterance — a measure of grammatical development.
**Phonology and speech sound production**
  Sounds tend to appear in an order: vowels, then stops and nasals (/p b m n t d/) and glides (/w j/) early; velars (/k ɡ/) around 2–3; fricatives and affricates (/f s ʃ tʃ dʒ/) later; /ɹ/, /θ/ and /ð/ last (up to about 7–8).
  Common processes:
    Deletion — final consonant ("ca" [kæ] for "cat"), weak syllable ("nana" for "banana").
    Consonant cluster reduction — "pider" [pɑedə] for "spider".
    Stopping — a fricative replaced by a stop: "tun" [tɐn] for "sun".
    Fronting — a velar replaced by an alveolar: "tat" [tæt] for "cat".
    Gliding — /ɹ/ or /l/ replaced by /w/ or /j/: "wabbit" for "rabbit".
    Assimilation — one sound becomes like another in the word: "gog" [ɡɔɡ] for "dog".
    Reduplication — repeating a syllable: "bo-bo" for "bottle".
**Morphology**
  Inflections appear in a rough order (Roger Brown's research): -ing and plural -s early; possessive -'s, regular past -ed and third-person -s later.
  Over-generalisation: "goed", "runned", "sheeps", "foots" — then correction. Children often say "went" correctly first, then "goed", then "went" again (U-shaped learning), showing a rule has been learned.
**Lexicology and semantics**
  First words: people, food, animals, toys, body parts, social words ("hi", "bye").
  Vocabulary spurt at about 18 months; roughly 50 words at 18 months, several hundred by 2, thousands by school age.
  Over-extension ("ball" for the moon and oranges), under-extension ("shoes" only for their own shoes).
  Content words first; function words later.
**Syntax**
  One word → two words → telegraphic → complex sentences; MLU rises.
  Questions: rising intonation ("Daddy go?") → wh-words ("Where Daddy go?") → auxiliary inversion ("Where did Daddy go?").
  Negation: "No bed" → "Me not go" → "I don't want to go".
**Discourse and pragmatics**
  Turn-taking starts in "conversations" of coos with a carer; later come politeness words ("please", "thank you" — often taught explicitly), topic maintenance and storytelling (about 4–5).
**Worked example**
  Analyse this sample from a 3-year-old: "Me goed to the zoo. I seed two big lions!"
    Morphology: over-generalisation of past "-ed" ("goed", "seed") — the child has learned the regular rule and applies it to irregular verbs.
    Lexicology: "Me" used as a subject pronoun — a common early non-standard form.
    Syntax: mostly complete, post-telegraphic sentences with determiners ("the", "two") and an adjective in the noun phrase ("two big lions").
    Stage: post-telegraphic.
**Going deeper**
  MLU is counted in morphemes, not words: "Doggies running" = doggy + -s + run + -ing = 4 morphemes.
  Children's "errors" are rule-governed and predictable, which is why linguists find them so revealing.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling over-generalisation "copying adults". Adults don't say "goed" — it's a rule the child created.
  Confusing over-extension (meaning too broad) with over-generalisation of a grammatical rule.
  Naming a phonological process without showing target and child forms.
**Exam tips**
  Present sound changes as target → child form with IPA, then name the process.
  Organise sample analysis by subsystem, with a quoted example each time.
**Connections**
  Phonetics, phonology and the IPA.
  Universal grammar and usage-based theories: explaining over-generalisation.
  Interpreting samples (key skill).
**Practice questions**
  A child says "wed" for "red" and "wun" for "run". Name and explain the process.
    Gliding: the approximant /ɹ/ is replaced by the glide /w/, a sound the child can already produce.
  Distinguish over-extension from under-extension, with examples.
    Over-extension uses a word too broadly ("daddy" for all men); under-extension uses it too narrowly ("cat" only for the family pet).

    It shows the child has learned the productive rule "add -ed for past tense" and is applying it — a stage before learning the irregular exception.
