@ 3d63b55a U1.3.1.1 | Bounded rationality, willpower, self-interest
**In a sentence**
  Behavioural economics finds that real people are only boundedly rational (they use mental shortcuts), have bounded willpower (they favour now over later) and bounded self-interest (they care about fairness and others) — so their choices depart from the traditional model in predictable ways.
**Key terms**
  **Behavioural economics**: the study of how psychological, social and emotional factors affect economic decisions.
  **Bounded rationality**: people's rationality is limited by the information they have, the time available and their mental capacity, so they use rules of thumb and are prone to biases.
  **Heuristic**: a mental shortcut or rule of thumb that simplifies a decision.
  **Bias**: a systematic, predictable error in judgement.
  **Bounded willpower**: people often lack the self-control to act in their long-term interest, even when they know what's best.
  **Present bias**: giving too much weight to immediate costs and rewards compared with future ones.
  **Bounded self-interest**: people care about fairness and others' wellbeing, not just their own gain.
  **Nudge**: a change in how a choice is presented that predictably changes behaviour without banning options or changing incentives much (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008).
**Bounded rationality**
  Herbert Simon (1950s) argued people "satisfice" — choose something good enough — rather than maximise.
  Anchoring: judgements are pulled towards the first number seen. In Tversky and Kahneman's experiment, people who saw a wheel stop at 10 guessed on average that 25% of UN countries were African; those who saw 65 guessed 45%.
  Framing: the same information presented differently changes choices ("95% fat-free" sells better than "5% fat").
  Loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains please (Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory).
  Endowment effect: people value things more once they own them. In a famous experiment, students given a mug asked about twice as much to sell it as others would pay to buy one.
  Status quo and default bias: people stick with pre-set options (default super funds, auto-renewing subscriptions).
  Choice overload: too many options can stop people choosing. In the "jam study", a display of 24 jams drew 60% of shoppers but only about 3% bought; 6 jams drew 40% and about 30% bought.
  Availability heuristic: judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind (fear of shark attacks vs road accidents).
  Herd behaviour: copying others (bidding wars, crypto booms).
**Bounded willpower**
  Present bias explains procrastination and under-saving: people plan to exercise, save or study "next week".
  Hyperbolic discounting: \$100 today is preferred to \$110 next week, yet \$110 in 53 weeks is preferred to \$100 in 52 weeks — impatience is strongest for the present.
  Walter Mischel's marshmallow test (1970s): many children couldn't wait 15 minutes to get two treats instead of one.
  Examples: gym memberships that go unused; buy now, pay later spending; smokers who want to quit.
  Commitment devices help (automatic savings transfers, pre-paid classes).
**Bounded self-interest**
  Ultimatum game: one player proposes how to split \$10; if the other rejects it, both get nothing. Traditional theory says any offer above zero should be accepted, but offers below about 20–30% are often rejected as unfair, and most proposers offer 40–50%.
  Dictator game: even when the other player can't reject, many people share some money.
  Real examples: donating blood and to charities, volunteering, tipping, boycotting firms seen as unfair, paying more for ethical or local products.
**Worked example**
  Identify which insight best explains each: (a) a shopper buys a \$120 jacket "reduced from \$300"; (b) Sam keeps meaning to switch to a cheaper phone plan but never does; (c) a customer refuses to buy from a store that raised umbrella prices during a storm.
    (a) Bounded rationality — anchoring on the \$300 "was" price makes \$120 seem a bargain.
    (b) Bounded willpower (present bias) and status quo bias — the small effort now outweighs the future savings in his mind.
    (c) Bounded self-interest — the customer gives up a convenient purchase to punish behaviour seen as unfair.
**Going deeper**
  These deviations are predictable, not random — which is what makes behavioural economics useful for designing policy and why businesses exploit it.
  Daniel Kahneman (Nobel 2002) and Richard Thaler (Nobel 2017) won the economics prize for this work.
  Some findings haven't replicated strongly (the jam study effect is smaller in later studies), so evaluate evidence carefully.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying behavioural economics shows people are "stupid" or "irrational". It shows they are human — limited information, time and self-control, and social motives.
  Mixing up the three bounds: shortcuts and biases = rationality; self-control = willpower; fairness and altruism = self-interest.
**Exam tips**
  Name the bound, name the specific bias or behaviour, and give an example — "bounded rationality: anchoring, e.g. the 'was \$300' price tag".
**Connections**
  The traditional view of the consumer: the model these insights challenge.
  Traditional vs behavioural economics.
  Government and business strategies that use these insights.
**Practice questions**
  Explain the concept of bounded rationality, with an example.
    People's ability to make rational decisions is limited by information, time and mental capacity, so they rely on heuristics that cause biases. E.g. anchoring: a shopper judges a \$150 price as cheap after seeing a crossed-out \$250.
  How does the ultimatum game challenge the assumption of self-interest?
    Traditional theory predicts responders accept any positive offer, but many reject low offers (below about 20–30%) as unfair, giving up money to punish unfairness — self-interest is bounded by fairness.
  Using bounded willpower, explain why many Australians don't make voluntary super contributions.
    Present bias: spending now feels much more valuable than retirement income decades away, so people delay saving even when they know it's in their long-term interest.
  What is loss aversion?
    The tendency for losses to feel roughly twice as painful as gains of the same size feel good.
**Flashcards**
  Three key insights of behavioural economics? :: Bounded rationality, bounded willpower and bounded self-interest.
  What is bounded rationality? :: Rationality limited by information, time and mental capacity, so people use heuristics.
  Judgements pulled towards the first number seen are {{anchoring}}.
  What is present bias? :: Over-weighting immediate costs and rewards relative to future ones.
  What does the ultimatum game show? :: People reject unfair offers — self-interest is bounded by fairness.
  Losses hurt about {{twice}} as much as equal gains please.
  What is a nudge? :: A change in how a choice is presented that shifts behaviour without banning options.
  Who wrote Nudge (2008)? :: Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein.

@ f73ae5b6 U1.3.1.2 | Traditional vs behavioural economics
**In a sentence**
  Traditional economics assumes rational, self-interested, fully informed utility-maximisers; behavioural economics adds evidence that people are limited and social, and the two together explain decisions better than either alone.
**Key terms**
  **Traditional (neoclassical) economics**: models agents as rational, self-interested and fully informed, maximising utility or profit.
  **Homo economicus**: the "economic man" of traditional models.
  **Complement**: to add to and improve something rather than replace it.
**The key differences**
  Rationality: traditional — consistent, logical, calculating; behavioural — bounded, using heuristics, prone to biases.
  Information: traditional — perfect; behavioural — limited, and people misread it (framing).
  Self-control: traditional — people act on their long-term preferences; behavioural — bounded willpower, present bias.
  Motives: traditional — self-interest; behavioural — also fairness, altruism, social norms.
  Context: traditional — how a choice is presented doesn't matter; behavioural — defaults, framing and order matter a lot.
  Method: traditional — theory and models; behavioural — experiments and field trials, drawing on psychology.
**Why they complement each other**
  Traditional models still predict many outcomes well (the law of demand holds; higher tobacco taxes do cut smoking).
  Behavioural insights explain where they fail (why people don't switch energy plans despite savings) and suggest cheaper policies (defaults, reminders).
  Policy often uses both: a tax (traditional incentive) plus graphic warnings (behavioural framing).
**Worked example**
  Explain how each approach would predict responses to a new law making superannuation contributions voluntary instead of compulsory.
    Traditional: rational workers would save the amount that maximises lifetime utility, so savings might fall only slightly.
    Behavioural: present bias and inertia mean many would stop saving, leading to much lower retirement incomes — which is why compulsion and defaults exist.
    Conclusion: behavioural insights explain why compulsory super and defaults are used.
**Going deeper**
  Behavioural economics doesn't reject rationality; it describes the conditions under which people are more or less rational (familiar, frequent, simple choices vs rare, complex, long-term ones).
**Common mistakes**
  Writing that behavioural economics "proves traditional economics wrong". The study design stresses that it complements it.
  Listing differences without examples.
**Exam tips**
  Use a table structure in words: for each assumption, state the traditional view, the behavioural view, and an example.
**Connections**
  The traditional view of the consumer.
  Bounded rationality, willpower, self-interest.
  Government nudges and their effectiveness.
**Practice questions**
  Outline two differences between traditional and behavioural economics.
    Traditional economics assumes people are fully rational and informed; behavioural economics finds rationality is bounded, so people use shortcuts and are affected by framing. Traditional economics assumes self-interest; behavioural economics finds people also care about fairness.
  Explain how behavioural economics complements traditional economics.
    It keeps the traditional framework where it predicts well but adds explanations for systematic departures, such as inertia and present bias, improving predictions and policy design.
  Why might a traditional economist predict that few people would need reminders to pay a bill?
    Rational, informed agents would pay to avoid late fees; the model doesn't allow for forgetfulness or present bias.
**Flashcards**
  Traditional view of rationality? :: Fully rational, consistent and informed.
  Behavioural view of rationality? :: Bounded — heuristics and biases.
  Behavioural economics {{complements}} traditional economics.
  Main method of behavioural economics? :: Experiments and field trials.
  Does framing matter in the traditional model? :: No — only the options and their costs and benefits.
  Example where traditional theory still predicts well? :: Higher tobacco taxes reduce smoking (law of demand).
  Motive traditional economics assumes? :: Self-interest.

@ 0bccfbb7 U1.3.1.3 | Government nudges and their effectiveness
**In a sentence**
  Governments use behavioural insights — defaults, framing, social norms, simplification and reminders — to steer people towards better choices, and these nudges work best when they are cheap, well targeted and tested.
**Key terms**
  **Choice architecture**: the way options are presented, which affects what people choose.
  **Default option**: what happens if a person makes no active choice.
  **Social norm**: what most people do or approve of, which influences behaviour.
  **BETA**: the Behavioural Economics Team of the Australian Government, set up in 2016 in the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet to design and test behavioural policies.
  **Randomised controlled trial (RCT)**: an experiment comparing a group that gets a policy with an otherwise similar group that doesn't.
**Strategies and examples**
  Defaults: compulsory superannuation (the Superannuation Guarantee reached 12% of wages on 1 July 2025) and MySuper default funds; since 2021, "stapling" a worker's existing super fund to each new job stops duplicate accounts and fees.
  Framing and salience: plain packaging of tobacco (a world first in 2012) with large graphic health warnings; Health Star Ratings on food; energy rating stickers on appliances.
  Social norms: BETA and the Chief Medical Officer wrote to the GPs prescribing the most antibiotics, comparing them with their peers — prescriptions fell about 12% over six months in the 2017 trial. The ATO uses "most people pay on time" messages.
  Simplification: pre-filled tax returns; one-page fact sheets; the Energy Made Easy comparison website.
  Reminders and commitment: text reminders for medical appointments and fines.
  Small incentives with behavioural design: Victoria's container deposit scheme (CDS Vic, from November 2023) pays 10c per eligible drink container returned.
**Judging effectiveness**
  Evidence: did an RCT or data show a change? (antibiotics: yes — a measurable fall.)
  Size and duration: do effects persist or fade? (Reminder effects often fade.)
  Cost: nudges are usually cheap compared with regulation or large subsidies.
  Reach and equity: who responds, and who doesn't? (Defaults help people least likely to act.)
  Unintended effects and ethics: critics call nudges paternalistic or manipulative; transparency matters.
**Worked example**
  Evaluate the effectiveness of tobacco plain packaging as a behavioural strategy.
    Insight: framing and salience — removing attractive branding and adding large graphic warnings makes the health costs vivid and the product less appealing.
    Evidence: smoking rates kept falling after 2012 (daily smoking among adults is now around one in ten), though tax rises at the same time make it hard to separate effects.
    Cost: low to government; other countries (UK, France, New Zealand) copied it.
    Limitations: addicted smokers respond less; illegal tobacco and vaping have grown.
    Judgement: fairly effective as part of a package with taxes and bans.
**Going deeper**
  Nudges are not a substitute for all regulation: for big problems (emissions), small nudges have small effects, and prices or rules may be needed.
  "Sludge" is the opposite of a nudge: friction that makes good choices harder (long forms to claim a rebate).
**Common mistakes**
  Calling any government policy a nudge. A tax or ban isn't a nudge; defaults, framing and reminders are.
  Evaluating without evidence — use data or trial results.
**Exam tips**
  Name the insight the strategy uses, describe the strategy, then evaluate with criteria: evidence, cost, reach, persistence, unintended effects.
**Connections**
  Bounded rationality, willpower, self-interest.
  Consumers, workers and incentives.
  Business nudges and their effectiveness.
**Practice questions**
  Explain how the government uses default options to influence behaviour, with an example.
    It sets the choice that applies if people do nothing. Superannuation contributions (12% of wages from July 2025) are made automatically into a default fund, so people save for retirement despite present bias and inertia.
  Describe the 2017 BETA antibiotic trial and its result.
    Letters comparing high-prescribing GPs with their peers used social norms; antibiotic prescriptions fell by about 12% over six months compared with the control group.
  Outline two criteria for evaluating a government nudge.
    Evidence of effect (ideally from a randomised trial) and cost relative to other policies (also persistence, equity and unintended effects).
**Flashcards**
  What is choice architecture? :: The way options are presented, which affects what people choose.
  Superannuation Guarantee rate from 1 July 2025? :: 12% of wages.
  Plain packaging of tobacco began in Australia in {{2012}}.
  What is BETA? :: The Behavioural Economics Team of the Australian Government (2016).
  Result of the 2017 antibiotic letters trial? :: About a 12% fall in prescriptions over six months.
  Is a tax a nudge? :: No — nudges change presentation, not incentives much.
  Refund per container under CDS Vic? :: 10 cents.
  What is "sludge"? :: Friction that makes good choices harder.

@ 41788111 U1.3.1.4 | Business nudges and their effectiveness
**In a sentence**
  Businesses use behavioural insights — anchoring, decoys, framing, scarcity, defaults and loyalty schemes — to increase sales and profits, often at the expense of consumers' true interests.
**Key terms**
  **Anchoring**: setting a reference price so another price looks attractive.
  **Decoy pricing**: adding an option designed to make another option look better value.
  **Charm pricing**: prices just below a round number (\$9.99).
  **Scarcity and urgency cues**: "only 2 left", countdown timers.
  **Drip pricing**: showing a low headline price and adding compulsory fees later.
  **Subscription trap**: free trials that roll into paid subscriptions and are hard to cancel.
**Strategies and examples**
  Anchoring: crossed-out "was" prices and "RRP" tags (the Coles "Down Down" case turned on whether the "was" price was genuine).
  Decoy pricing: Dan Ariely's Economist experiment — web only \$59, print only \$125, print + web \$125. With the decoy (print only), 84% chose print + web; without it, 68% chose web only.
  Framing: "95% fat-free", "save \$50", "only \$1 a day".
  Charm pricing: \$19.99 reads as "19-something".
  Scarcity and urgency: hotel booking sites, flash sales, limited editions (herd behaviour and loss aversion).
  Defaults: auto-renewing subscriptions, pre-ticked add-ons (travel insurance on flights).
  Loyalty programs: Flybuys and Everyday Rewards — points exploit the endowment effect and make switching feel like a loss, while giving firms detailed data.
  Buy now, pay later (Afterpay, Zip): splitting a price into four payments reduces the "pain of paying" (present bias).
  Store layout: essentials at the back, treats at checkouts, eye-level placement of high-margin brands.
  Social proof: star ratings, "bestseller", "12 people are viewing this".
**Judging effectiveness**
  For businesses: higher conversion rates, sales and margins; loyalty programs raise repeat purchases.
  For consumers: some nudges help (reminders to reorder medication), but many lead to overspending, debt (BNPL) or paying for unwanted subscriptions — lowering living standards.
  Limits: consumers learn and become sceptical; the ACCC acts on misleading conduct under the Australian Consumer Law; the government has proposed banning unfair practices such as drip pricing and subscription traps.
**Worked example**
  A cinema sells popcorn: small \$6, medium \$9.50, large \$10. Explain the strategy and evaluate its effect.
    The medium is a decoy: it's close in price to the large, so the large looks like great value (only 50c more for much more popcorn).
    Many customers who would have bought a small or medium trade up to the large, raising revenue per customer at little extra cost to the cinema.
    Consumers may buy more than they wanted (bounded rationality), lowering the value they get per dollar.
**Going deeper**
  The same insight can serve good or bad ends: defaults enrol people in super (government) or in subscriptions they forget (business). The difference is whether the nudge serves the chooser's own interest.
  Regulators increasingly treat "dark patterns" online (hidden cancel buttons, fake timers) as misleading conduct.
**Common mistakes**
  Describing the strategy without naming the behavioural insight it exploits.
  Judging effectiveness only for the business — also consider consumers' living standards.
**Exam tips**
  Strategy → insight exploited → example → effectiveness for the firm → effect on consumers → any regulation.
**Connections**
  Bounded rationality, willpower, self-interest.
  Strategies to increase profit (Unit 1 Area of Study 2): price discrimination and multiple branding.
  Government nudges and their effectiveness: the same insights used for public goals.
**Practice questions**
  Explain how anchoring is used by retailers, with an example.
    Retailers show a high reference price ("was \$300") beside the sale price (\$120) so the sale price seems a bargain, increasing sales even if the "was" price was rarely charged.
  How do loyalty programs use behavioural insights to increase profit?
    Points feel like an asset the customer owns (endowment effect) and switching feels like a loss (loss aversion), so customers keep returning; the firm also gains data to target offers.
  Evaluate the effect of buy now, pay later on consumers.
    It makes purchases feel cheaper by splitting payments (present bias), raising spending; convenient for some, but it can lead to overspending, late fees and debt for others — lowering their living standards.
**Flashcards**
  What is decoy pricing? :: Adding an option to make another look better value.
  Ariely's Economist experiment: share choosing print + web with the decoy? :: 84%.
  A \$9.99 price is {{charm}} pricing.
  Which insight do "only 2 left" messages exploit? :: Loss aversion and herd behaviour (scarcity).
  Which insight do loyalty points exploit? :: The endowment effect and loss aversion.
  What is drip pricing? :: A low headline price with compulsory fees added later.
  Auto-renewing free trials exploit which bias? :: Default (status quo) bias.
  Who enforces rules against misleading pricing? :: The ACCC, under the Australian Consumer Law.

@ 4a6e408c U1.3.2.2 | Telling traditional from behavioural
**In a sentence**
  This skill is recognising whether an explanation or prediction uses the traditional model or behavioural insights, and explaining the difference with examples.
**Key terms**
  **Traditional explanation**: relies on rational responses to prices, incomes and incentives.
  **Behavioural explanation**: relies on biases, heuristics, limited willpower, social norms or fairness.
**How to do it**
  Ask: does the explanation assume people calculate and respond to incentives (traditional), or that presentation, habit, emotion or fairness drives the choice (behavioural)?
  Give the traditional prediction, then the behavioural one, and say which the evidence supports.
**Going deeper**
  Many real policies combine both: a sugar tax (traditional price incentive) with front-of-pack warnings (behavioural salience).
**Worked example**
  Why do more people become organ donors in countries with an opt-out system?
    Traditional: registration costs are tiny, so the default shouldn't matter — rates should be similar.
    Behavioural: default bias — most people stay with the default, so opt-out countries have far higher consent rates (above 90% in some, versus much lower in opt-in countries like Australia).
    The evidence supports the behavioural explanation.
**Connections**
  Traditional vs behavioural economics.
  Bounded rationality, willpower, self-interest.
**Practice questions**
  Is this traditional or behavioural: "Petrol sales fell because the price rose 30c a litre"?
    Traditional — a rational response to a higher price (the law of demand).
  Is this traditional or behavioural: "Sales rose after the shop moved lollies to the checkout"?
    Behavioural — placement and impulse (bounded willpower), not price, changed behaviour.
**Flashcards**
  Explanation based on price and incentives? :: Traditional.
  Explanation based on defaults, framing or habits? :: Behavioural.
  Why do opt-out organ donation systems get higher consent? :: Default bias — most people keep the pre-set option.
  Can one policy use both approaches? :: Yes — e.g. a tax plus warning labels.

@ e138ef94 U1.3.2.3 | Predicting with economic theory
**In a sentence**
  This skill is using traditional and behavioural theory to predict how consumers and other agents will respond to a change — especially to a change in how choices are presented.
**Key terms**
  **Prediction**: a reasoned, testable statement about the likely effect of a change.
**How to do it**
  Identify the change (a new default, a new price, a new label).
  Choose the theory: incentives and the law of demand (traditional) or a named bias (behavioural).
  Chain: change → how it alters costs, benefits or perception → response → outcome for the agents and living standards.
  Note uncertainty: effects may fade or differ between groups.
**Going deeper**
  Behavioural predictions are especially strong for defaults: participation rates usually rise from well under half to over 80–90% when a scheme becomes opt-out.
**Worked example**
  Predict the effect of an employer changing its salary-sacrifice super scheme from opt-in to opt-out (workers are enrolled at 2% extra unless they leave).
    Default bias and inertia mean most workers stay enrolled.
    Participation rises sharply, so retirement savings rise.
    Take-home pay falls slightly for those enrolled; a few who can't afford it opt out.
**Connections**
  Traditional vs behavioural economics.
  Government nudges and their effectiveness.
**Practice questions**
  Predict the effect of putting calorie counts on fast-food menus.
    Salience of health information (framing) should reduce average calories ordered a little, especially among health-conscious customers; effects are often small and may fade.
  Predict the effect of a supermarket removing lollies from checkouts.
    Fewer impulse purchases (bounded willpower), so confectionery sales fall at the checkout, though some shoppers buy them elsewhere in the store.
**Flashcards**
  Strongest behavioural prediction tool? :: Defaults — most people stick with them.
  Effect of making a scheme opt-out? :: Participation rises sharply.
  Behavioural effects often {{fade}} over time.
  First step in a prediction? :: Identify the change and the theory that applies.

@ 1754de0f U1.3.2.4 | Running a behavioural experiment
**In a sentence**
  This skill is investigating or running a behavioural economics experiment — designing it, collecting data, analysing the results and reporting what they show about decision-making.
**Key terms**
  **Hypothesis**: a testable prediction ("A 'most students do this' sign will raise correct recycling").
  **Control group**: participants who don't receive the treatment, for comparison.
  **Treatment group**: participants who do.
  **Variable**: the factor changed (independent) and the outcome measured (dependent).
  **Ethics**: informed consent, no harm, privacy, and debriefing participants.
**How to do it**
  1. Choose an insight to test (anchoring, framing, decoy, social norms, ultimatum game).
  2. Write a hypothesis.
  3. Design: two groups as similar as possible (randomly assigned), one change only.
  4. Collect data: count choices, prices guessed, offers made.
  5. Analyse: compare averages or percentages between groups.
  6. Report: findings, whether the hypothesis was supported, limitations (small sample, participants knew each other), and real-world applications.
**Going deeper**
  VCAA's support material suggests testing whether a nudge or reward changes rubbish disposal at school — a real field experiment with a clear outcome to count.
**Worked example**
  Design an anchoring experiment and interpret sample results.
    Hypothesis: people shown a high number will guess a higher price for a jar of jam.
    Method: Group A (20 students) first writes the last two digits of 20; Group B (20 students) writes 90; both then guess the jam's price.
    Results (sample): Group A average \$5.20; Group B average \$7.60.
    Analysis: Group B guessed \$2.40 higher on average (7.60 − 5.20 = 2.40), about 46% more.
    Conclusion: the results support anchoring — an irrelevant number shifted price judgements. Limitations: small, non-random sample; students may have discussed answers.
**Connections**
  Bounded rationality, willpower, self-interest.
  Gathering and using economic data.
**Practice questions**
  Why does a behavioural experiment need a control group?
    To compare with the treatment group, so any difference can be attributed to the change being tested rather than other factors.
  Identify two ethical requirements for a classroom economics experiment.
    Informed consent from participants and protecting their privacy (also no harm and debriefing).
**Flashcards**
  Group that doesn't receive the treatment? :: The control group.
  What is a hypothesis? :: A testable prediction.
  Participants should be assigned to groups {{randomly}}.
  Two limitations common in class experiments? :: Small samples and participants influencing each other.

@ 32c12552 U1.3.2.5 | Evaluating behavioural policies
**In a sentence**
  This skill is judging how effective government and business actions based on behavioural economics are, using data and clear economic reasoning to reach a supported conclusion.
**Key terms**
  **Effectiveness**: how far an action achieves its aim, at what cost, and with what side effects.
  **Evaluation criteria**: evidence of effect, size and persistence, cost, reach and equity, unintended consequences, effect on living standards.
**How to do it**
  State the action and its aim.
  Name the behavioural insight it uses.
  Present evidence (trial results, before/after data) with dates and sources.
  Apply criteria and weigh them.
  Conclude: effective, partly effective or ineffective — for whom, and why.
**Going deeper**
  Separate the aim of the actor from the consumer's interest: a business nudge can be very effective for profits while lowering consumer living standards.
**Worked example**
  Evaluate the Superannuation Guarantee default as a behavioural policy.
    Aim: adequate retirement incomes. Insight: present bias and inertia — people under-save if left to choose.
    Evidence: almost all employees now have super; total super savings exceed \$4 trillion.
    Criteria: highly effective in reach; low administrative cost; but it lowers take-home pay now and helps higher earners more (tax concessions).
    Conclusion: very effective at raising saving, with equity concerns about who benefits most.
**Connections**
  Government nudges and their effectiveness.
  Business nudges and their effectiveness.
**Practice questions**
  List four criteria for evaluating a behavioural policy.
    Evidence of effect; size and persistence of the effect; cost; equity and unintended consequences.
  Why might a business nudge be judged effective and harmful at the same time?
    It can raise the firm's sales and profit (effective for its aim) while leading consumers to spend more than is in their interest, lowering their living standards.
**Flashcards**
  First step in evaluating a behavioural policy? :: State its aim and the insight it uses.
  Best evidence of a nudge's effect? :: A randomised controlled trial.
  Effectiveness should be judged for {{whom}} as well as how much.
  Evaluation criteria? :: Evidence, size/persistence, cost, equity, unintended effects, living standards.
