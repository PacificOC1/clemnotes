@ 71fcba93 U2.1.1.1 | The purpose of economic activity
**In a sentence**
  Economic activity — producing, distributing and consuming goods and services — exists to satisfy needs and wants, and so to raise the living standards of the community.
**Key terms**
  **Economic activity**: the production, distribution, exchange and consumption of goods and services in an economy.
  **Level of economic activity**: how much is being produced and spent overall, usually measured by real GDP.
  **Living standards**: the wellbeing of people — material and non-material.
**What activity involves**
  Production: firms combine land, labour, capital and enterprise to make goods and services.
  Distribution and exchange: goods move through markets to buyers; incomes flow to resource owners.
  Consumption: households use goods and services to satisfy needs and wants.
**Why the level matters**
  Higher activity means more output, more jobs and higher incomes — more needs and wants satisfied.
  Too little activity (a recession) leaves resources idle: unemployment rises and incomes fall.
  Too much (demand racing ahead of capacity) can cause inflation and bottlenecks, which the RBA fought in 2026 by raising the cash rate to 4.60%.
**Worked example**
  A Bendigo bakery employs 12 people and sells bread to local families. Explain how its activity contributes to living standards.
    Production: it combines flour (land), bakers (labour), ovens (capital) and the owner's enterprise to make bread.
    Incomes: it pays wages, rent and profits, which households spend on their own needs and wants.
    Consumption: families satisfy a basic need (food).
    Result: material living standards rise; jobs also give workers purpose and social connection (non-material).
**Going deeper**
  The aim isn't activity for its own sake: activity that damages health or the environment can lower overall living standards — the theme of the costs of growth.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying the purpose of economic activity is "to make profit". Profit is the business motive; the purpose of activity in the economy is to satisfy needs and wants.
**Exam tips**
  Link "purpose" directly to needs and wants and to living standards in your first sentence.
**Connections**
  Economic activity and living standards (Unit 1).
  Material and non-material living standards.
  Measuring growth with real GDP.
**Practice questions**
  What is the purpose of economic activity?
    To produce and distribute goods and services that satisfy people's needs and wants, raising living standards.
  Explain why both very low and very high levels of economic activity can be a problem.
    Low activity leaves resources unemployed and incomes low; activity growing faster than productive capacity creates inflation and bottlenecks that later require slowing the economy.
  Name the three parts of economic activity.
    Production, distribution/exchange and consumption.
**Flashcards**
  Purpose of economic activity? :: To satisfy needs and wants and raise living standards.
  Three parts of economic activity? :: Production, distribution/exchange and consumption.
  The level of economic activity is usually measured by {{real GDP}}.
  Problem of too little activity? :: Unemployment and falling incomes.
  Problem of too much activity? :: Inflation and bottlenecks.
  Is profit the purpose of economic activity? :: No — it's the business motive; the purpose is satisfying needs and wants.
  Why did the RBA raise rates in 2026? :: To slow demand and bring inflation back to its 2–3% target.

@ fe6be277 U2.1.1.2 | Material and non-material living standards
**In a sentence**
  Material living standards are the goods and services people can access and are easy to measure; non-material living standards are the quality-of-life factors — health, education, environment, safety, freedom, leisure — that money measures poorly.
**Key terms**
  **Material living standards**: the quantity and quality of goods and services people have access to; tangible and measurable, e.g. real income per person, housing, cars.
  **Non-material living standards**: aspects of wellbeing that are hard to quantify — health, life expectancy, education, a clean environment, leisure, safety, political freedom, equity, social connection.
  **Real income**: income adjusted for inflation — what it can actually buy.
**Material living standards**
  Measured by real GDP per capita, real household disposable income, home ownership, access to technology.
  Rise when real incomes grow faster than prices; fall when inflation outpaces wages, as it did for many workers in 2022–23 and again in 2026 (wages +3.2% vs prices +3.5–3.8%).
**Non-material living standards**
  Health (life expectancy in Australia is about 83 years), education, the natural environment, safety, freedom of speech and voting, leisure time, stress, community and cultural connection.
  Harder to measure, so economists use indicators (life expectancy, school completion, air quality, crime rates) or surveys of wellbeing.
**How they relate**
  Often together: higher incomes pay for better health care and education.
  Sometimes in conflict: longer working hours raise income but cut leisure and family time; mining raises incomes but may damage the environment.
**Worked example**
  Assess the effect on living standards of an Australian worker taking a fly-in fly-out mining job paying 40% more than their current job.
    Material: real income rises 40%, so they can afford more goods and services — material living standards rise.
    Non-material: long rosters away from family, fatigue and social isolation may reduce wellbeing; the job's safety risks also matter.
    Judgement: material living standards clearly rise; overall wellbeing depends on how the worker values time with family.
**Going deeper**
  Treasury's Measuring What Matters framework (2023) tracks 50 indicators across five themes — healthy, secure, sustainable, cohesive, prosperous — because GDP alone misses non-material living standards.
**Common mistakes**
  Listing "money" as a non-material factor.
  Treating non-material living standards as unimportant because they are hard to measure.
**Exam tips**
  Define both with the words "tangible/measurable" and "hard to quantify", then give an Australian example of each and show how an event affects both.
**Connections**
  The purpose of economic activity.
  Benefits of economic growth and Costs of economic growth.
  Alternative measures of living standards.
**Practice questions**
  Distinguish between material and non-material living standards.
    Material living standards are the goods and services people can access and are measurable (e.g. real income per person). Non-material living standards are quality-of-life factors that are hard to measure, such as health, environment and freedom.
  Explain how an increase in real income could raise non-material living standards.
    Higher income lets people afford better health care, education and leisure activities, and raises tax revenue that funds public services.
  Explain how economic growth might reduce non-material living standards.
    Growth can increase pollution, congestion and working hours, harming health, the environment and leisure.
**Flashcards**
  Material living standards are {{tangible}} and measurable.
  Three examples of non-material living standards? :: Health, environment, freedom (also leisure, safety, education).
  Measure of material living standards? :: Real GDP per capita or real household disposable income.
  What is real income? :: Income adjusted for inflation.
  Australia's life expectancy (approx.)? :: About 83 years.
  Treasury's wellbeing framework? :: Measuring What Matters (2023).
  How can higher material living standards lower non-material ones? :: Longer hours, stress, pollution.

@ 4711628c U2.1.1.3 | The five-sector circular flow model
**In a sentence**
  The five-sector circular flow model adds the financial sector and the overseas sector to households, firms and government, showing how leakages (saving, taxes, imports) and injections (investment, government spending, exports) raise or lower economic activity.
**Key terms**
  **Financial sector**: banks, super funds and other institutions that collect saving and lend it for investment.
  **Overseas sector**: the rest of the world, which buys Australia's exports and sells us imports.
  **Leakage (withdrawal)**: income that leaves the circular flow and isn't spent on domestic output — saving (S), taxation (T), imports (M).
  **Injection**: spending that enters the circular flow from outside household consumption — investment (I), government spending (G), exports (X).
  **Investment**: spending by firms on capital goods (machinery, buildings, technology).
**The five sectors and their flows**
  Households: supply resources, receive incomes, consume, save, pay taxes, buy imports.
  Firms: produce, pay incomes, borrow to invest, sell exports.
  Government: taxes (T) and spends (G) — on goods, services and transfer payments.
  Financial sector: receives saving (S) and lends it for investment (I) and to households.
  Overseas sector: buys exports (X), sells imports (M).
**Leakages and injections**
  If injections (I + G + X) > leakages (S + T + M): spending grows, so production, jobs and incomes rise — economic activity increases.
  If injections < leakages: spending shrinks and activity falls.
  If equal: activity is stable.
**Worked example**
  In a year (billions of dollars): saving 300, taxes 500, imports 600, investment 400, government spending 550, exports 520. What happens to economic activity?
    Leakages = 300 + 500 + 600 = \$1 400 billion.
    Injections = 400 + 550 + 520 = \$1 470 billion.
    Injections exceed leakages by \$70 billion, so spending is growing: production, employment and incomes are likely to rise.
**Going deeper**
  Saving isn't bad: saving flows through the financial sector to fund investment, which builds productive capacity. Problems arise when saving isn't matched by investment (as in a downturn when firms stop borrowing).
  The model implies a multiplier: an injection is spent and re-spent, raising total income by more than the injection itself (covered in Unit 3).
**Common mistakes**
  Confusing saving (a leakage) with investment (an injection).
  Treating imports as an injection — spending on imports leaves Australia.
  Forgetting that the overseas sector buys exports (injection) as well as selling imports (leakage).
**Exam tips**
  Draw or describe all five sectors, label each flow, and state the injections vs leakages rule when explaining a change.
**Connections**
  The three-sector circular flow model (Unit 1): the simpler version.
  Aggregate demand and its components: C + I + G + (X − M).
  The business cycle: changes in injections and leakages drive it.
**Practice questions**
  Identify the three leakages and three injections in the five-sector model.
    Leakages: saving, taxation, imports. Injections: investment, government spending, exports.
  Explain the role of the financial sector in the circular flow.
    It takes households' saving (a leakage) and lends it to firms for investment (an injection) and to households for consumption, linking saving to investment.
  A fall in China's demand for Australian iron ore reduces exports by \$20 billion. Use the model to explain the effect on activity.
    Exports are an injection; if they fall and nothing else changes, injections fall below leakages, so spending on Australian output falls, firms produce less and employ fewer workers, and incomes fall.
**Flashcards**
  Five sectors? :: Households, firms, government, financial sector, overseas sector.
  Three leakages? :: Saving, taxation, imports.
  Three injections? :: Investment, government spending, exports.
  If injections exceed leakages, economic activity {{rises}}.
  Is saving an injection or a leakage? :: A leakage.
  Role of the financial sector? :: Channels saving into loans for investment and consumption.
  Calculate: S 300, T 500, M 600; I 400, G 550, X 520 (\$b). Injections minus leakages? :: +\$70 billion — activity rises.

@ 17e15030 U2.1.1.4 | The business cycle
**In a sentence**
  The business cycle is the pattern of rises and falls in economic activity around its long-run trend — expansion to a peak, contraction to a trough, and recovery — with typical changes in jobs, prices and confidence at each stage.
**Key terms**
  **Business cycle**: fluctuations in the level of economic activity (real GDP) over time.
  **Trend growth**: the average rate at which the economy's productive capacity grows over the long term (about 2–2.5% a year for Australia).
  **Expansion (upswing)**: a period of rising activity.
  **Peak (boom)**: the top of the cycle — activity at its highest; pressure on capacity.
  **Contraction (downswing)**: a period of slowing or falling activity.
  **Trough**: the bottom of the cycle.
  **Recession**: commonly defined as two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth; more broadly, a significant fall in activity and rise in unemployment.
**Phases and their features**
  Expansion/recovery: spending, production and employment rise; confidence improves; unemployment falls; inflation starts to rise.
  Peak/boom: resources are close to fully used; labour shortages; inflation and interest rates high; asset prices high.
  Contraction: spending and production slow; confidence falls; firms cut hours, then jobs; unemployment rises; inflation eases.
  Trough: activity at its lowest; high unemployment; low inflation and interest rates; the stage is set for recovery.
**Australian examples**
  1990–91 recession: unemployment rose above 10%.
  2020 COVID recession: real GDP fell about 7% in the June quarter of 2020 — the largest fall on record — then recovered quickly with stimulus.
  2025–26: growth around 2% with inflation above target; the RBA raised rates four times in 2026 to 4.60% to slow demand.
**Worked example**
  Quarterly real GDP growth: Q1 +0.8%, Q2 +0.6%, Q3 +0.2%, Q4 −0.3%, Q5 −0.4%, Q6 +0.1%, Q7 +0.5%. Identify the phases and whether a recession occurred.
    Q1–Q3: expansion, but growth is slowing — approaching a peak around Q3.
    Q4–Q5: two consecutive negative quarters — a (technical) recession; contraction.
    Q5–Q6: trough around Q5 (the lowest growth).
    Q6–Q7: recovery.
**Going deeper**
  Cycles are irregular: they vary in length and depth, caused by shocks to aggregate demand (confidence, interest rates, overseas downturns) or aggregate supply (pandemics, energy price shocks).
  Governments try to smooth the cycle (stabilisation) with monetary and fiscal policy.
  Real GDP per capita can fall even when total GDP grows (a "per capita recession") if population grows faster — as happened in Australia during 2023–24.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying the economy "shrinks" in every downswing. Growth can slow but stay positive.
  Treating the cycle as perfectly regular or predictable.
  Confusing the trough (the bottom) with the recession (the period of falling activity).
**Exam tips**
  Describe each phase by what happens to real GDP, unemployment and inflation.
**Connections**
  Leading, lagging and coincident indicators and Indicators across the business cycle.
  Aggregate demand and aggregate supply: what drives the cycle.
  Costs of economic growth: boom and bust.
**Practice questions**
  Describe the features of the peak and trough of the business cycle.
    Peak: activity at its highest, resources nearly fully employed, labour shortages and rising inflation. Trough: activity at its lowest, high unemployment, low inflation and weak confidence.
  What is a recession? When did Australia last have one?
    Commonly, two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth. Australia's last recession was in 2020 during COVID.
  Explain how unemployment and inflation typically change during an expansion.
    As spending and production rise, firms hire more workers, so unemployment falls; as the economy approaches capacity, costs and prices rise faster, so inflation increases.
**Flashcards**
  Four phases of the business cycle? :: Expansion, peak, contraction, trough (then recovery).
  Common definition of a recession? :: Two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth.
  Australia's last recession? :: 2020 (COVID).
  At the {{peak}}, inflation pressures are highest.
  At the trough, unemployment is {{high}}.
  Fall in real GDP in June quarter 2020? :: About 7%.
  What is a per capita recession? :: Real GDP per person falling while total GDP still grows.

@ 2139425b U2.1.1.5 | Leading, lagging and coincident indicators
**In a sentence**
  Economic indicators are statistics that show the state of the economy: leading indicators change before the business cycle turns, coincident ones move with it, and lagging ones change after it.
**Key terms**
  **Economic indicator**: a statistic that shows the direction or level of economic activity.
  **Leading indicator**: changes before the economy as a whole changes, so it helps forecast.
  **Coincident indicator**: changes at about the same time as the economy.
  **Lagging indicator**: changes after the economy has changed, so it confirms a trend.
**Leading indicators**
  Building approvals: builders get approval months before construction.
  Job advertisements and vacancies: firms advertise before hiring.
  Consumer sentiment (Westpac–Melbourne Institute index) and business confidence (NAB survey): expectations drive later spending.
  Share prices: reflect expected future profits.
  Interest rate changes and the yield curve.
**Coincident indicators**
  Real GDP, retail sales, industrial production, hours worked, employment.
**Lagging indicators**
  Unemployment rate: firms cut hours first and delay rehiring, so unemployment peaks after the trough.
  Inflation (CPI): price pressures build after demand has picked up.
  Wages growth (Wage Price Index): wages are renegotiated only periodically.
**Worked example**
  Classify: (a) building approvals fell 8% in a month; (b) the unemployment rate rose to 4.6% in August 2026; (c) retail sales rose 0.5% in the quarter; (d) consumer sentiment slumped after the September rate rise.
    (a) Leading — signals less construction activity in coming months.
    (b) Lagging — reflects earlier slowing in activity.
    (c) Coincident — measures current spending.
    (d) Leading — weaker confidence predicts weaker spending ahead.
**Going deeper**
  No single indicator is reliable: leading indicators give false signals, so economists look for several pointing the same way.
  Some indicators can fit different categories depending on context (employment is often coincident; the unemployment rate lags).
**Common mistakes**
  Calling unemployment a leading indicator.
  Mixing up coincident (now) with lagging (after).
**Exam tips**
  For each indicator, say why it leads or lags (e.g. "firms apply for approvals before they build").
**Connections**
  The business cycle.
  Indicators across the business cycle.
  Spotting trends in economic data.
**Practice questions**
  Explain why building approvals are a leading indicator.
    Approvals are granted months before construction begins, so a change in approvals signals a later change in construction activity, jobs and spending.
  Why is the unemployment rate usually a lagging indicator?
    Firms respond to falling demand first by cutting hours and not replacing staff, and are slow to hire again in a recovery, so unemployment changes after activity does.
  Give one example each of a leading, coincident and lagging indicator.
    Leading: consumer sentiment. Coincident: real GDP. Lagging: inflation.
**Flashcards**
  What is a leading indicator? :: One that changes before the economy does.
  What is a lagging indicator? :: One that changes after the economy has changed.
  Real GDP is a {{coincident}} indicator.
  Three leading indicators? :: Building approvals, job ads, consumer/business confidence (also share prices).
  Three lagging indicators? :: Unemployment rate, inflation, wages growth.
  Why does unemployment lag? :: Firms cut hours first and hire slowly again.
  Australian consumer sentiment index? :: Westpac–Melbourne Institute.

@ 08893d43 U2.1.1.6 | Indicators across the business cycle
**In a sentence**
  Because different indicators turn at different times, reading them together lets economists forecast turning points (leading), confirm where the economy is now (coincident) and check that a trend has taken hold (lagging).
**Key terms**
  **Turning point**: a change from expansion to contraction (a peak) or contraction to expansion (a trough).
  **Forecast**: a prediction of future economic conditions.
**Using indicators through the cycle**
  Before a peak: leading indicators (sentiment, approvals, job ads) start to fall while GDP is still rising and unemployment is still low.
  Through the downswing: coincident indicators (GDP, retail sales) slow or fall.
  After the trough: unemployment keeps rising for a while even as leading indicators recover — which is why a recovery can feel like a recession to job seekers.
**Why it matters for policy**
  The RBA and Treasury act on forecasts, because policy works with a lag (a rate change takes a year or more to have its full effect). They watch leading indicators to act early.
**Worked example**
  In 2026: consumer sentiment fell after rate rises; building approvals weakened; real GDP grew 0.4% in the June quarter; unemployment rose to 4.6% in August. Where is the economy in the cycle?
    Leading indicators (sentiment, approvals) are falling — they point to slower activity ahead.
    Coincident (GDP) is still growing but modestly — the economy is expanding slowly.
    Lagging (unemployment) is starting to rise — confirming earlier slowing.
    Conclusion: the economy appears to be past a peak and slowing (early contraction phase), though not in recession.
**Going deeper**
  Composite indexes combine several leading indicators (e.g. the Westpac–Melbourne Institute Leading Index) to reduce false signals.
**Common mistakes**
  Drawing a conclusion from one indicator.
  Forgetting the time dimension: indicators must be compared over the same period.
**Exam tips**
  Classify each indicator in a data set, then synthesise: "Leading indicators suggest…, coincident show…, lagging confirm…".
**Connections**
  Leading, lagging and coincident indicators.
  The business cycle.
  Spotting trends in economic data.
**Practice questions**
  Why do policymakers pay particular attention to leading indicators?
    Policy takes time to work, so to stabilise the economy they must act before a turning point; leading indicators give early warning.
  During a recovery, why might unemployment still be rising?
    It is a lagging indicator — firms first increase hours for existing workers and wait until they are confident before hiring.
  If consumer sentiment and job ads are rising, but unemployment is still high, where is the economy likely to be?
    Near the trough, beginning to recover.
**Flashcards**
  Which indicators help forecast turning points? :: Leading indicators.
  Which confirm a trend? :: Lagging indicators.
  Which indicators show current activity? :: Coincident indicators.
  Why do policymakers use forecasts? :: Policy works with a lag.
  Unemployment rising in early recovery shows it is a {{lagging}} indicator.
  What is a turning point? :: A peak or a trough in the cycle.
  Mid-2026 reading of indicators? :: Slowing — leading falling, GDP modest, unemployment rising.

@ 4be64e98 U2.1.1.7 | Aggregate demand and its components
**In a sentence**
  Aggregate demand is total spending on an economy's final goods and services — consumption, investment, government spending and net exports — and because spending drives production, it largely sets the level of economic activity in the short run.
**Key terms**
  **Aggregate demand (AD)**: total planned spending on final goods and services produced in an economy over a period.
  **Consumption (C)**: household spending on goods and services — the largest component, about half of GDP.
  **Investment (I)**: spending by firms (and households on new housing) on capital goods — machinery, buildings, equipment, software.
  **Government spending (G)**: government consumption (public servants' wages, services) and public investment (roads, hospitals). Transfer payments aren't counted directly — they count when households spend them.
  **Net exports (X − M)**: exports minus imports.
**The formula**
  $AD = C + I + G + (X - M)$
**Why AD matters**
  Spending is someone's income: when AD rises, firms sell more, produce more, hire more and pay more incomes, so activity and employment rise.
  If AD grows faster than the economy's capacity (aggregate supply), prices rise — inflation. If it falls, unemployment rises.
**Worked example**
  An economy has (\$ billions): C 1 300, I 560, G 700, X 650, M 620. Calculate AD, the share of consumption, and the effect of a \$40 billion fall in investment.
    AD = 1 300 + 560 + 700 + (650 − 620) = 1 300 + 560 + 700 + 30 = \$2 590 billion.
    Consumption share = 1 300 ÷ 2 590 × 100 ≈ 50.2%.
    If I falls by 40: AD = \$2 550 billion, a fall of about 1.5% (40 ÷ 2 590). Firms produce less, employ fewer workers and pay less income — activity slows.
**Going deeper**
  Because consumption is so large, household confidence and interest rates are powerful drivers of the cycle: a small percentage change in C moves AD a lot.
  Net exports can be negative (a trade deficit) — they subtract from AD.
**Common mistakes**
  Counting imports as a positive — imports are subtracted.
  Counting transfer payments as G.
  Saying investment means buying shares. In economics, investment is spending on real capital goods.
**Exam tips**
  Know the formula, which component is largest, and one factor that changes each component.
**Connections**
  The five-sector circular flow model: the same flows.
  What changes aggregate demand.
  Aggregate supply: AD must be matched by capacity to avoid inflation.
**Practice questions**
  Define aggregate demand and list its components.
    Total spending on final goods and services produced in the economy: consumption, investment, government spending and net exports (exports minus imports).
  Explain why aggregate demand is important for employment.
    Firms hire workers to produce what they can sell; higher total spending means more production and more jobs, lower spending means fewer.
  Calculate AD if C = 800, I = 300, G = 400, X = 350 and M = 380 (\$ billions).
    AD = 800 + 300 + 400 + (350 − 380) = \$1 470 billion.
**Flashcards**
  Aggregate demand formula? :: C + I + G + (X − M).
  Largest component of AD? :: Household consumption (about half of GDP).
  What is investment in economics? :: Spending on capital goods — machinery, buildings, equipment.
  Are transfer payments part of G? :: No — they're counted when households spend them (in C).
  Imports are {{subtracted}} in the AD formula.
  If AD grows faster than capacity, what happens? :: Inflation.
  Calculate AD: C 1 300, I 560, G 700, X 650, M 620 (\$b). :: \$2 590 billion.

@ 5ab96a5a U2.1.1.8 | What changes aggregate demand
**In a sentence**
  Anything that changes the spending of households, firms, government or overseas buyers — incomes, interest rates, confidence, wealth, government budgets, world growth, the exchange rate — changes aggregate demand and so the level of economic activity.
**Key terms**
  **Interest rate**: the cost of borrowing and reward for saving; the RBA's cash rate influences all other rates.
  **Wealth effect**: when asset values (houses, shares) rise, people feel richer and spend more.
  **Exchange rate**: the value of the Australian dollar in terms of other currencies.
  **Budget (fiscal) stance**: whether government is adding to spending (stimulatory) or restraining it (contractionary).
**Consumption (C)**
  Disposable income (wages, tax cuts, transfers): up → C up. The 1 July 2026 cut in the lowest tax rate (16% to 15%) lifts disposable income.
  Interest rates: up → loan repayments rise and saving is more rewarding → C down. The 2026 rises to 4.60% were meant to do exactly this.
  Consumer confidence: optimism → C up.
  Wealth: rising house prices → C up (wealth effect); falling values (down 3.1% in the three months to August 2026) → C down.
  Inflation: erodes real income → real C down.
**Investment (I)**
  Interest rates: higher → borrowing dearer → I down.
  Business confidence and expected profits: optimism → I up.
  Capacity utilisation: firms near full capacity invest to expand.
  Tax incentives and technology: instant asset write-offs, AI and data-centre investment → I up.
**Government spending (G)**
  Budget decisions: infrastructure projects, health and education spending, defence. More G → AD up.
**Net exports (X − M)**
  World growth, especially China: stronger overseas growth → X up.
  Commodity prices: higher iron ore and LNG prices raise export earnings.
  Exchange rate: a lower Australian dollar makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports dearer for Australians → net exports up.
  Domestic income: higher Australian incomes → more imports → net exports down.
**Worked example**
  Explain the likely effect on AD and economic activity of the RBA raising the cash rate from 4.35% to 4.60% in September 2026.
    Higher rates raise mortgage and loan repayments: households with debt have less to spend, and saving becomes more attractive → consumption falls.
    Borrowing for investment becomes dearer → some business investment is delayed.
    A higher rate can raise the dollar, cutting net exports.
    AD grows more slowly (or falls), so production and hiring slow; this reduces inflationary pressure — the RBA's aim — but may raise unemployment.
**Going deeper**
  Effects take time: most home loans are variable, but fixed-rate borrowers feel rises only when their fixed terms end, so monetary policy works with "long and variable lags".
  Several factors often pull in different directions (a tax cut raising C while rate rises cut it), so the net effect depends on their relative size.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying higher interest rates increase consumption because savers earn more. For the economy as a whole, the borrowing effect usually dominates.
  Forgetting that a lower dollar raises exports and lowers imports.
**Exam tips**
  Factor → which component → direction → effect on AD → effect on activity (production, employment, income, inflation).
**Connections**
  Aggregate demand and its components.
  What shifts demand (Unit 1): the same factors in one market.
  The business cycle: AD changes drive most cycles.
**Practice questions**
  Explain how a fall in consumer confidence affects aggregate demand and activity.
    Worried households save more and delay spending, so consumption falls; lower AD means firms produce and hire less, so activity slows.
  How would a depreciation of the Australian dollar affect aggregate demand?
    Exports become cheaper for overseas buyers and imports dearer for Australians, so exports rise and imports fall: net exports and AD rise.
  Explain two factors that could increase investment.
    Lower interest rates (cheaper borrowing) and higher business confidence or expected profits (e.g. from strong demand or new technology).
  Why might a rise in house prices increase consumption?
    Homeowners feel wealthier (wealth effect) and can borrow against their homes, so they spend more.
**Flashcards**
  Higher interest rates → effect on C and I? :: Both fall.
  Lower Australian dollar → effect on net exports? :: Rises.
  Rising house prices boost consumption via the {{wealth effect}}.
  Stronger Chinese growth → effect on AD? :: Exports rise, AD rises.
  Two factors affecting investment? :: Interest rates and business confidence (also capacity use, tax incentives).
  Why does monetary policy work with a lag? :: Fixed loans, contracts and slow spending responses.
  A tax cut raises AD through which component? :: Consumption (higher disposable income).
  Cash rate after the 29 September 2026 decision? :: 4.60%.

@ dc0f6669 U2.1.1.9 | Aggregate supply
**In a sentence**
  Aggregate supply is the total amount of goods and services an economy's producers are willing and able to supply at each price level — in effect, the economy's productive capacity — and it limits how fast the economy can grow without inflation.
**Key terms**
  **Aggregate supply (AS)**: the total quantity of final goods and services that producers in an economy are willing and able to supply at each general price level over a period.
  **Productive capacity (potential output)**: the maximum sustainable output when resources are fully and efficiently employed.
  **Capacity constraint**: a limit on output from a shortage of workers, materials or equipment.
**Why AS matters**

@ 3605498f U2.1.1.11 | Measuring growth with real GDP
**In a sentence**

**Key terms**
  **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**: the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period.
  **Nominal GDP**: GDP measured at current prices (includes the effect of inflation).
  **Real GDP**: GDP adjusted for inflation (measured at constant prices; the ABS publishes "chain volume measures").
  **Economic growth**: an increase in real GDP over time.
  **Real GDP per capita**: real GDP divided by population — average output (income) per person.
**Why "real"?**
  If prices rise 3.5% and output doesn't change, nominal GDP rises 3.5% but the economy hasn't produced any more. Real GDP removes the price effect.
  Approximation: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation.
**The formulas**
  $\text{Growth rate} = \frac{\text{Real GDP}_{\text{this period}} - \text{Real GDP}_{\text{last period}}}{\text{Real GDP}_{\text{last period}}} \times 100$
  $\text{Real GDP per capita} = \frac{\text{Real GDP}}{\text{Population}}$
**Australia's recent figures**
  Nominal GDP is roughly \$2.7 trillion a year.
  June quarter 2026: real GDP +0.4% in the quarter, +2.1% over the year; real GDP per capita flat in the quarter and +0.7% over the year (ABS).
  Growth per capita is lower than total growth because Australia's population grows about 1.5% a year.
**Worked example**
  Real GDP was \$2 400 billion last year and \$2 448 billion this year. Population rose from 27.2 million to 27.6 million. Calculate real GDP growth and the change in real GDP per capita.
    Growth = (2 448 − 2 400) ÷ 2 400 × 100 = 48 ÷ 2 400 × 100 = 2.0%.
    Per capita last year = \$2 400 billion ÷ 27.2 million ≈ \$88 235.
    Per capita this year = \$2 448 billion ÷ 27.6 million ≈ \$88 696.
    Change = (88 696 − 88 235) ÷ 88 235 × 100 ≈ 0.5%.
    Sense check: population grew about 1.5% (0.4 ÷ 27.2), so per capita growth ≈ 2.0 − 1.5 ≈ 0.5%.
**Worked example 2**
  Nominal GDP grew 5.5% in a year and the GDP deflator (inflation) was 3.5%. Estimate real growth.
    Real growth ≈ 5.5 − 3.5 = 2.0%. (Exactly: 1.055 ÷ 1.035 − 1 ≈ 1.9%.)
**Going deeper**
  GDP counts only final goods to avoid double counting (the flour in bread isn't counted separately from the bread).
  GDP can be measured three ways — production, income and expenditure — which in principle give the same total (the circular flow: output = income = spending).
**Common mistakes**
  Using nominal GDP to measure growth.
  Confusing a fall in the growth rate with a fall in GDP. If growth slows from 3% to 1%, GDP is still rising.
  Forgetting the × 100 or using the new value as the base.
**Exam tips**

@ c179ad75 U2.1.2.4 | Spotting trends in economic data
**In a sentence**
  This skill is reading tables and graphs of economic data to identify trends (direction over time), patterns, similarities and differences, and drawing conclusions from them.
**Key terms**
  **Trend**: the general direction of data over time (rising, falling, stable).
  **Pattern**: a regular or repeated feature (seasonal peaks, cycles).
  **Fluctuation**: short-term ups and downs around a trend.
**How to do it**
  Describe the overall trend first, with start and end values and dates.
  Note the highest and lowest points and any turning points.
  Compare two series: do they move together (similarity) or opposite (difference)?
  Explain with economic reasoning: why did it happen?
  Quote the numbers: "Unemployment rose from 4.1% to 4.6% between…"
**Going deeper**
  Distinguish levels from rates of change: inflation falling from 3.8% to 3.5% means prices are still rising, just more slowly.
**Worked example**

    Explanation: inflation stayed above the 2–3% target and energy prices rose, so the RBA tightened policy to slow aggregate demand.
**Connections**

  Leading, lagging and coincident indicators.

@ 8b085ee4 U2.1.2.5 | Weighing the costs and benefits of growth
**In a sentence**
  This skill is discussing the potential costs and benefits of higher economic activity for different groups and for material and non-material living standards, and reaching a reasoned judgement.
**Key terms**
  **Discuss**: present more than one side and reach a supported conclusion.
  **Sustainable growth**: growth that can be maintained without causing high inflation, environmental harm or unmanageable debt.
**How to do it**
  Benefits: incomes, jobs, tax revenue, development — with data.
  Costs: inflation and boom-bust, congestion, environmental damage, inequality, affluenza — with examples.
  Consider who gains and who loses (workers, borrowers, future generations).
  Judge using a criterion: overall living standards, sustainability or equity.
**Going deeper**
  The best answers consider the quality of growth, not just its rate: growth driven by productivity and clean energy has different costs from growth driven by population or resource extraction.
**Worked example**
  Discuss whether Australia should aim for faster economic growth.
    Benefits: more jobs (unemployment has risen to 4.6%), higher real incomes and tax revenue for services.
    Costs: with inflation above target, faster demand-led growth would add to price pressures and interest rates; more emissions and congestion unless growth is green.
    Judgement: faster growth driven by productivity (raising aggregate supply) would raise living standards; faster demand-driven growth now would mostly add to inflation.
**Connections**
  Benefits of economic growth.
  Costs of economic growth.
**Practice questions**
  What is sustainable growth?
    Growth that can be maintained over time without causing high inflation, environmental damage or unmanageable debt.
  Why does the source of growth matter when discussing its costs?
    Productivity-driven or green growth raises living standards with fewer costs; population- or demand-driven growth may add to congestion, emissions or inflation.
**Flashcards**
  What does "discuss" require? :: More than one side and a supported conclusion.
  Growth that doesn't cause inflation, environmental harm or unmanageable debt is {{sustainable}} growth.
  Better source of growth when inflation is high? :: Productivity (raising AS).
  Criteria for judging growth? :: Living standards, sustainability, equity.
