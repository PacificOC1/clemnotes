@ 8c78961d U1.1.1.1 | Microeconomics and macroeconomics
**In a sentence**
  Microeconomics zooms in on individual consumers, businesses and markets; macroeconomics zooms out to the whole economy — output, jobs, prices and growth.
**Key terms**
  **Economics**: the study of how society allocates scarce resources to satisfy needs and wants.
  **Microeconomics**: the study of the decisions of individual economic agents and of individual markets.
  **Macroeconomics**: the study of the economy as a whole — aggregates such as total output (GDP), unemployment, inflation and economic growth.
  **Aggregate**: a total for the whole economy, such as aggregate demand (total spending).
**Microeconomics**
  Asks how one household, firm or market behaves: why the price of avocados rose, how a café sets its prices, whether a teenager takes a part-time job.
  Its main tools are demand and supply, costs and revenue, and market structures — the content of Unit 1 Area of Study 2.
**Macroeconomics**
  Asks how the whole economy performs: why unemployment rose to 4.6% in August 2026, why the Reserve Bank lifted the cash rate to 4.60% in September 2026, whether real GDP is growing.
  Its main tools are the circular flow model, aggregate demand and aggregate supply, and the business cycle — the content of Unit 2 Area of Study 1.
**One issue, two lenses**
  A jump in petrol prices is a micro issue (the fuel market: why the price rose, how drivers respond) and a macro issue (it pushes up the consumer price index and lowers real incomes across the economy).
  Good answers say which lens they are using.
**Worked example**
  Classify each as micro or macro: (a) Woolworths raises the price of milk; (b) Australia's inflation rate is 3.5%; (c) a farmer switches from wheat to canola; (d) the government cuts income tax for all workers.
    (a) Micro — one firm's pricing in one market.
    (b) Macro — the general price level of the whole economy.
    (c) Micro — one producer's decision about how to use resources.
    (d) Macro — a policy aimed at total spending and incomes across the economy (even though each worker also feels it individually).
**Going deeper**
  The line blurs: macro outcomes are the sum of millions of micro decisions, and modern macroeconomics builds its models from how individual households and firms behave ("micro foundations").
  Behavioural economics (Area of Study 3) is mainly micro — it studies individual decision-making.
**Common mistakes**
  Thinking "macro" means "big". A decision by BHP, a very large firm, about one mine is still micro; the national unemployment rate is macro.
  Thinking a policy is micro just because it affects individuals — an income tax cut for everyone targets the whole economy.
**Exam tips**
  When asked to "distinguish", give a definition of each plus one contrasting example.
**Connections**
  Positive and normative economics: both branches use both kinds of statement.
  The three-sector circular flow model: the first macro model you meet.
  Decision-making in markets (Unit 1 Area of Study 2): the heart of microeconomics.
**Practice questions**
  Distinguish between microeconomics and macroeconomics, using an example of each.
    Microeconomics studies individual agents and markets, such as how a rise in the price of beef changes the quantity households buy. Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole, such as the national unemployment rate or the rate of economic growth.
  Is the Reserve Bank's decision to change the cash rate a micro or a macro issue? Explain.
    Macro: the cash rate is set to influence total spending, inflation and employment across the whole economy, not the outcome of one market.
  Explain how a rise in petrol prices could be studied from both a micro and a macro perspective.
    Micro: how the fuel market reaches a new price and how drivers and transport firms respond. Macro: how higher fuel prices raise the CPI, cut real incomes and may slow total spending in the economy.
**Flashcards**
  What does microeconomics study? :: Individual economic agents and individual markets.
  What does macroeconomics study? :: The economy as a whole — aggregates like GDP, unemployment and inflation.
  The national unemployment rate is a {{macroeconomic}} variable.
  Is a single firm's pricing decision micro or macro? :: Micro, however large the firm.
  What is an aggregate? :: A total for the whole economy, e.g. aggregate demand.
  Main micro tool? :: Demand and supply (markets).
  Main macro tools? :: The circular flow model, AD/AS and the business cycle.

@ 40a21d83 U1.1.1.2 | Positive and normative economics
**In a sentence**
  Positive economics describes what is and can be tested against evidence; normative economics says what ought to be and rests on value judgements.
**Key terms**
  **Positive statement**: a statement about what is, was or will be that can be tested with data — it may be true or false.
  **Normative statement**: a statement about what should be, based on opinion or values; it cannot be proved true or false.
  **Value judgement**: a view about what is good, fair or desirable.
**Positive economics**
  Examples: "Australia's unemployment rate was 4.6% in August 2026." "A higher tobacco excise reduces the number of cigarettes sold." "Raising the minimum wage by 6% will raise the incomes of award-reliant workers."
  Test: could you check it with data or an experiment? If yes, it is positive — even if it turns out to be wrong.
**Normative economics**
  Examples: "The minimum wage should be raised." "Inequality in Australia is too high." "Governments ought to spend more on public transport than on roads."
  Signal words: should, ought, too, fair, unfair, better, worse, deserve.
**Why the distinction matters**
  Economists can settle positive disagreements with evidence; normative disagreements come down to values (efficiency vs equity, present vs future).
  Most policy debates mix both: evidence on what a policy does (positive) feeds a judgement about whether to do it (normative).
**Worked example**
  Classify: (a) "The RBA raised the cash rate to 4.60% in September 2026." (b) "Interest rates are too high for young home buyers." (c) "Higher interest rates reduce consumer spending." (d) "The government should cut GST on groceries."
    (a) Positive — a checkable fact (and true).
    (b) Normative — "too high" is a judgement.
    (c) Positive — a testable cause-and-effect claim.
    (d) Normative — "should" signals a recommendation based on values.
**Going deeper**
  Positive economics is not value-free in practice: choosing which question to study, or which data to use, involves judgement. But the statements themselves can still be classified.
  A normative argument is stronger when it is backed by positive evidence: "Because the Age Pension is below the poverty line for renters (positive), it should be raised (normative)."
**Common mistakes**
  Calling a statement normative because it is false. "Australia has no unemployment" is false, but it is positive — it can be tested.
  Calling a statement positive because it contains numbers. "A 6% wage rise is fair" contains a number but is normative.
**Exam tips**
  To justify a classification, say why: "positive because it can be tested against ABS data" or "normative because it contains the value judgement 'fair'".
**Connections**
  Microeconomics and macroeconomics: both use positive and normative statements.
  Trade-offs and cost-benefit analysis: weighing costs and benefits often ends in a normative choice.
  Evaluating responses to the issue (Unit 2 Area of Study 2).
**Practice questions**
  Explain the difference between positive and normative economic statements, with an example of each.
    A positive statement can be tested with evidence, e.g. "Unemployment rose to 4.6% in August 2026." A normative statement expresses a value judgement that can't be tested, e.g. "The government should do more to reduce unemployment."
  "A tax on sugary drinks would reduce obesity, so the government should introduce one." Identify the positive and normative parts.
    Positive: "a tax on sugary drinks would reduce obesity" (testable). Normative: "the government should introduce one" (a recommendation based on values).
  Can a positive statement be false? Explain.
    Yes. A positive statement is one that can be tested; testing may show it is false, but it remains positive.
**Flashcards**
  What is a positive statement? :: A statement about what is that can be tested with evidence.
  What is a normative statement? :: A value judgement about what ought to be; it can't be tested.
  Words like "should", "fair" and "too high" signal a {{normative}} statement.
  "Higher interest rates reduce spending" — positive or normative? :: Positive: it can be tested.
  Can a positive statement be false? :: Yes — it is still testable.
  Why do most policy debates involve both? :: Evidence on effects (positive) informs a judgement on what to do (normative).
  Classify "The minimum wage should rise 6%." :: Normative.

@ 73953b70 U1.1.1.3 | Resources and needs and wants
**In a sentence**
  Everything we produce comes from four kinds of resources — land, labour, capital and enterprise — and we use them to satisfy needs, which keep us alive, and wants, which never run out.
**Key terms**
  **Resources (factors of production)**: the inputs used to produce goods and services.
  **Land (natural resources)**: gifts of nature — minerals, soil, water, forests, fish, sunlight, wind.
  **Labour**: human effort, physical and mental, used in production; it has quantity (hours, workers) and quality (skills, education — human capital).
  **Capital**: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services — machinery, tools, buildings, vehicles, software, infrastructure.
  **Enterprise (entrepreneurship)**: the organising of the other resources and bearing of risk to start and run a business.
  **Need**: something required for survival — food, water, shelter, clothing, basic health care.
  **Want**: something desired beyond survival — a new phone, a holiday, a streaming subscription.
**The factors and their rewards**
  Each factor earns an income: land earns rent, labour earns wages, capital earns interest, enterprise earns profit.
  These incomes flow to households, which own the factors — the link to the circular flow model.
**Australian examples**
  Land: iron ore in the Pilbara, the Murray–Darling Basin's water, Victorian farmland, offshore gas.
  Labour: about 14.8 million employed people in 2026, from nurses to software engineers.
  Capital: the Metro Tunnel, a dairy's robotic milking machines, a café's coffee machine, cloud servers.
  Enterprise: the founders of Canva, or a local bakery owner who risks their savings.
**Needs versus wants**
  Needs are limited; wants are unlimited — satisfy one want and another appears.
  The line depends on context: a car is a want in inner Melbourne but close to a need for work in rural Victoria.
**Worked example**
  A bakery uses flour, an oven, the baker's work, its shop and the owner's decision to open a second store. Classify each resource.
    Flour: comes from wheat, a natural resource, so it traces back to land (strictly, milled flour is an intermediate good made from land).
    Oven: capital — man-made and used to produce bread.
    Baker's work: labour.
    The shop building: capital.
    The owner's decision and risk: enterprise.
**Going deeper**
  Money is not capital in the economist's sense — it is a claim on resources. "Capital" means real productive goods; "financial capital" is the money used to buy them.
  Human capital (skills, education, health) raises the quality of labour, which is why governments fund schools and TAFE.
  Some resources are renewable (fish stocks, forests if managed) and some are non-renewable (coal, iron ore) — central to environmental sustainability in Unit 2.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling money a resource. Money buys resources; it isn't one.
  Calling a factory worker "capital". Workers are labour; the machines they use are capital.
  Treating needs and wants as fixed lists — context matters.
**Exam tips**
  Name the specific factor and give its reward if asked how resources link to incomes.
**Connections**
  Relative scarcity: resources are limited relative to wants.
  The production possibility frontier: resources and technology fix the frontier.
  The three-sector circular flow model: households own resources and earn factor incomes.
**Practice questions**
  Identify and give an example of each of the factors of production.
    Land — natural resources such as iron ore; labour — human effort such as a nurse's work; capital — man-made goods used in production such as a tractor; enterprise — the risk-taking and organisation of an entrepreneur starting a business.
  Distinguish between a need and a want, using an example of each.
    A need is required for survival, such as food or shelter; a want is desired but not essential, such as a new gaming console.
  Why isn't money considered capital by economists?
    Capital means man-made goods used to produce other goods. Money doesn't produce anything itself; it is a means of buying resources.
**Flashcards**
  Four factors of production? :: Land, labour, capital and enterprise.
  What is capital? :: Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services.
  Reward for each factor? :: Land — rent; labour — wages; capital — interest; enterprise — profit.
  Iron ore in the ground is an example of {{land}}.
  What is human capital? :: The skills, education and health that raise the quality of labour.
  Is money capital? :: No — it buys resources but produces nothing itself.
  Needs are limited but wants are {{unlimited}}.

@ 02939696 U1.1.1.4 | Relative scarcity
**In a sentence**
  Because resources are limited compared with our unlimited wants, every person, business and country must choose how to use them — that is the basic economic problem.
**Key terms**
  **Relative scarcity**: the situation where resources are limited relative to the unlimited needs and wants they could satisfy.
  **Basic economic problem**: how to allocate scarce resources to best satisfy society's needs and wants.
  **Economic decision-making**: choosing between alternative uses of scarce resources.
**Relative, not absolute**
  Scarcity is relative: there may be plenty of water in the world, but not enough to satisfy every farmer, household, mine and river at once. Something can be common and still scarce.
  Absolute scarcity (none at all) is rare; relative scarcity is universal — it affects billionaires (time) and governments (budgets) as much as students.
**Why it forces decisions**
  If we can't have everything, we must choose — and every choice means giving something up (opportunity cost).
  Society must answer the three economic questions: what and how much to produce, how, and for whom.
**Australian examples**
  Water in the Murray–Darling Basin: irrigators, towns and the environment all want more than the river provides.
  The health budget: money for a new hospital in one suburb isn't available for mental-health services.
  A student's time: 24 hours to split between study, work, sport, sleep and friends.
**Worked example**
  A council has \$5 million. Residents want a new pool (\$4 million), road repairs (\$3 million) and a library upgrade (\$2 million). Explain how this shows relative scarcity.
    Total wants cost \$9 million but only \$5 million is available: resources are limited relative to wants.
    The council must decide — for example the pool alone, or roads plus library — and whatever it doesn't fund is given up.
**Going deeper**
  Technology and growth push out the limits of what can be produced, but wants grow too, so scarcity never disappears.
  Free goods (air to breathe, in most places) are the exception: when there's more than enough for everyone at zero price, no choice is needed. Clean air in a polluted city, though, is scarce.
**Common mistakes**
  Confusing scarcity with a shortage. A shortage is a market situation (quantity demanded exceeds supply at the current price); scarcity is permanent and universal.
  Saying rich countries don't face scarcity. They still must choose.
**Exam tips**
  Define relative scarcity precisely ("limited resources relative to unlimited wants") and then link it to the need to choose and to opportunity cost.
**Connections**
  Resources and needs and wants: the two sides of the problem.
  Opportunity cost: the price of every choice scarcity forces.
  The three basic economic questions: what society must decide because of scarcity.
**Practice questions**
  Explain the basic economic problem of relative scarcity.
    Resources such as land, labour and capital are limited, while needs and wants are unlimited. So society can't satisfy every want and must decide how to allocate resources among competing uses.
  Why does relative scarcity affect even very wealthy people?
    Their wants still exceed their resources in some way — especially time — so they must still make choices and give things up.
  Distinguish between scarcity and a shortage.
    Scarcity is the permanent condition of limited resources relative to wants. A shortage is a temporary market situation where, at the current price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
**Flashcards**
  What is relative scarcity? :: Limited resources relative to unlimited needs and wants.
  What is the basic economic problem? :: How to allocate scarce resources to best satisfy society's needs and wants.
  Scarcity is {{relative}}, not absolute.
  Why does scarcity force decisions? :: We can't have everything, so we must choose between alternative uses.
  Scarcity vs shortage? :: Scarcity is permanent and universal; a shortage is a temporary market imbalance at a price.
  Australian example of relative scarcity? :: Water in the Murray–Darling Basin, wanted by farmers, towns and the environment.
  Does growth end scarcity? :: No — wants grow too.

@ 16939555 U1.1.1.5 | Opportunity cost
**In a sentence**
  The opportunity cost of any choice is the value of the next best alternative you give up to make it.
**Key terms**
  **Opportunity cost**: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  **Next best alternative**: the single most valued option you didn't choose — not every option.
  **Trade-off**: giving up some of one thing to get more of another.
**What it includes**
  Money is only part of it: time, effort and enjoyment count too.
  The cost of going to university is not just the fees (a HECS debt) but the wages you could have earned working full-time.
**Applications**
  Individuals: a Saturday shift at \$20 an hour means giving up footy training (if that was your next best use of the time).
  Businesses: a farmer who plants wheat gives up the canola that land could have grown; a café that buys a second coffee machine gives up the money's next best use, such as new seating.
  Governments: \$10 billion on a new motorway is \$10 billion not spent on hospitals, schools or tax cuts.
  Society: using land for housing means it can't be used for farming or bushland.
**Worked example**
  Ella has a free Saturday. Her options, in order of preference: (1) work a 6-hour shift at \$25 an hour; (2) study for a SAC; (3) go to the beach. She chooses to work. What is her opportunity cost? If she had chosen to study, what would it have been?
    Choosing work: the opportunity cost is the next best alternative, studying for the SAC — not the beach as well.
    Choosing study: the opportunity cost is the shift, which is worth 6 × \$25 = \$150 in wages (plus anything else she valued about working).
    Sense check: opportunity cost is always one alternative — the best one given up.
**Going deeper**
  Sunk costs — money already spent that can't be recovered — are not part of opportunity cost. If a non-refundable \$50 concert ticket clashes with a better option, the \$50 is gone either way; the decision should rest only on what each option gives from now on.
  Opportunity cost explains specialisation and trade: countries produce what they give up least to make (comparative advantage, Unit 2).
**Common mistakes**
  Adding up all the alternatives. Only the next best one counts.
  Counting only money. Time and satisfaction are costs too.
  Counting sunk costs.
**Exam tips**
  Name the specific alternative forgone: "The opportunity cost of the \$10 billion motorway is the next best use of those funds, such as a new hospital."
**Connections**
  Relative scarcity: the reason every choice has an opportunity cost.
  The production possibility frontier: its slope shows opportunity cost.
  Trade-offs and cost-benefit analysis.
**Practice questions**
  Define opportunity cost and give an example for a government.
    The value of the next best alternative forgone. If the government spends \$2 billion on defence, the opportunity cost might be the new public hospitals that money could have built.
  Sam gives up a \$120 shift to go to a friend's party. His next best alternative was the shift. What is his opportunity cost?
    The shift and the \$120 of wages it would have paid.
  Why should a sunk cost be ignored when making a decision?
    It has already been paid and can't be recovered whichever option is chosen, so it doesn't change what each option gives up from now on.
**Flashcards**
  What is opportunity cost? :: The value of the next best alternative forgone.
  Does opportunity cost include every alternative? :: No — only the next best one.
  Does opportunity cost include non-money costs? :: Yes — time, effort, enjoyment.
  A cost already paid that can't be recovered is a {{sunk cost}}.
  Opportunity cost of going to uni full-time? :: The next best use of the time — e.g. full-time wages — as well as fees.
  Why does every choice have an opportunity cost? :: Relative scarcity means choosing one use gives up another.
  Calculate: a 5-hour shift at \$24 an hour is forgone. Money value of the opportunity cost? :: \$120.

@ 5278c60e U1.1.1.6 | The production possibility frontier
**In a sentence**
  The production possibility frontier (PPF) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its resources and technology, so it pictures scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency and unused resources in one graph.
**Key terms**
  **Production possibility frontier (PPF)**: a curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods that can be produced when all resources are used fully and efficiently with the current technology.
  **Productive efficiency**: producing on the frontier — getting the most output from the resources available.
  **Under-utilisation of resources**: resources idle or not used to capacity (unemployment, empty factories), shown by a point inside the frontier.
  **Unattainable**: a point outside the frontier — impossible with current resources and technology.
**Reading the model**
  Axes: quantity of one good on each (e.g. wool bales and wheat tonnes).
  On the curve: efficient — all resources employed.
  Inside the curve: under-utilisation — the economy could produce more of both goods (Australia in mid-2020, when COVID lockdowns left workers and businesses idle).
  Outside the curve: unattainable — for now.
**What the PPF illustrates**
  Scarcity: the frontier is a limit; we can't reach points beyond it.
  Choice: society must pick one point on (or inside) the curve.
  Opportunity cost: moving along the curve means giving up some of one good to get more of the other.
  Efficiency and under-utilisation: on the curve vs inside it.
**Why the curve bows outward**
  Resources aren't equally good at making both goods. The first resources moved into wool are those least useful for wheat, so little wheat is lost; the last are the best wheat land, so a lot is lost. Opportunity cost increases as you produce more of one good.
  A straight-line PPF would mean constant opportunity cost (resources equally suited to both).
**Shifting the frontier**
  Outward: more resources (skilled migration, new mineral finds), better quality resources (education, training), better technology or productivity (AI tools, better seed varieties). This is economic growth.
  Inward: loss of resources (a severe drought, bushfires, a pandemic that kills or disables workers, emigration).
  If new technology helps only one good, the frontier pivots outward on that axis.
**Worked example**
  A farm's possible output: A (0 wool, 100 wheat), B (10 wool, 90 wheat), C (20 wool, 70 wheat), D (30 wool, 40 wheat), E (40 wool, 0 wheat). (a) Find the opportunity cost of each extra 10 bales of wool. (b) What does producing 20 wool and 50 wheat show? (c) Can it produce 30 wool and 60 wheat?
    (a) A→B: 100 − 90 = 10 t wheat (1 t per bale). B→C: 90 − 70 = 20 t (2 t per bale). C→D: 70 − 40 = 30 t (3 t per bale). D→E: 40 − 0 = 40 t (4 t per bale).
    The opportunity cost increases, which is why the curve bows outward.
    (b) 20 wool with 50 wheat is inside the frontier (the farm could make 70 wheat with 20 wool): under-utilisation — 20 t of wheat is being lost.
    (c) No: with 30 wool the most wheat is 40 t, so (30, 60) is outside the frontier — unattainable unless resources or technology improve.
**Going deeper**
  Choosing capital goods (machinery, infrastructure) over consumer goods today gives up some present consumption but pushes the frontier out faster in future — a trade-off between living standards now and later.
  The PPF shows productive efficiency, not allocative efficiency: every point on the curve is productively efficient, but only one is the combination society values most.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying a point inside the curve is "unattainable". It is attainable — just inefficient.
  Shifting the curve when resources become unemployed. Unemployment moves the economy to a point inside the curve; the curve only shifts when the quantity or quality of resources or technology changes.
  Measuring opportunity cost in the good you gain instead of the good you give up.
**Exam tips**
  Label axes, the curve, and every point. When explaining a movement, state the opportunity cost in units of the good given up.
**Connections**
  Opportunity cost: the slope of the frontier.
  Resources and needs and wants: resources and technology fix where the frontier lies.
  Measuring growth with real GDP (Unit 2): an outward shift of the frontier.
**Practice questions**
  Using a PPF, explain the difference between a point on the curve and a point inside it.
    A point on the curve is productively efficient: all resources are fully used. A point inside shows under-utilisation, e.g. unemployment: more of both goods could be produced without giving anything up.
  Explain why a PPF is usually bowed outward.
    Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods, so as more of one good is produced, increasingly unsuitable resources are moved across and the opportunity cost rises.
  What would shift Australia's PPF outward? Give two examples.
    An increase in the quantity or quality of resources or better technology — e.g. skilled migration adding workers, or AI and automation raising productivity.
  A drought destroys crops for a year. How would you show this on a PPF of food and manufactured goods?
    The frontier shifts (pivots) inward on the food axis, because the productive capacity of land has fallen.
**Flashcards**
  What does the PPF show? :: The maximum combinations of two goods producible with current resources and technology.
  A point inside the PPF shows {{under-utilisation}} of resources.
  A point outside the PPF is {{unattainable}} with current resources and technology.
  Why is the PPF bowed outward? :: Increasing opportunity cost — resources aren't equally suited to both goods.
  What shifts the PPF outward? :: More or better resources, or improved technology/productivity.
  Does rising unemployment shift the PPF? :: No — it moves the economy to a point inside it.
  Calculate: wheat falls from 90 t to 70 t as wool rises from 10 to 20 bales. Opportunity cost per bale? :: 2 t of wheat.
  Five concepts the PPF illustrates? :: Scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency and under-utilisation.

@ 8287dbf8 U1.1.1.7 | Trade-offs and cost-benefit analysis
**In a sentence**
  Because we can't have everything, decision-makers trade off one thing for another, and cost-benefit analysis weighs up the costs and benefits of each option — with opportunity cost as the key cost.
**Key terms**
  **Trade-off**: accepting less of one thing in order to get more of another.
  **Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)**: a systematic comparison of the costs and benefits of a decision, in money terms where possible, to see whether it is worthwhile.
  **Private costs and benefits**: those borne or received by the decision-maker.
  **External (social) costs and benefits**: effects on third parties, such as pollution or reduced traffic.
**Trade-offs**
  Every trade-off has an opportunity cost: what you give up is the cost of what you get.
  Individuals trade off study vs work, spending vs saving; businesses trade off wages vs new machines; governments trade off tax cuts vs services, growth vs environment, efficiency vs equity.
**How a cost-benefit analysis works**
  1. List the options, including doing nothing.
  2. Identify costs and benefits for all affected groups, now and in the future, including external ones.
  3. Put a money value on them where possible (and describe the rest).
  4. Compare: proceed if benefits exceed costs; choose the option with the largest net benefit.
  5. Remember the opportunity cost: the benefit of the best alternative use of the resources.
**Relationship to opportunity cost**
  A project's true cost is what else the resources could have done. A CBA that ignores that alternative overstates the case for the project.
  So a project should go ahead only if its net benefit is greater than the net benefit of the next best alternative.
**Worked example**
  A council considers a \$6 million skate park. Estimated benefits over 10 years: \$4 million in health and recreation value, \$1.5 million from reduced youth crime, \$1 million in extra trade for nearby shops. Costs: \$6 million to build, \$0.8 million in maintenance, and some noise for neighbours (not valued). Should it proceed?
    Benefits: 4 + 1.5 + 1 = \$6.5 million.
    Measurable costs: 6 + 0.8 = \$6.8 million.
    Net benefit: 6.5 − 6.8 = −\$0.3 million, before counting the noise.
    On these numbers, costs exceed benefits, so it shouldn't proceed — unless the council values unmeasured benefits (community pride) highly. And even a positive result would need to beat the best alternative use of \$6 million.
**Going deeper**
  CBA is only as good as its estimates: benefits such as lives saved or clean air are hard to value, and future amounts are usually "discounted" because a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in ten years.
  Who gains and who pays matters too: a project with a net benefit can still leave some groups worse off, which is a normative question about fairness.
**Common mistakes**
  Leaving out external costs and benefits.
  Leaving out the opportunity cost — comparing the project only with "nothing".
  Treating a CBA result as the final answer; unquantified factors and equity still matter.
**Exam tips**
  In a "discuss the costs and benefits" answer, cover at least two groups (e.g. consumers and government), short and long term, and end with a judgement based on an economic criterion (living standards, efficiency, equity).
**Connections**
  Opportunity cost: the central cost in every trade-off.
  The roles of government: CBA is used to judge public projects.
  Benefits of economic growth and Costs of economic growth (Unit 2).
**Practice questions**
  Explain the relationship between a trade-off and opportunity cost.
    A trade-off means accepting less of one thing to get more of another; the amount given up is the opportunity cost of what is gained.
  Outline the steps in a cost-benefit analysis.
    List the options; identify private and external costs and benefits for all affected groups, now and later; value them in money where possible; compare them, choosing the option with the greatest net benefit, and compare with the best alternative use of the resources.
  Why might a project with benefits greater than costs still be rejected?
    The same resources might produce an even larger net benefit elsewhere (a higher opportunity cost), or the gains and losses may be distributed unfairly.

  **Businesses (producers, firms)**: hire factors to produce goods and services.

  **Factor incomes**: rent, wages, interest and profit paid to households for their resources.

**The flows**
  Households → firms: resources (land, labour, capital, enterprise), a real flow.
  Firms → households: factor incomes (rent, wages, interest, profit), a money flow.
  Firms → households: goods and services, a real flow.
  Households → firms: consumption spending, a money flow.
  Households and firms → government: taxes (income tax, GST, company tax).
  Government → households and firms: spending on goods and services (roads, schools, hospitals), wages of public servants, and transfer payments to households.
**Real and money flows**

**What the model shows**
  One agent's spending is another's income. If households cut spending, firms' revenue falls, they employ fewer workers, and household incomes fall — activity shrinks.

**Worked example**

    Households keep more of their income (a smaller leakage), so disposable income rises.
    Consumption spending rises, so firms' revenue rises.

    Government tax revenue from income tax falls, so it must spend less, borrow or collect more elsewhere.
**Going deeper**
  The model is simplified: it has no saving, investment, banks or trade. The five-sector model in Unit 2 adds the financial sector (saving and investment) and the overseas sector (exports and imports).

  Drawing the arrows the wrong way — resources go from households to firms, incomes go from firms to households.
  Calling transfer payments income from production. They are payments for which no good or service is given in return.
  Forgetting that households pay taxes and receive government services as well as transfers.

**Connections**

  In the traditional model, owners (shareholders) want the highest return, and managers act for them.
**How firms raise profit**
  Raise revenue: sell more, raise prices (if demand allows), launch products, enter new markets.
  Cut costs: better technology, cheaper inputs, higher productivity, economies of scale.

**Common mistakes**
  Confusing revenue with profit.
  Assuming a higher price always means higher profit — sales may fall by more.
**Exam tips**
  Show revenue and costs separately before stating profit, with units.
**Connections**

  The law of supply: higher prices raise the profit from supplying more.
