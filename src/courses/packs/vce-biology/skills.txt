@ d3bc4dd5 S.1.1 | Constructing aims and questions
**In a sentence**
  A good investigation starts with a focused, researchable question that names what will be changed or compared and what will be measured.
**Key ideas**
  A good question is specific, testable and achievable with the time and equipment you have.
    Weak: "Does salt affect plants?" Better: "How does the concentration of sodium chloride (0–200 mM) in watering solution affect the height of radish seedlings after 14 days?"
  An aim states what the investigation will do: "To investigate the effect of … on …" or "To determine …".
  Research the background first, so the question is worth asking and you know what's already known.
**Practice questions**
  Improve this question: "Do enzymes work better when warm?"
    "How does temperature (10–60 °C) affect the rate at which catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide, measured as the volume of oxygen produced in 2 minutes?"
  Why should an investigation question be achievable with school equipment?
    Otherwise you can't generate enough reliable data to answer it in the time and with the resources available.
**Flashcards**
  What makes a research question good? :: It is specific, testable, names the variables, and is achievable.
  How does an aim usually begin? :: "To investigate…" or "To determine…".
  Turn "Does light affect plants?" into a better question. :: E.g. "How does light intensity affect the rate of transpiration of a leafy shoot, measured with a potometer?"

@ 1d1c4cc7 S.1.2 | Independent, dependent and controlled variables
**In a sentence**
  Change one thing (independent variable), measure one thing (dependent variable), and keep everything else the same (controlled variables).
**Key ideas**
  **Independent variable**: the one you deliberately change.
  **Dependent variable**: the one you measure, which responds.
  **Controlled variables**: everything else that could affect the result, kept the same so the test is fair.
  A **control** (control group or experimental control) is a separate treatment for comparison — e.g. potato strips in distilled water, or plants given no fertiliser. It is not the same as a controlled variable.
  Example — effect of sucrose concentration on potato mass: independent = sucrose concentration; dependent = percentage change in mass; controlled = strip size, potato variety, temperature, time in solution, volume of solution.
**Practice questions**
  An investigation tests how exercise duration affects heart rate. Identify the variables.
    Independent: exercise duration. Dependent: heart rate (beats per minute). Controlled: type and intensity of exercise, time of measurement after exercise, the person's resting state, room temperature.
  What is the difference between a controlled variable and a control?
    A controlled variable is kept constant in every trial; a control is a separate comparison treatment (e.g. no treatment) to show what happens without the independent variable.
**Flashcards**
  Define the independent variable. :: The variable deliberately changed by the investigator.
  Define the dependent variable. :: The variable measured, which responds to the independent variable.
  Why keep controlled variables constant? :: So any change in the dependent variable can be attributed to the independent variable.
  In testing light intensity on transpiration, name two controlled variables. :: Temperature, humidity, air movement, the plant species or leaf area.

@ f13732eb S.1.3 | Formulating hypotheses
**In a sentence**
  A hypothesis is a testable prediction about how the independent variable will affect the dependent variable, with a biological reason.
**Key ideas**
  Common form: "If [independent variable changes], then [dependent variable will change in this way], because [biological reason]."
  It must be testable and falsifiable — the results must be able to show it's wrong.
  Results support or don't support a hypothesis; they never prove it.
**Practice questions**
  Write a hypothesis for: "How does temperature affect the rate of transpiration?"
    If temperature increases, then the rate of transpiration will increase, because water evaporates faster from the mesophyll cells and water vapour molecules diffuse out of the stomata more quickly.
  Is a hypothesis useful if the results don't support it?
    Yes — rejecting a hypothesis is new knowledge and points to a better explanation.
**Flashcards**
  What is the "If … then … because" structure for? :: Writing a hypothesis linking the variables and giving a biological reason.
  What makes a hypothesis scientific? :: It is testable and could be shown to be false.
  Can results prove a hypothesis? :: No — they support or fail to support it.

@ d30a227c S.1.4 | Predicting possible outcomes
**In a sentence**
  Before you start, use biology to predict what you expect to see — it guides the method and gives you something to compare the results with.
**Key ideas**
  A prediction is the specific result expected in this experiment: "Strips in 0.6 M sucrose will lose the most mass; strips in water will gain mass."
  Predictions help you choose sensible ranges and instruments (will a 0.01 g balance detect the change?).
  Predict a trend and, if possible, a shape (e.g. mass change falls as concentration rises, crossing zero where the solution is isotonic).
**Practice questions**
  Predict the result of placing onion epidermis cells in a concentrated salt solution.
    The cells will lose water by osmosis; the cytoplasm will shrink and pull away from the cell wall (plasmolysis).
  How does a prediction differ from a hypothesis?
    A hypothesis is a general, reasoned statement about the relationship between variables; a prediction is the specific outcome expected in this particular experiment if the hypothesis is correct.
**Flashcards**
  What is a prediction? :: The specific result expected in an experiment, based on the hypothesis and biology.
  Why predict before starting? :: To choose suitable ranges and equipment and to have something to compare results with.
  Predict: red blood cells in distilled water. :: They gain water by osmosis, swell and burst.

@ 5928776a S.2.1 | Choosing an investigation methodology
**In a sentence**
  Match the type of investigation to the question: experiments for cause and effect, correlational studies and fieldwork for relationships in nature, literature reviews and case studies for questions answered from existing information.
**Key ideas**
  **Case study**: an in-depth look at a real or hypothetical situation (e.g. the spread of devil facial tumour disease).
  **Classification and identification**: sorting organisms into groups or identifying them (using a dichotomous key).
  **Controlled experiment**: change one variable, measure another, control the rest — shows cause and effect.
  **Correlational study**: measure variables that aren't manipulated to look for associations (smoking and lung cancer rates).
  **Fieldwork**: observation and sampling in a natural environment (quadrats, transects).
  **Literature review**: collating and analysing others' findings from secondary sources.
  **Modelling**: physical, conceptual or mathematical representations (agar cubes, a population model).
  **Product, process or system development**: designing something to meet a need.
  **Simulation**: using a model to study a system that can't easily be studied directly.
**Practice questions**
  Which methodology suits "Is the density of a native grass related to distance from a walking track?"
    Fieldwork using a correlational approach (e.g. quadrats along a transect), because the variables are measured in place, not manipulated.
  Why can't a correlational study show that one variable causes another?
    Other variables weren't controlled, so a third factor could cause both, or the relationship could be coincidence.
**Flashcards**
  Which methodology shows cause and effect? :: A controlled experiment.
  What is a literature review? :: Collating and analysing others' findings from secondary sources.
  Using quadrats along a transect is an example of {{fieldwork}}.
  What does a correlational study show? :: An association between variables, not cause.

@ cd150219 S.2.2 | Designing and conducting investigations
**In a sentence**
  A good design chooses a suitable sample and sample size, equipment and procedure, allows for sources of error, and decides in advance what data to collect and how much.
**Key ideas**
  **Sampling**: choose samples that represent the population (random sampling avoids bias). Bigger samples make results more reliable.
  **Replicates**: repeat each treatment (e.g. five strips per concentration) to reduce the effect of random variation.
  **Equipment**: enough resolution for the change you're measuring; calibrate before use.
  **Procedure**: clear, numbered steps someone else could follow.
  **Data plan**: which qualitative and quantitative data, how many readings, over what range.
  Field sampling (going further): quadrats for plants and slow animals; transects to sample along a gradient; capture–mark–recapture for mobile animals — estimated population = (number marked × number captured second time) ÷ number recaptured with marks.
**Worked example**
  20 lizards were caught, marked and released. Later 25 were caught, of which 5 were marked. Estimate the population.
    Population ≈ (20 × 25) ÷ 5 = 500 ÷ 5 = 100 lizards.
**Practice questions**
  Why is random sampling important in fieldwork?
    It avoids choosing unusual or convenient spots, so the sample is more likely to represent the whole area.
  A student tests one leaf at each light intensity. Suggest an improvement and explain it.
    Use several leaves (e.g. five) per light intensity and calculate the mean; this reduces the effect of random variation between leaves and increases confidence in the results.
**Flashcards**
  Why use a large sample size? :: To reduce the effect of random variation and make results more representative.
  What are replicates? :: Repeated samples or trials of the same treatment.
  Capture–mark–recapture: 30 marked, 40 caught later, 10 marked among them. Population? :: (30 × 40) ÷ 10 = 120.
  What does random sampling avoid? :: Bias from choosing unusual or convenient samples.

**In a sentence**

  What should you do when a method needs changing mid-investigation? :: Make the change and record what changed and why in the logbook.
  Name two constraints on a school investigation. :: Time, equipment, safety, ethics, cost.

@ 768f8adc S.3.1 | Risk assessment and safety data sheets
**In a sentence**
  Before practical work, identify each hazard, judge the risk and decide how to control it, using safety data sheets for any chemicals.
**Key ideas**
  **Hazard**: something that could cause harm. **Risk**: how likely and how serious the harm is. **Control**: what reduces the risk.
  **Safety data sheet (SDS)**: gives a chemical's hazards, safe handling, PPE, first aid, storage and disposal.
  Biology hazards: sharp instruments (scalpels), stains and chemicals (iodine, ethanol, hydrochloric acid), heat, glassware, microorganisms, allergens (pollen, latex).
  A risk assessment is written into your logbook before starting.
**Practice questions**
  Write a risk assessment line for using ethanol to extract chlorophyll.
    Hazard: flammable, irritant liquid. Risk: fire if near a flame; eye irritation. Control: no naked flames (heat in a water bath), wear safety glasses, use small volumes, ventilate.
  Where would you find the correct disposal method for a stain?
    On its safety data sheet (SDS).
**Flashcards**
  What is an SDS? :: A safety data sheet giving a chemical's hazards, handling, first aid and disposal.
  Hazard vs risk? :: A hazard could cause harm; risk is the likelihood and severity of that harm.
  A risk assessment lists hazards, risks and {{control measures}}.

@ 07d2f53d S.3.2 | Occupational health and safety in the lab
**In a sentence**
  Follow safe lab practice every time: wear PPE, handle equipment and microbes correctly, keep the bench clean, and dispose of waste properly.
**Key ideas**
  PPE: safety glasses, lab coat, gloves where needed, closed shoes, hair tied back.
  Aseptic technique with microorganisms: disinfect benches, flame or sterilise equipment, keep lids on plates, seal and label plates, never open incubated plates, autoclave waste.
  Sharps: cut away from yourself onto a tile; dispose of blades in a sharps container.
  Know where the eyewash, fire blanket and first aid are; report all accidents and spills.
**Practice questions**
  Give two aseptic techniques used when culturing bacteria.
    Disinfect the bench before and after; flame the inoculating loop; keep plates closed and sealed; incubate at temperatures that don't favour human pathogens (e.g. 25–30 °C).
  Why should incubated Petri dishes not be opened?
    Colonies may include harmful microbes; opening the dish could release them.
**Flashcards**
  Name three items of PPE. :: Safety glasses, lab coat, gloves (also closed shoes).
  What is aseptic technique? :: Methods that prevent contamination of cultures and exposure to microbes.
  How should used scalpel blades be disposed of? :: In a sharps container.

@ a2cd6a79 S.3.3 | Ethical conduct in investigations
**In a sentence**
  Behave ethically in how you run and report investigations: be honest, protect human participants, minimise harm to animals and the environment, and acknowledge others' work.
**Key ideas**
  Honesty: record and report all data truthfully, including results that don't fit; never invent or alter data.
  Human participants: informed consent, the right to withdraw, privacy and confidentiality, no harm.
  Animals: the 3 Rs — replace, reduce, refine; animal ethics approval.
  Environment: minimise disturbance in fieldwork; return organisms; take only what's needed.
  Acknowledge sources and help.
**Practice questions**
  A student leaves out two trials that didn't match the others. Is this ethical?
    No, unless there is a clear, recorded reason (e.g. a known mistake). Omitting data because it doesn't fit is dishonest; it should be reported and discussed.
  What does informed consent require?
    That participants are told what the study involves, including any risks, and freely agree, knowing they can withdraw at any time.
**Flashcards**
  The 3 Rs of animal research? :: Replace, reduce, refine.
  Name two ethical requirements for human participants. :: Informed consent and confidentiality (also the right to withdraw, no harm).
  Is leaving out data that don't fit ethical? :: No — all data should be reported unless there is a recorded valid reason.

@ d6096133 S.4.4 | Graphing linear and non-linear relationships
**In a sentence**
  Plot the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis, with even scales and labels, and draw a line or curve of best fit that shows whether the relationship is linear or not.
**Key ideas**
  Title that names both variables; axes labelled with quantity and unit; even scales that use most of the grid.
  Plot points accurately; draw a smooth line or curve of best fit through the trend, not dot-to-dot, unless readings are a sequence over time.
  Linear: a straight line (constant rate of change). Non-linear: a curve — e.g. exponential bacterial growth, an enzyme's activity rising then falling with temperature, a population levelling off at carrying capacity.
**Practice questions**
  Sketch and describe the shape of a graph of bacterial numbers against time in ideal conditions.
    A curve getting steeper over time — exponential growth, as the number doubles each generation.
  Why should a line of best fit not be drawn dot-to-dot?
    Individual points include random error; a line of best fit shows the overall trend.
**Flashcards**
  Which variable goes on the y-axis? :: The dependent variable.
  Describe a non-linear relationship. :: One where the rate of change is not constant, giving a curve.
  Name a biological example of exponential growth. :: Bacteria dividing by binary fission in ideal conditions.

@ 363e0850 S.5.1 | Processing quantitative data
**In a sentence**
  Turn raw numbers into meaningful values — means, ratios, percentages and percentage change — with correct units and sensible decimal places.
**Key ideas**
  Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values.
  Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100.
  Percentage change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100 (negative for a decrease).
  Ratios: simplify to the smallest whole numbers or to x : 1 (e.g. 12 : 4 = 3 : 1; SA:V 24 : 8 = 3 : 1).
  Rate = change ÷ time (e.g. mm per minute).
**Worked example**
  Of 160 offspring, 118 have the dominant phenotype. Express the ratio and compare it with 3 : 1.
    Recessive = 160 − 118 = 42.
    Ratio = 118 : 42 = 2.8 : 1.
    Close to the expected 3 : 1 for a monohybrid cross of two heterozygotes.
**Practice questions**
  A plant grew from 12.0 cm to 15.6 cm. Calculate the percentage change.
    (15.6 − 12.0) ÷ 12.0 × 100 = +30%.
  In a class of 25, 5 are colour-blind. What percentage is that?
    5 ÷ 25 × 100 = 20%.
**Flashcards**
  Formula for percentage change? :: (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  Simplify 45 : 15. :: 3 : 1.
  Mean of 4, 6 and 8? :: 6.
  A value falls from 50 to 40. Percentage change? :: −20%.

@ 0a8351a0 S.5.2 | Data quality: accuracy, precision, errors and sample size
**In a sentence**
  Judge your data by its accuracy, precision, repeatability, reproducibility and validity, identify random and systematic errors, and remember that bigger samples give more certainty.
**Key ideas**
  Accuracy — close to the true value. Precision — repeated values close together.
  Repeatability — same conditions, same person. Reproducibility — different person, method or equipment.
  Validity — measures what it's meant to; experiment tests what it claims.
  **Random errors**: unpredictable scatter; reduce precision; reduced by more readings and averaging.
  **Systematic errors**: consistent shift in one direction (e.g. uncalibrated instrument); reduce accuracy; not fixed by repeating — fix the instrument or method.
  **Personal errors** are mistakes (misreading, spilling); they are not measurement errors — redo the trial.
  **Sample size**: small samples are more affected by chance and individual variation; larger samples increase certainty.
**Practice questions**
  A thermometer reads 2 °C too high throughout. What type of error is this and what does it affect?
    A systematic error; it reduces accuracy (precision is unaffected).
  Why is a sample of 5 plants less reliable than a sample of 50?
    With few plants, one unusual plant has a big effect on the mean; a larger sample averages out individual variation.
**Flashcards**
  Random errors affect {{precision}}; systematic errors affect {{accuracy}}.
  How do you reduce random error? :: Take more readings and average them.
  Does repeating trials fix a systematic error? :: No — the instrument or method must be corrected.
  Is misreading a scale a random error? :: No — it's a personal error (a mistake); redo the reading.

@ fbef95a5 S.5.3 | Outliers, contradictory and provisional data
**In a sentence**
  Spot results that don't fit, find out why before deciding what to do with them, and recognise when data conflict or are only provisional.
**Key ideas**
  **Outlier**: a value far from the others. Check for a mistake; exclude only with a recorded reason; otherwise keep it and discuss it. Repeating the reading can help.
  **Contradictory data**: results or sources that disagree — compare methods and conditions to explain why.
  **Provisional data**: results that may change with more evidence (e.g. early trial results for a new therapy).
  **Uncertainty** (qualitative in VCE Biology): the lack of exact knowledge of a value; not the same as error.
**Practice questions**
  Heart rates after exercise: 118, 122, 120, 164, 119 bpm. How should 164 be treated?
    It's an outlier. Check whether there was a mistake (e.g. measured at a different time). If so, exclude it with a stated reason; if not, keep it and discuss it, and repeat if possible.
  Why are results from the first small trial of a new treatment called provisional?
    Small early studies may not be representative; further, larger studies could change the conclusion.
**Flashcards**
  What is an outlier? :: A value that lies far from the other results.
  Should outliers be deleted automatically? :: No — investigate; exclude only with a recorded reason.
  Data that may change with further evidence are {{provisional}}.

@ c2f7f34e S.5.4 | Repeating experiments
**In a sentence**
  Repeating an experiment checks that a result wasn't a fluke — consistent repeats make findings robust, and results others can reproduce are trusted most.
**Key ideas**
  Repeat trials within your investigation to reduce random error and check repeatability.
  Independent repetition by others, with different equipment or methods, tests reproducibility.
  A single result, however striking, is weak evidence.
**Practice questions**
  Why did scientists doubt early claims until other laboratories repeated them?
    A single result could be caused by error, chance or bias; when independent labs reproduce it, confidence grows that it is real.
  How many trials should you do?
    At least three per treatment, and more if the results vary a lot, so the mean is reliable and outliers can be identified.
**Flashcards**
  Why repeat trials? :: To reduce random error, check repeatability and make findings robust.
  What does reproducibility test? :: Whether others get the same result with different people, methods or equipment.
  Is one striking result strong evidence? :: No — it needs to be repeated.

@ e171ecdf S.5.5 | Evaluating methods and suggesting improvements
**In a sentence**
  Look critically at your method for sources of error, mistakes and bias, and suggest specific changes that would improve accuracy and precision.
**Key ideas**
  Identify the weakness → explain its effect on the data → suggest a specific, realistic improvement.
  Sources: uncontrolled variables, imprecise instruments, small samples, inconsistent technique, observer bias (e.g. judging colour change by eye).
  Improvements: control a variable, use a more precise instrument or a data logger, increase sample size, standardise technique, blind the observer.
**Practice questions**
  Students judged when an iodine test turned from blue-black to yellow by eye. Suggest an improvement.
    Use a colorimeter to measure the colour change objectively, or compare against a standard colour chart, to reduce observer judgement and improve precision.
  Why is "be more careful" a poor improvement?
    It is vague — it doesn't identify the problem or give a specific change that would reduce the error.
**Flashcards**
  Three parts of a good evaluation point? :: The weakness, its effect on the data, and a specific improvement.
  How can observer bias be reduced? :: Use an instrument to measure objectively, or blind the observer to the treatment.
  Give an improvement for inconsistent blotting of potato strips. :: Blot each strip the same way (e.g. two presses on the same paper) for a standard time.

**Key ideas**
  Evidence: systematic observations or measurements.

  What makes an idea scientific? :: It can be tested by evidence and possibly shown wrong.

@ 037b8ee4 S.6.2 | How well the evidence supports the aim
**In a sentence**
  Judge whether your data actually answer the aim — fully, partly or not at all — and recommend how the investigation could be changed or extended.
**Key ideas**
  Compare what the aim set out to find with what the data show.
  Consider the range tested, sample size and controls: can you generalise, or only comment on the conditions tested?

@ 8f59e09d S.6.3 | Supporting or refuting a hypothesis
**In a sentence**
  Compare your results with the hypothesis and say whether they support it, partly support it, or don't — quoting the data that show it.
**Key ideas**
  Use "the results support the hypothesis" or "do not support" — never "prove".
  Quote specific data: "mass change fell from +12% in water to −15% in 0.6 M sucrose, supporting the hypothesis".
  If results only partly support it (e.g. true up to 40 °C but not beyond), say so and explain.
**Practice questions**
  Hypothesis: "If light intensity increases, transpiration increases." Results: transpiration rose from 2.0 to 5.5 mm/min as light increased, then levelled off. Evaluate.
    The results partly support the hypothesis: transpiration increased with light at lower intensities, but levelled off at high intensity, when stomata were likely fully open and another factor became limiting.
  Why don't scientists say a hypothesis is "proven"?
    Future evidence could always contradict it; results can only support it.
**Flashcards**
  Which words describe the result of testing a hypothesis? :: Supported or not supported (never "proven").
  What should back up a statement that data support a hypothesis? :: Specific data values from the results.
  Can results partly support a hypothesis? :: Yes — e.g. true over part of the range tested.

@ 978fda40 S.6.4 | Constructing evidence-based conclusions
**In a sentence**
  Build a conclusion by making a claim that answers the question, backing it with evidence from your data, and explaining the reasoning using biology.
**Key ideas**
  Claim → evidence → reasoning.
  Claim: answer the question directly.
  Evidence: the key data or trend.
  Reasoning: the biological explanation linking evidence to claim.
  Don't go beyond the data or introduce new information.
**Worked example**
  Question: How does sucrose concentration affect potato mass?
    Claim: As sucrose concentration increases, potato strips lose more mass.
    Evidence: mean mass change fell from +12% (0 M) to −15% (0.6 M), crossing 0% at about 0.25 M.
    Reasoning: in more concentrated solutions water leaves the cells by osmosis; around 0.25 M the solution is isotonic with the cells.
**Practice questions**
  What three parts make a strong scientific conclusion?
    A claim answering the question, evidence from the data, and reasoning linking them through biology.
  Why shouldn't new information appear in a conclusion?
