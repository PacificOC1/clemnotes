@ bc5092a1 U2.3.1.1 | Primary and secondary data
**In a sentence**
  Primary data is generated first-hand by you; secondary data was collected by someone else — and a bioethics investigation relies mostly on secondary data, so you need to know where it came from.
**Key terms**
  **Primary data**: data generated first-hand by the investigator.
  **Secondary data**: data collected by others that the investigator gathers and uses.
  **Collate**: gather and organise data from several sources.
**In this investigation**
  You research a bioethical issue using secondary sources: journal articles, government reports, databases (e.g. the Atlas of Living Australia, ABS or AIHW health statistics), scientific organisations, reputable news.
  You might add a small amount of primary data, such as a survey of opinions — with consent and anonymity.
**Strengths and limitations**
  Secondary: large data sets, long time spans and expert methods you couldn't replicate; but you rely on others' methods, may not know their limitations, and data can be out of date or biased.
  Primary: collected for your exact question and you know how; but limited in size and scope.
**Going deeper**
  The same data can be primary for one person and secondary for another: a zoo geneticist's measurements are primary for them and secondary for you.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling a journal article "primary data" because it reports an experiment — for you it's secondary.
  Using secondary data without recording the source.
**Connections**
  Characteristics of primary data (Unit 1).
  Quality of evidence: secondary sources must be judged.
  Logbook for secondary data.
**Practice questions**
  Classify: (a) survival rates of cloned embryos from a published study; (b) a class survey you ran on attitudes to cloning.
    (a) Secondary. (b) Primary.
  Give one limitation of relying on secondary data.
    You depend on other researchers' methods and may not know their errors, assumptions or biases; the data may also be out of date.
**Flashcards**
  Define secondary data. :: Data collected by others that you gather and use.
  Is data from a journal article primary or secondary for you? :: Secondary.
  Give one advantage of secondary data. :: Large data sets and expert methods beyond what you could produce.
  Data you generate yourself is {{primary}} data.
  Name one reputable source of Australian secondary data. :: ABS, AIHW, CSIRO, the Atlas of Living Australia (or a peer-reviewed journal).

@ dd3ac8d6 U2.3.1.2 | Evidence, opinion and anecdote
**In a sentence**
  Evidence comes from systematic observation or experiment; an anecdote is one person's story; an opinion is a view — and scientific ideas can be tested, while non-scientific ideas (values, beliefs) can't, though both matter in an ethical debate.
**Key terms**
  **Evidence**: data from systematic observation or experiment that can support or refute a claim.
  **Anecdote**: a personal story or single case, not collected systematically.
  **Opinion**: a personal view or judgement, which may or may not be based on evidence.
  **Scientific idea**: an idea that can be tested and possibly shown wrong by evidence.
  **Non-scientific idea**: an idea that can't be tested by evidence (values, beliefs, aesthetics).
**Telling them apart**
  Evidence: "Of 277 fused eggs, one produced a live lamb (Dolly)."
  Anecdote: "My neighbour's cloned dog seems just like her old one."
  Opinion: "Cloning pets is a waste of money."
  Scientific idea: "Cloned animals have a shorter lifespan" — testable with data.
  Non-scientific idea: "Cloning is unnatural and therefore wrong" — a value judgement, not testable.
**Why it matters in bioethics**
  Science can tell you what is likely to happen (risks, success rates); it can't tell you what should happen. Ethical decisions weigh evidence together with values.
  A strong argument uses evidence for its factual claims and is open about which parts are values.
**Going deeper**
  Science can test whether a claim is true (do clones age faster?) but values decide whether an action is right; keeping the two apart makes an ethical argument clearer.
**Common mistakes**
  Treating a vivid anecdote as proof.
  Dismissing values as irrelevant — in ethics they're central, but they aren't evidence.
**Connections**
  Quality of evidence.
  Key science skills: distinguishing opinion, anecdote and evidence.
  Approaches to bioethics: where values come in.
**Practice questions**
  Classify: "Studies of 100 cloned cattle found most were healthy as adults."
    Evidence (systematically collected data).
  Is "Genetic testing will make society less accepting of disability" a scientific idea? Explain.
    Partly. It makes a prediction that could in principle be studied (attitudes could be surveyed over time), but as stated it is mainly an opinion; to be scientific it would need to be framed as a testable claim.
**Flashcards**
  Define anecdote. :: A personal story or single case, not collected systematically.
  Define evidence. :: Data from systematic observation or experiment that can support or refute a claim.
  "Cloning is unnatural" is a {{non-scientific}} idea (a value judgement).
  What makes an idea scientific? :: It can be tested and possibly shown wrong by evidence.
  Can science alone decide an ethical question? :: No — it provides evidence about outcomes; values decide what should be done.

@ 7cd87ab1 U2.3.1.3 | Judging the quality of evidence
**In a sentence**
  Good evidence comes from authoritative, current, unbiased sources using valid methods with large enough samples — so check who produced it, how, when and why.
**Key terms**
  **Validity**: whether the evidence actually addresses the question it's used for.
  **Authority**: the expertise and credibility of the source.
  **Bias**: a lean in how information is gathered or presented that distorts it.
  **Peer review**: evaluation of research by independent experts before publication.
  **Conflict of interest**: when a source stands to gain from a particular conclusion.
**Questions to ask of a source**
  Who wrote it, and what is their expertise? (A geneticist at a university vs an anonymous blog.)
  Was it peer reviewed?
  When was it published — is it still current?
  What was the method and sample size? Were there controls?
  Who funded it, and could that influence the conclusion?
  Does it agree with other good sources? Is it reproducible?
  Is it presenting facts, or trying to persuade?
**Types of bias**
  Funding or conflict of interest (a company selling a product studying its own product).
  Selection bias (only surveying people who already agree).
  Publication bias (successful results more likely to be published).
  Selective reporting (quoting only the data that support one side).
**Going deeper**
  Systematic reviews and meta-analyses combine many studies and are usually the strongest evidence; a single study, however well reported, is weaker.
**Common mistakes**
  Trusting a source because it looks professional.
  Rejecting all industry-funded research automatically — judge the method, but note the conflict.
**Connections**
  Evidence, opinion and anecdote.
  Organising and evaluating secondary data.
  Key science skills: evaluating scientific and media texts.
**Practice questions**
  A website selling pet cloning claims clones "are exact copies of your pet". Evaluate this source.
    It has a conflict of interest (it profits from sales), is not peer reviewed, and the claim is inaccurate: clones share nuclear DNA but differ in environment, epigenetics and even features such as coat pattern. Low reliability.
  Why is peer review a sign of higher-quality evidence?
    Independent experts have checked the methods, analysis and conclusions before publication, making serious errors less likely.
**Flashcards**
  What is peer review? :: Evaluation of research by independent experts before it is published.
  Name two questions to judge a source's authority. :: Who wrote it and what is their expertise? Is it peer reviewed or from a reputable organisation?
  A company testing its own product has a {{conflict of interest}}.
  Why does sample size matter to quality? :: Small samples are more affected by chance, so results may not be representative.
  What is publication bias? :: Studies with positive results are more likely to be published than those with negative results.

@ f4d2f552 U2.3.1.4 | Organising and evaluating secondary data
**In a sentence**
  Pull the data from your sources into tables and graphs you can compare, look for patterns and disagreements, and judge how much weight each source deserves.
**Key terms**
  **Synthesis**: combining information from several sources into one coherent picture.
  **Trend**: a general pattern across the data.
  **Contradictory data**: data from different sources that disagree.
  **Provisional data**: data or conclusions that may change as more evidence comes in.
**Organising**
  Summary table of sources: author, date, type, key finding, sample size, strengths and limitations.
  Convert data to comparable units (e.g. all success rates as percentages).
  Graph key figures (e.g. cloning success rates across species or over time).
**Analysing and evaluating**
  Look for agreement across independent sources — it strengthens a conclusion.
  Where sources disagree, compare their methods, sample sizes, dates and funding to explain why.
  Note where the evidence is limited or provisional (new technologies often have few studies).
  Separate the data from the authors' interpretations.
**Going deeper**
  When sources disagree, check they measured the same thing — live births per embryo transferred and per egg used give very different cloning success rates.
**Worked example**
  Three sources report live-birth rates from cloned embryos of 1%, 3% and 15%. How should you deal with this?
    Check what each measured (per embryo transferred or per egg fused), the species and the year.
    Newer studies and some species (e.g. cattle) have higher success than early work.
    Report the range, explain the differences, and avoid quoting one figure as "the" success rate.
**Common mistakes**
  Copying data without checking it measures the same thing.
  Ignoring sources that disagree with your position.
**Connections**
  Judging the quality of evidence.
  Logbook for secondary data.
  Analysing data (Unit 1): similar tools for primary data.
**Practice questions**
  Why is it useful to tabulate sources with their sample sizes and dates?
    It lets you compare sources directly and weigh them — larger, more recent, better-designed studies usually deserve more weight.
  Two reputable studies disagree about the health of cloned cattle. Suggest two reasons.
    Different sample sizes or selection of animals, different ages at assessment, different cloning methods or years (techniques improved), or different definitions of "healthy".
**Flashcards**
  What is synthesis? :: Combining information from several sources into one coherent picture.
  What should you do when sources disagree? :: Compare their methods, sample sizes, dates and funding to explain the difference.
  Agreement across independent sources {{strengthens}} a conclusion.
  What does "provisional" mean for data? :: It may change as more evidence becomes available.
  Why convert data to the same units? :: So values from different sources can be compared fairly.

@ 27a090ce U2.3.2.2 | Effective science communication
**In a sentence**
  Good science communication is accurate, clear, set in context, concise and coherent, and pitched to its purpose and audience.
**Key terms**

**Common mistakes**

@ ee7df0a2 U2.3.2.3 | Data representations, models and limits
**In a sentence**
  Graphs, diagrams and models make complex biology understandable — but every one simplifies, so say what it leaves out.
**Key terms**
  **Data representation**: a graph, table, chart or infographic showing data.
  **Model**: a simplified representation of a system or process.
  **Limitation of a model**: what the model leaves out or gets wrong compared with reality.
**Using them**
  Choose the representation that fits: a line graph for change over time (population of a species), a bar chart to compare categories (success rates by species), a flow chart for a process (SCNT), a food web for interactions.
  Models used in Unit 2: Punnett squares, pedigree charts, food webs, the stimulus–response model, population graphs.
**Their limits**
  A Punnett square gives probabilities, not what will happen in one family.
  A food web leaves out many species and doesn't show the strength of each interaction.
  A graph can mislead: a y-axis that doesn't start at zero exaggerates differences; cherry-picked time ranges hide trends.
**Going deeper**
  Every graph involves choices — the scale, the range, which data to include. Asking what was left out is a good test of a source.
**Common mistakes**
  Presenting a model as if it were reality.
  Using a graph type that hides the pattern.
**Connections**
  Aims, hypotheses, models, theories and laws (Unit 1).
  Key science skills: models and their limitations.
**Practice questions**
  Give one limitation of using a food web to predict the effect of removing dingoes.
    It shows who eats whom but not how strongly each species depends on another, and it leaves out non-feeding effects (e.g. prey changing where they feed) and other factors such as rainfall.
  How can a graph mislead a reader?
    By starting the y-axis above zero to exaggerate differences, choosing a selective time range, or using inconsistent scales.
**Flashcards**
  Why does every model have limitations? :: It simplifies reality, leaving some features out.
  Limitation of a Punnett square? :: It predicts probabilities, not the actual outcome for a small number of offspring.
  A {{flow chart}} suits showing the steps of a process.
  How can a truncated y-axis mislead? :: It makes small differences look large.
  What graph suits change in a population over time? :: A line graph.

@ 00187253 U2.3.3.3 | Approaches to bioethics and ethical concepts
**In a sentence**
  Ethical approaches ask whether an action is right by its consequences, by duties and rules, or by what a virtuous person would do — and ethical concepts (integrity, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, respect) give the principles to weigh.
**Key terms**
  **Consequences-based approach**: judges an action by its outcomes — aims to maximise benefits and minimise harms (the ends).
  **Duty- and/or rule-based approach**: some actions are right or wrong in themselves, whatever the outcome; people must follow certain duties or rules (the means).
  **Virtues-based approach**: focuses on the character of the person acting — what would a good, honest, compassionate person do?
  **Integrity**: honest searching for knowledge and honest reporting of all results, favourable or not.
  **Justice**: fair consideration of competing claims, no unfair burden on any group, and fair access to benefits.
  **Beneficence**: maximising benefits and minimising risks and harms.
  **Non-maleficence**: avoiding harm — or ensuring any harm is not out of proportion to the benefits.
  **Respect**: recognising the intrinsic and instrumental value of living things; respecting welfare, autonomy, beliefs and culture; protecting those with reduced capacity to decide for themselves.
**Applying the approaches — cloning an endangered species**
  Consequences-based: benefits (restoring lost alleles, public interest in conservation) versus harms (many failed pregnancies and animal suffering, money diverted from protecting habitat). Supports cloning if benefits clearly outweigh harms.
  Duty/rule-based: a duty to prevent extinctions caused by humans may support it; a rule against causing unnecessary animal suffering, or against "interfering with nature", may oppose it — regardless of outcomes.
  Virtues-based: would a responsible, compassionate conservationist do this? Is it motivated by care for the species or by publicity?
**Applying the concepts**
  Integrity: report low success rates honestly, not just the successes.
  Justice: is money spent fairly across species? Do Traditional Owners share in decisions about species on their Country?
  Beneficence: maximise conservation benefit; choose methods with the best chance of success.
  Non-maleficence: minimise harm to surrogate and cloned animals.
  Respect: value the species itself and the welfare of individual animals; respect cultural values.
**Going deeper**
  The approaches often point the same way. When they conflict, say which you give more weight to and why — that reasoning is what earns marks.
**Common mistakes**
  Naming an approach without applying it to the issue.
  Confusing beneficence (doing good) with non-maleficence (avoiding harm).
  Assuming each approach leads to only one answer — the same approach can support different positions depending on how outcomes or duties are weighed.
**Exam tips**
  Name the approach or concept, define it briefly, then apply it with a specific example from the issue.

**Practice questions**
  Distinguish between a consequences-based and a duty/rule-based approach.
    A consequences-based approach judges an action by its outcomes, aiming for the greatest benefit and least harm. A duty/rule-based approach judges the action itself against duties or rules, regardless of the outcomes it produces.
  Apply the concept of justice to publicly funded genetic testing.
    Justice asks whether access is fair: if testing is expensive, only wealthier families may benefit, placing an unfair burden on others. Public funding could make access more equal, but costs must be weighed against other health needs.
  Explain the difference between beneficence and non-maleficence.
    Beneficence is actively maximising benefits and minimising risks; non-maleficence is avoiding causing harm, or ensuring any harm is proportionate to the benefits.
