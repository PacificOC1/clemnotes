@ a1b1e90f U2.1.1.1 | Genes, alleles and genomes
**In a sentence**
  A gene is a section of DNA coding for a product that influences a trait, an allele is one version of a gene, and the genome is all of an organism's DNA.
**Key terms**
  **Gene**: a section of DNA at a particular position on a chromosome that codes for a product (usually a protein) and influences a characteristic.
  **Allele**: an alternative form of a gene, differing slightly in its DNA sequence.
  **Genome**: the complete set of genetic material of an organism (or organelle), including non-coding DNA.
  **Locus** (plural loci): the position of a gene on a chromosome.
  **Trait**: a characteristic, such as blood group or flower colour.
**Genes and alleles**
  A gene is an instruction; alleles are versions of that instruction. The gene for the CFTR protein has a normal allele and a cystic fibrosis allele.
  Alleles usually differ by just one or a few bases, but this can change the protein — sometimes making it not work at all.
  In a diploid organism each body cell has two alleles for each autosomal gene (one from each parent). They can be the same (homozygous) or different (heterozygous).
  A population can have many alleles for one gene: the ABO blood group gene has three common alleles (Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i) — this is called **multiple alleles**.
**The genome**
  The human genome is about 3 billion base pairs per set of chromosomes, with roughly 20 000 protein-coding genes.
  Only a small fraction (a few per cent) of human DNA codes for proteins; the rest includes regulatory sequences and other non-coding DNA.
  Mitochondrial DNA is also part of the genome.
  Genome size and gene number don't track how complex an organism looks — some plants and amphibians have far bigger genomes than humans.
**The hierarchy**
  Genome → chromosomes → genes → alleles → the base sequence.
**Going deeper**
  The Human Genome Project finished sequencing the human genome in 2003. Comparing genomes now helps diagnose genetic disease, choose medicines (pharmacogenomics) and trace human migration.
  Any two people's genomes are about 99.9% identical; most of our differences come from small variations, such as single-base differences.
**Common mistakes**
  Using gene and allele as if they mean the same thing: "the blue-eye gene" should be "the allele for blue eyes of the eye colour gene".
  Saying the genome is only the genes. It includes all DNA, coding and non-coding.
  Saying a person has two genes for a trait. They have two alleles of one gene.
**Exam tips**
  In definitions, include position (locus) for a gene and "alternative form" for an allele.
**Connections**
  Homologous chromosomes: alleles sit at matching loci on each pair.
  Writing genotypes: symbols stand for alleles.
  Genetic diversity (Area of Study 2): diversity is the variety of alleles in a population.
**Practice questions**
  Distinguish between a gene and an allele.
    A gene is a section of DNA at a particular locus coding for a product that influences a trait. An allele is one of the alternative forms of that gene, differing slightly in sequence.
  Explain why a person can carry at most two alleles for the ABO gene, even though three exist in the population.
    A person has two copies of chromosome 9 (one from each parent), and each copy carries one allele at the ABO locus — so at most two different alleles.
  Define genome and state approximately how many base pairs are in one set of human chromosomes.
    All the genetic material of an organism, coding and non-coding; about 3 billion base pairs.
**Flashcards**
  Define gene. :: A section of DNA at a particular locus that codes for a product and influences a trait.
  Define allele. :: An alternative form of a gene.
  Define genome. :: All the genetic material of an organism, including non-coding DNA.
  The position of a gene on a chromosome is its {{locus}}.
  About how many protein-coding genes are in the human genome? :: About 20 000.
  What are multiple alleles? :: More than two alleles for one gene in a population, e.g. Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i for ABO blood groups.
  Does most human DNA code for proteins? :: No — only a small fraction (a few per cent) does.

@ b3f305fd U2.1.1.2 | Homologous and sex chromosomes
**In a sentence**
  Diploid cells have chromosomes in homologous pairs — one from each parent, carrying the same genes at the same loci but possibly different alleles — and in humans 22 pairs are autosomes and one pair are the sex chromosomes, X and Y.
**Key terms**
  **Homologous chromosomes**: a matching pair of chromosomes, one maternal and one paternal, with the same genes at the same loci.
  **Autosome**: any chromosome that isn't a sex chromosome.
  **Sex chromosomes**: the chromosomes that determine sex (X and Y in mammals).
  **Diploid (2n)**: having two sets of chromosomes.
  **Haploid (n)**: having one set of chromosomes.
  **Sister chromatids**: the two identical copies of one chromosome after DNA replication.
**Homologous pairs**
  One chromosome of each pair came from the mother's egg, the other from the father's sperm.
  They match in size, centromere position and banding pattern, and carry the same genes in the same order.
  They are not identical: at any locus they may carry different alleles.
**Autosomes and sex chromosomes**
  Humans have 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes (numbered 1 to 22, roughly largest to smallest) and one pair of sex chromosomes.
  Females are XX; males are XY.
  The X is large and carries around 800–900 protein-coding genes; the Y is small with few genes, including SRY, which triggers male development.
  X and Y pair up only over small regions, so a male has just one copy of most X genes.
  Other systems exist: in birds the female is ZW and the male ZZ; in many reptiles incubation temperature sets sex.
**Counting**
  Body cells (somatic cells) are diploid: 2n = 46 in humans.
  Gametes (sperm, eggs) are haploid: n = 23.
  After S phase each chromosome has two sister chromatids — still one chromosome.
**Going deeper**
  Because males have only one X, a single recessive allele on the X is expressed in males — why X-linked conditions such as red–green colour blindness are more common in males.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling sister chromatids a homologous pair. Sister chromatids are identical copies; homologues come from different parents.
  Saying homologous chromosomes are identical. They carry the same genes, not necessarily the same alleles.
  Saying the X and Y are a fully homologous pair.
**Exam tips**
  When counting, say exactly what you are counting — chromosomes, chromatids or pairs: 46 chromosomes is 23 homologous pairs.
  Define homologous chromosomes with all three parts: one from each parent, the same genes, at the same loci.
**Connections**
  Genes, alleles and genomes.
  Karyotypes: pairs are arranged by homology.
  Meiosis: homologues pair up and separate.
  Pedigrees: sex-linked inheritance depends on X and Y.
**Practice questions**
  State three features shared by the two chromosomes of a homologous pair.
    Same size, same centromere position and banding pattern, and the same genes at the same loci.
  How many autosomes are in a human sperm cell, and which sex chromosome might it carry?
    22 autosomes, plus either an X or a Y chromosome.
  Explain why homologous chromosomes are not identical.
    One comes from each parent, so although they carry the same genes, the alleles at any locus may differ.
**Flashcards**
  What is a homologous pair? :: Two chromosomes, one from each parent, carrying the same genes at the same loci.
  How many pairs of autosomes do humans have? :: 22.
  A human male's sex chromosomes are {{XY}}; a female's are {{XX}}.
  Diploid number of a human body cell? :: 2n = 46.
  Sister chromatids vs homologous chromosomes? :: Sister chromatids are identical copies of one chromosome; homologues are a maternal and a paternal chromosome with the same genes.
  Which Y-chromosome gene triggers male development? :: SRY.
  How many chromosomes are in a human gamete? :: 23 (haploid, n).

@ 5f6161ea U2.1.1.3 | Chromosome number and size across species
**In a sentence**
  Every species has a characteristic number of chromosomes, but the number and size vary enormously between species and have nothing to do with how complex the organism is.
**Key terms**
  **Chromosome number**: the number of chromosomes in a species' body cells (the diploid number).
  **Polyploidy**: having more than two complete sets of chromosomes.
  **Hybrid**: offspring of two different species.
**Variation between species (diploid numbers)**
  Jack jumper ant (an Australian ant, Myrmecia pilosula): females 2 — males have a single chromosome.
  Fruit fly: 8. Garden pea: 14. Human: 46. Chimpanzee: 48. Potato: 48. Donkey: 62. Horse: 64. Dog: 78.
  Some ferns have more than 1000.
  Size varies too: human chromosome 1 is about five times longer than chromosome 21.
**Why it matters**
  Chromosome number is fixed within a species, so all individuals can pair their chromosomes in meiosis.
  Different chromosome numbers usually stop hybrids being fertile. A horse (64) × donkey (62) gives a mule (63): its chromosomes can't all pair up in meiosis, so it makes almost no viable gametes.
  More chromosomes doesn't mean more genes or more complexity — dogs have more chromosomes than humans; potatoes as many as chimps.
**Going deeper**
  Polyploidy is common in plants: bread wheat has six sets (hexaploid), cultivated strawberries eight. Extra sets often make larger cells, fruit and flowers, which is why many crops are polyploid.
  Humans and chimpanzees differ by one pair because two ancestral chromosomes fused to form human chromosome 2.
**Common mistakes**
  Assuming more chromosomes means a more complex organism.
  Forgetting to say whether a number is diploid or haploid.
**Exam tips**
  Always say whether a number is diploid (2n) or haploid (n).
  To explain hybrid infertility, link unmatched chromosomes to failed pairing in meiosis — not just "different numbers".
**Connections**
  Homologous chromosomes: pairs must match to separate properly.
  Karyotypes: how chromosome number and size are seen.
  Meiosis: why mismatched chromosomes make hybrids infertile.
**Practice questions**
  A mule has 63 chromosomes. Explain why mules are almost always infertile.
    A mule gets 32 chromosomes from its horse parent and 31 from its donkey parent. These don't form matching homologous pairs, so meiosis fails and it can't make viable gametes.
  Use examples to show that chromosome number doesn't indicate complexity.
    Dogs (78) and potatoes (48) have more chromosomes than humans (46), and some ferns have over 1000.
  A species has a haploid number of 12. How many chromosomes are in its body cells and in its gametes?
    Body cells 24 (2n); gametes 12 (n).
**Flashcards**
  Diploid chromosome number of a horse and a donkey? :: Horse 64, donkey 62.
  Why is a mule infertile? :: Its 63 chromosomes can't form matching pairs, so meiosis fails.
  Does a higher chromosome number mean a more complex organism? :: No — e.g. dogs have 78, humans 46.
  Having more than two complete sets of chromosomes is called {{polyploidy}}.
  Which Australian ant has males with a single chromosome? :: The jack jumper ant (Myrmecia pilosula).
  Diploid number of a human and a chimpanzee? :: Human 46, chimpanzee 48.
  What is a hybrid? :: The offspring of two different species.

@ 0b6ec480 U2.1.1.4 | Karyotypes and chromosome abnormalities
**In a sentence**
  A karyotype is a picture of all an organism's chromosomes, arranged in homologous pairs, used to check the sex and spot abnormalities in chromosome number or large changes in structure.
**Key terms**
  **Karyotype**: an image of an individual's chromosomes arranged in pairs by size, centromere position and banding pattern.
  **Aneuploidy**: having an extra or missing chromosome (e.g. 47 or 45 instead of 46).
  **Trisomy**: three copies of a chromosome instead of two.
  **Monosomy**: one copy of a chromosome instead of two.
  **Non-disjunction**: failure of chromosomes or chromatids to separate properly in meiosis.
**Making a karyotype**
  Cells are collected (white blood cells, or before birth by amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling), grown and stopped in **metaphase**, when chromosomes are most condensed and visible.
  Chromosomes are stained, photographed, and arranged in pairs by size, centromere position and banding pattern: pairs 1–22 roughly largest to smallest, then the sex chromosomes.
  Written as number,sex chromosomes: normal female 46,XX; normal male 46,XY.
**What a karyotype can and can't show**
  Can show: sex; aneuploidy (extra or missing chromosomes); large structural changes such as deletions, duplications and translocations.
  Can't show: single-gene mutations or which alleles a person carries (such as the cystic fibrosis allele).
**How aneuploidy happens — non-disjunction**
  In meiosis I a homologous pair fails to separate, or in meiosis II sister chromatids fail to separate.
  Some gametes get an extra chromosome (n + 1), others one fewer (n − 1).
  Fertilisation by a normal gamete gives a trisomy (2n + 1 = 47) or a monosomy (2n − 1 = 45).
**Examples**
  **Down syndrome**: trisomy 21 — 47,XX,+21 or 47,XY,+21. The risk rises with the mother's age.
  **Edwards syndrome** (trisomy 18) and **Patau syndrome** (trisomy 13): severe, usually fatal in infancy.
  **Turner syndrome**: 45,X — female, short stature, usually infertile.
  **Klinefelter syndrome**: 47,XXY — male, often tall with reduced fertility.
  Most other aneuploidies are lethal before birth.
**Worked example**
  A karyotype shows 22 pairs of autosomes, three copies of chromosome 21 and one X and one Y. Describe it.
    Total chromosomes: 44 autosomes + 1 extra 21 + 2 sex chromosomes = 47.
    Sex: male (XY).
    Abnormality: trisomy 21 (Down syndrome). Written 47,XY,+21.
**Going deeper**
  A few per cent (about 3–4%) of Down syndrome cases come from a translocation: part of chromosome 21 is attached to another chromosome, so the count can be 46 but there's still extra chromosome 21 material.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying a karyotype shows genes or alleles. It shows whole chromosomes only.
  Forgetting why metaphase is used (chromosomes are most condensed).
  Writing the notation without the total number first.
**Exam tips**
  Write the notation in order: total number, sex chromosomes, then the change (47,XY,+21).
  When explaining non-disjunction, name the stage (anaphase I or II) and the gametes it makes (n + 1 and n − 1).
**Connections**
  Homologous chromosomes: pairing is how karyotypes are arranged.
  Meiosis: where non-disjunction happens.
  Bioethical issues: prenatal testing is a common Unit 2 investigation topic.
**Practice questions**
  Why are cells stopped at metaphase to make a karyotype?
    In metaphase the chromosomes are fully condensed, so they are thickest, most visible and easiest to identify and pair.
  Explain how non-disjunction can result in a child with Turner syndrome (45,X).
    During meiosis in one parent the sex chromosomes fail to separate, so one gamete has no sex chromosome. If it fuses with a normal gamete carrying an X, the zygote has 45 chromosomes with a single X.
  Would a karyotype detect cystic fibrosis? Explain.
    No. Cystic fibrosis is caused by an allele of a single gene; the chromosomes look normal in a karyotype.
**Flashcards**
  What is a karyotype? :: An image of an individual's chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs by size, centromere position and banding.
  At which stage are chromosomes photographed for a karyotype? :: Metaphase.
  Down syndrome is trisomy {{21}}.
  Karyotype notation for Turner syndrome? :: 45,X.
  What is non-disjunction? :: Failure of homologues or sister chromatids to separate in meiosis, giving gametes with an extra or missing chromosome.
  Name something a karyotype cannot detect. :: A single-gene mutation or allele (e.g. cystic fibrosis).
  Karyotype notation for Klinefelter syndrome? :: 47,XXY.

@ 958f3320 U2.1.1.5 | Meiosis and genetic diversity
**In a sentence**
  Meiosis turns one diploid cell into four haploid, genetically different gametes through two divisions, and crossing over and independent assortment during meiosis I shuffle alleles into new combinations.
**Key terms**
  **Meiosis**: cell division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid cells.
  **Gamete**: a haploid sex cell (sperm or egg; pollen and ovules in plants).
  **Bivalent**: a pair of homologous chromosomes lying side by side in prophase I.
  **Crossing over**: exchange of DNA between non-sister chromatids of a homologous pair in prophase I.
  **Chiasma** (plural chiasmata): the point where chromatids cross over.
  **Independent assortment**: the random orientation of each homologous pair at the equator in metaphase I.
  **Recombinant chromatid**: a chromatid carrying a new combination of alleles after crossing over.
**Overview**
  Happens in the testes and ovaries (anthers and ovules in plants).
  DNA replicates **once** (in S phase), then the cell divides **twice**.
  Result: four haploid cells, each genetically different from each other and from the parent cell.
**Meiosis I — homologous chromosomes separate**
  **Prophase I**: chromosomes condense; homologous chromosomes pair up to form bivalents; **crossing over** happens at chiasmata; the nuclear membrane breaks down.
  **Metaphase I**: bivalents line up in **pairs** across the equator, each pair oriented randomly (independent assortment).
  **Anaphase I**: homologous chromosomes are pulled to opposite poles; sister chromatids stay together.
  **Telophase I and cytokinesis**: two haploid cells form, each chromosome still made of two chromatids.
**Meiosis II — sister chromatids separate**
  Like mitosis, but in haploid cells.
  **Prophase II**: chromosomes condense again; spindles form.
  **Metaphase II**: chromosomes line up single-file across the equator.
  **Anaphase II**: sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles.
  **Telophase II and cytokinesis**: four haploid cells.
**How meiosis creates diversity**
  **Crossing over** (prophase I): swaps sections between maternal and paternal chromatids, creating new combinations of alleles on the same chromosome.
  **Independent assortment** (metaphase I): each pair lines up independently, so each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes. With n pairs there are 2ⁿ combinations — for humans 2²³, about 8.4 million, before crossing over is counted.
  **Random fertilisation**: any sperm can fertilise any egg, so one couple could produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million ≈ 70 trillion combinations.
  This variation is the raw material natural selection acts on.
**Mitosis and meiosis compared**
  Purpose: mitosis for growth and repair; meiosis for making gametes.
  Divisions: one vs two.
  Cells produced: two diploid vs four haploid.
  Genetically: identical vs all different.
  Homologues pair and cross over: no vs yes (prophase I).
**Worked example**
  An organism has a diploid number of 8. How many chromosome combinations can its gametes have from independent assortment alone?
    n = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 pairs.
    Combinations = 2⁴ = 16.
**Going deeper**
  In males all four cells become sperm. In females meiosis is unequal: one large egg and small polar bodies that break down — the egg keeps most of the cytoplasm for the early embryo.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying crossing over happens between sister chromatids. It happens between non-sister chromatids of a homologous pair.
  Placing crossing over in meiosis II, or independent assortment in anaphase. Crossing over is prophase I; independent assortment is metaphase I.
  "Meiosis produces two cells." It produces four.
  Forgetting that DNA is replicated only once, before meiosis I.
**Exam tips**
  To explain diversity, name the process, the stage and the effect: "crossing over in prophase I exchanges alleles between non-sister chromatids, producing new combinations of alleles".
  In diagrams, meiosis I lines up pairs (two chromosomes side by side); meiosis II lines up single chromosomes.
**Connections**
  The cell cycle and mitosis (Unit 1): the division meiosis is compared with.
  Karyotypes: non-disjunction in meiosis causes aneuploidy.
  Linked genes: crossing over creates recombinants.
  Sexual reproduction (Area of Study 2): the genetic advantage comes from meiosis.
**Practice questions**
  Describe the events of prophase I and explain their significance.
    Chromosomes condense and homologous chromosomes pair into bivalents. Non-sister chromatids exchange segments at chiasmata (crossing over), producing recombinant chromatids with new allele combinations — increasing genetic diversity of gametes.
  Explain how independent assortment increases genetic diversity.
    In metaphase I each homologous pair lines up independently of the others, so whether the maternal or paternal chromosome goes to a particular pole is random. Each gamete therefore gets a different mixture of chromosomes (2ⁿ possibilities).
  Give three differences between mitosis and meiosis.
    Mitosis has one division, meiosis two; mitosis makes two diploid cells, meiosis four haploid cells; mitosis cells are identical, meiosis cells are genetically different (homologues pair and cross over only in meiosis).
  A cell with 2n = 6 undergoes meiosis. How many chromosomes are in each cell at the end of meiosis I and at the end of meiosis II?
    End of meiosis I: 3 chromosomes (each still two chromatids). End of meiosis II: 3 chromosomes (each a single chromatid).
**Flashcards**
  What does meiosis produce? :: Four haploid cells, each genetically different.
  In which stage does crossing over happen? :: Prophase I.
  Independent assortment happens in {{metaphase I}}.
  What separates in anaphase I, and what in anaphase II? :: Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes. Anaphase II: sister chromatids.
  How many chromosome combinations from independent assortment in humans? :: 2²³, about 8.4 million.
  Crossing over is between {{non-sister}} chromatids of a homologous pair.
  Three sources of genetic variation in sexual reproduction? :: Crossing over, independent assortment, random fertilisation.

@ 9d38cfff U2.1.2.1 | Writing genotypes
**In a sentence**
  A genotype is written with one letter per gene — capital for a dominant allele, lower case for a recessive one — with superscripts for codominant alleles and for alleles on the X chromosome.
**Key terms**
  **Genotype**: the alleles an individual has for a gene.
  **Phenotype**: the observable characteristic that results (structure, biochemistry, physiology or behaviour).
  **Homozygous**: two identical alleles (TT or tt).
  **Heterozygous**: two different alleles (Tt).
  **Hemizygous**: having only one allele of a gene — males for genes on the X chromosome.
**The rules**
  Choose a letter for the gene, often the first letter of the dominant trait: T = tall, t = short.
  Pick letters whose capital and lower case look different: T/t, B/b, not S/s, O/o or W/w when handwriting.
  Always write a key first: "Let T = allele for tall, t = allele for short".
  Write the dominant allele first in a heterozygote: Tt, not tT.
**Codominance and incomplete dominance**
  Use a base letter with superscripts, because neither allele is recessive: flower colour Cᴿ (red) and Cᵂ (white) → Cᴿ Cᴿ, Cᴿ Cᵂ, Cᵂ Cᵂ.
  ABO blood groups: Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant, i is recessive. Genotypes: Iᴬ Iᴬ or Iᴬ i (group A), Iᴮ Iᴮ or Iᴮ i (group B), Iᴬ Iᴮ (group AB), i i (group O).
**Sex-linked (X-linked) genes**
  Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting) and Xʰ (haemophilia).
  Females: Xᴴ Xᴴ, Xᴴ Xʰ (a carrier), Xʰ Xʰ.
  Males: Xᴴ Y or Xʰ Y — the Y carries no allele for this gene, so a male is hemizygous.
**Going deeper**
  Multiple alleles can form a dominance series. In rabbits, full colour is dominant to chinchilla, which is dominant to Himalayan, which is dominant to albino — four alleles of one gene give four coat types.
**Common mistakes**
  Using different letters for alleles of one gene (T for tall and S for short).
  Writing a sex-linked genotype without the X and Y.
  Writing gametes with two alleles of one gene — a gamete carries only one.
**Exam tips**
  Write a key before every cross ("Let T = tall, t = short"); undefined symbols lose marks.
  For X-linked traits, write the Y with no allele and never give a male two alleles for that gene.
**Connections**
  Dominance, codominance and incomplete dominance.
  Monohybrid crosses and pedigrees: genotypes are the starting point.
**Practice questions**
  In pea plants purple flowers (P) are dominant to white (p). Write the genotypes of a homozygous purple, a heterozygous and a white plant.
    PP, Pp, pp.
  Write all possible genotypes of a person with blood group A.
    Iᴬ Iᴬ or Iᴬ i.
  Red–green colour blindness is X-linked recessive. Using B and b, write the genotype of a colour-blind man and a carrier woman.
    Man: Xᵇ Y. Carrier woman: Xᴮ Xᵇ.
**Flashcards**
  Define genotype. :: The combination of alleles an individual has for a gene.
  Define phenotype. :: The observable characteristic resulting from the genotype and environment.
  Genotype Tt is {{heterozygous}}; tt is {{homozygous}} recessive.
  Genotype of a man with haemophilia (H = normal, h = haemophilia)? :: Xʰ Y.
  Genotypes for blood group O and AB? :: O: i i. AB: Iᴬ Iᴮ.
  Why are males called hemizygous for X-linked genes? :: They have only one X chromosome, so only one allele for those genes.
  Why use letters like T/t rather than S/s for alleles? :: The capital and lower-case forms must look clearly different.

@ 7f06ea76 U2.1.2.2 | Dominance, codominance, incomplete dominance
**In a sentence**
  With complete dominance one allele masks the other in a heterozygote; with codominance both alleles show fully and separately; with incomplete dominance the heterozygote is an intermediate blend.
**Key terms**
  **Dominant allele**: an allele expressed in the phenotype whenever it's present.
  **Recessive allele**: an allele expressed only when there's no dominant allele (homozygous recessive).
  **Codominance**: both alleles in a heterozygote are fully and separately expressed.
  **Incomplete dominance**: the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype between the two homozygotes.
**Complete dominance**
  Tall (T) is dominant to short (t) in peas: TT and Tt are both tall; only tt is short.
  Why: the recessive allele often codes for a protein that doesn't work. One working allele makes enough protein, so the heterozygote looks the same as the dominant homozygote.
  Dominant doesn't mean common. The allele for Huntington's disease is dominant but rare; extra fingers (polydactyly) is dominant too.
**Codominance — both show**
  ABO blood groups: someone with Iᴬ Iᴮ has both A and B antigens on their red blood cells — blood group AB.
  Roan cattle: a red × white cross gives roan calves with a mix of red hairs and white hairs (not pink hairs).
**Incomplete dominance — a blend**
  Snapdragons: red (Cᴿ Cᴿ) × white (Cᵂ Cᵂ) gives pink (Cᴿ Cᵂ). Each allele makes some pigment; one copy of the red allele makes only enough for pink.
  Crossing two pink plants gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white — here the phenotype ratio matches the genotype ratio, because every genotype looks different.
**Telling them apart**
  Heterozygote shows both parental traits separately (red hairs and white hairs; A and B antigens) → codominance.
  Heterozygote is in between (pink) → incomplete dominance.
  Heterozygote looks like one parent → complete dominance.
**Worked example**
  In four-o'clock flowers, red and white show incomplete dominance. Predict the offspring of a pink × white cross.
    Pink Cᴿ Cᵂ × white Cᵂ Cᵂ.
    Gametes: Cᴿ or Cᵂ from pink; Cᵂ from white.
    Offspring: Cᴿ Cᵂ (pink) and Cᵂ Cᵂ (white).
    Ratio 1 pink : 1 white.
**Going deeper**
  Sickle-cell is a useful case: at the level of the whole person the allele is recessive (only homozygotes have the disease), but at the level of the protein both normal and sickle haemoglobin are made — codominance.
**Common mistakes**
  Swapping codominance (both shown separately) and incomplete dominance (blended).
  Saying the dominant allele is "stronger" or "more common".
  Using capital and lower-case letters for codominant alleles, which implies one is recessive.
**Exam tips**
  Name the pattern and describe the heterozygote's phenotype — that is what separates dominance, codominance and incomplete dominance.
**Connections**
  Writing genotypes: superscript notation for codominance.
  Monohybrid crosses: 3 : 1 versus 1 : 2 : 1 ratios.
  Genes, environment and epigenetics: other influences on phenotype.
**Practice questions**
  Distinguish between codominance and incomplete dominance, with an example of each.
    In codominance both alleles are fully expressed side by side in the heterozygote (AB blood group, roan cattle). In incomplete dominance the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype (pink snapdragons from red × white).
  Explain why a heterozygous tall pea plant is as tall as a homozygous tall plant.
    The tall allele is dominant: one copy produces enough of the functional product for full height, so the recessive allele has no visible effect.
  Two roan cattle are crossed. Give the expected phenotype ratio of the calves.
    1 red : 2 roan : 1 white.
**Flashcards**
  What is a dominant allele? :: One that is expressed in the phenotype whenever present.
  Blood group AB is an example of {{codominance}}.
  Pink snapdragons from red × white parents show {{incomplete dominance}}.
  Phenotype ratio from crossing two heterozygotes with incomplete dominance? :: 1 : 2 : 1.
  Why can one functional allele be enough for the dominant phenotype? :: It makes enough working protein, so the non-functional recessive allele is masked.
  Is a dominant allele always the most common? :: No — e.g. the Huntington's allele is dominant but rare.
  Roan cattle (red hairs and white hairs) show which pattern? :: Codominance.

@ 5f07321f U2.1.2.3 | Genes, environment and epigenetics
**In a sentence**
  A phenotype is the product of genes and the environment working together — some traits are set almost entirely by one gene, others by many genes plus the environment, and epigenetic marks change which genes are switched on without changing the DNA.
**Key terms**
  **Monogenic trait**: controlled by one gene (e.g. ABO blood group, cystic fibrosis).
  **Polygenic trait**: controlled by many genes (e.g. height, skin colour).
  **Discontinuous variation**: distinct categories with no in-between (blood groups).
  **Continuous variation**: a range of values, usually a bell curve (height).
  **Epigenetics**: changes in gene expression that don't change the DNA sequence, and can sometimes be inherited.
  **DNA methylation**: adding methyl groups to DNA, which usually switches a gene off.
  **Histone modification**: chemical changes to the proteins DNA wraps around, loosening or tightening the DNA and so switching genes on or off.
**Genes alone, or genes plus environment**
  Phenotype = genotype + environment (+ epigenetic effects).
  Monogenic traits usually show discontinuous variation and are little affected by environment: your ABO group doesn't change with diet.
  Polygenic traits show continuous variation, and the environment often has a big influence: height depends on many genes and on childhood nutrition.
**Environment examples**
  Hydrangea flowers are blue in acid soil and pink in alkaline soil (aluminium availability) — same genotype, different colour.
  Himalayan rabbits and Siamese cats have a temperature-sensitive pigment enzyme: it works only in cooler body parts, so ears, nose, paws and tail are dark.
  Skin darkens with UV exposure (tanning).
  In many reptiles, including saltwater crocodiles and sea turtles, the temperature of the nest decides the sex of the hatchlings.
  Plants of one clone grown in sun and shade differ in height and leaf size.
**Epigenetics**
  DNA methylation: methyl groups on a gene's control region stop it being read — the gene is silenced.
  Histone modification: acetylation loosens the DNA so genes can be read (switched on); removing it packs DNA tightly (switched off).
  Triggers include diet, stress, toxins, smoking and ageing.
  Honeybees: queens and workers have the same genome; larvae fed royal jelly have different methylation patterns and become queens.
  Agouti mice: genetically identical mice differ in coat colour and body weight depending on their mother's diet during pregnancy, through methylation of the agouti gene.
  Identical twins start with near-identical epigenetic marks, but their marks drift apart with age and lifestyle — one reason twins can differ in disease.
  Early environment can leave lasting marks: people conceived during the Dutch Hunger Winter famine (1944–45) still had different methylation of a growth gene (IGF2) about 60 years later, and different rates of some diseases.
**Weighing the influences**
  Twin studies compare identical twins (same genotype) with non-identical twins (share about half their alleles). If identical twins are much more alike for a trait, genes have a large influence; if both types of twins are equally alike, environment matters more.
**Going deeper**
  "Heritability" measures how much of the variation in a trait in a population is due to genetic differences. It says nothing about a single person, and it changes if the environment changes.
**Common mistakes**
  Calling epigenetic changes mutations. The base sequence doesn't change.
  Saying characteristics gained from the environment (a tan, big muscles) are passed to offspring. Only some epigenetic marks can be inherited.
  Confusing polygenic (many genes) with multiple alleles (many versions of one gene).
**Exam tips**
  When given data (e.g. twin concordance), state which influence is larger and support it with numbers from the data.
**Connections**
  Genes, alleles and genomes.
  Dominance: single-gene traits.
  Stem cells and differentiation (Unit 1): switching genes on and off is epigenetic control.
  Adaptations (Area of Study 2): phenotypes that improve survival.
**Practice questions**
  Explain why height shows continuous variation but ABO blood group shows discontinuous variation.
    Height is polygenic and strongly affected by environment (nutrition), so many small effects add up to a smooth range. ABO is controlled by one gene with a few alleles and isn't affected by environment, so people fall into four distinct groups.
  Identical twins raised apart differ in body mass by 12 kg. What does this suggest?
    Because they have the same genotype, the difference must come from environmental (and epigenetic) factors such as diet and activity.
  Describe how DNA methylation can change a phenotype without changing the DNA sequence.
    Methyl groups added to a gene's control region prevent it being transcribed, so the protein isn't made. The phenotype changes, but the base sequence is the same.
  Identical twins are 90% concordant for a trait; non-identical twins 30%. What does this suggest?
    Genes have a strong influence: the pairs who share all their alleles are far more alike than those sharing about half. It isn't 100%, so environment has some effect too.
**Flashcards**
  What is a polygenic trait? :: A trait controlled by many genes, e.g. height.
  Continuous variation vs discontinuous variation? :: Continuous: a range of values (height). Discontinuous: distinct categories (blood group).
  Define epigenetics. :: Changes in gene expression that don't alter the DNA sequence.
  DNA {{methylation}} usually switches a gene off.
  Why are Siamese cats darker on their ears and paws? :: Their pigment enzyme only works at the lower temperatures of these body parts.
  How do honeybee queens and workers differ genetically? :: They don't — diet (royal jelly) changes gene expression epigenetically.
  What decides the sex of a saltwater crocodile? :: The incubation temperature of the nest.

@ 80b598d0 U2.1.3.1 | Pedigrees and patterns of inheritance
**In a sentence**
  A pedigree chart traces a trait through a family, and by testing each mode of inheritance against specific individuals you can work out whether the trait is dominant or recessive and autosomal or X-linked.
**Key terms**
  **Pedigree**: a chart of a family showing who has a trait over several generations.
  **Autosomal inheritance**: the gene is on an autosome, so males and females are affected equally.
  **X-linked (sex-linked) inheritance**: the gene is on the X chromosome.
  **Carrier**: a heterozygous person who has a recessive allele but not the recessive phenotype.
  **Proband**: the person through whom a family's pedigree was first identified.
**Reading the symbols**
  Square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected; unshaded = unaffected; half-shaded (or a dot) = known carrier, if shown.
  A horizontal line joins parents; a vertical line leads to their children, shown left to right from oldest.
  Generations in Roman numerals (I, II, III); individuals numbered within each generation (II-3).
**Autosomal recessive**
  Two unaffected parents have an affected child → the trait must be recessive (both parents are carriers).
  Often skips generations; males and females about equally affected.
  Autosomal, not X-linked, if an affected female has an unaffected son, or an affected daughter has an unaffected father.
  Examples: cystic fibrosis, phenylketonuria (PKU), sickle-cell anaemia, albinism.
**Autosomal dominant**
  Two affected parents have an unaffected child → the trait must be dominant (both parents heterozygous).
  Every affected person has at least one affected parent; it doesn't skip generations; males and females equally affected.
  Autosomal, not X-linked, if an affected father has an unaffected daughter, or an affected son has an unaffected mother.
  Examples: Huntington's disease, achondroplasia, Marfan syndrome.
**X-linked recessive**
  Many more males affected than females (males need only one copy).
  An affected male usually has an unaffected carrier mother; he passes the allele to all his daughters (carriers) and none of his sons.
  An affected female must have an affected father, and all her sons will be affected.
  Examples: haemophilia A, red–green colour blindness, Duchenne muscular dystrophy.
**X-linked dominant**
  An affected male passes the trait to **all** his daughters and **none** of his sons.
  An affected male must have an affected mother.
  Usually more females affected. Example: hypophosphataemic (vitamin D–resistant) rickets.
**Y-linked (going further)**
  Only males affected, and every son of an affected male is affected.
**A method that always works**
  1. Look for two unaffected parents with an affected child (recessive) or two affected parents with an unaffected child (dominant).
  2. For the remaining options, find a specific individual (or pair) that rules each one out.
  3. Assign genotypes to everyone you can, then answer probability questions.
**Worked example**
  Two unaffected parents (I-1 male, I-2 female) have an affected daughter (II-2). What is the mode of inheritance, and what is the chance their next child is affected?
    Unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive.
    If it were X-linked recessive, the affected daughter's father would have to be affected. He isn't, so it's autosomal recessive.
    Both parents are Aa (carriers). Aa × Aa → 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa.
    Chance the next child is affected = 1/4 (25%).
**Going deeper**
  Real pedigrees can hide the pattern: small families, new mutations, late-onset conditions (Huntington's disease often appears after 40) and dominant alleles that don't always show (incomplete penetrance) all make them harder to read.
**Common mistakes**
  Concluding X-linked just because more males are affected in one small family. Use a rule-out with named individuals.
  Saying a mode "can't be" without citing the individuals that rule it out.
  Forgetting that unaffected people in a recessive pedigree may be carriers.
  Mixing "chance a son is affected" with "chance a child is an affected son".
**Exam tips**
  Name individuals by generation and number (II-3) in every justification.
  For sex-linked probability questions, read exactly what is asked: of the sons? Of all children?
**Connections**
  Writing genotypes: Xᴴ Xʰ notation.
  Monohybrid crosses: predicting risks for future children.
  Homologous chromosomes: why males show X-linked recessive traits more often.
**Practice questions**
  An affected man and an unaffected woman have four children: all daughters affected, all sons unaffected. Which mode of inheritance does this suggest, and why?
    X-linked dominant: an affected male gives his X (with the dominant allele) to every daughter and his Y to every son, so all daughters are affected and no sons.
  Explain why two parents who both have an autosomal dominant condition can have an unaffected child.
    Both parents can be heterozygous (Aa). Each can pass on the recessive allele, so there is a 1/4 chance of an aa child, who is unaffected.
  A woman is a carrier of haemophilia (Xᴴ Xʰ) and her partner is unaffected (Xᴴ Y). What is the probability that a son will have haemophilia?
    Sons get Y from their father and either Xᴴ or Xʰ from their mother, so 1/2 (50%) of sons are expected to have haemophilia.
  Why can an unaffected woman have a son with an X-linked recessive condition?
    She can be a carrier (heterozygous). Her son gets his only X from her, so if he inherits the X with the recessive allele he is affected.
**Flashcards**
  Two unaffected parents have an affected child. What does this tell you? :: The trait is recessive (both parents are carriers).
  Two affected parents have an unaffected child. What does this tell you? :: The trait is dominant (both parents heterozygous).
  Rule for X-linked recessive: an affected female must have an affected {{father}}.
  An affected male with an X-linked dominant trait passes it to… :: all his daughters and none of his sons.
  Give two examples of X-linked recessive conditions. :: Haemophilia A, red–green colour blindness (also Duchenne muscular dystrophy).
  Give an example of an autosomal dominant condition. :: Huntington's disease (also achondroplasia, Marfan syndrome).
  Why are X-linked recessive traits more common in males? :: Males have one X, so a single recessive allele is expressed.

@ 528f41cf U2.1.3.2 | Monohybrid crosses and test crosses
**In a sentence**
  A monohybrid cross follows one gene: write the parents' genotypes, their gametes and a Punnett square to predict the offspring ratios — and a test cross with a homozygous recessive reveals whether a dominant-looking individual is homozygous or heterozygous.
**Key terms**
  **Monohybrid cross**: a cross that follows the inheritance of one gene.
  **Punnett square**: a grid showing every combination of the parents' gametes.
  **P, F1, F2**: the parent generation, their offspring (first filial), and the offspring of F1 × F1.
  **Test cross**: crossing an individual with the dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive individual to find its genotype.
  **True-breeding**: homozygous; always produces offspring like itself when crossed with the same.
**The steps for any cross**
  1. Define the alleles (Let B = black, b = brown).
  2. Write the parents' genotypes and phenotypes.
  3. Write the gametes each parent can make (one allele each).
  4. Combine the gametes in a Punnett square.
  5. Give the genotype and phenotype ratios.
**The key crosses**
  BB × bb → all Bb (all black). A true-breeding cross gives a uniform F1.
  Bb × Bb → 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb; phenotypes 3 black : 1 brown.
    Punnett: gametes B, b from each parent → BB, Bb, Bb, bb.
  Bb × bb → 1 Bb : 1 bb; phenotypes 1 black : 1 brown.
  Codominance or incomplete dominance (e.g. Cᴿ Cᵂ × Cᴿ Cᵂ) → 1 : 2 : 1 phenotypes.
  X-linked: Xᴴ Xʰ × Xᴴ Y → daughters Xᴴ Xᴴ and Xᴴ Xʰ (all unaffected, half carriers); sons Xᴴ Y and Xʰ Y (half affected).
  ABO: Iᴬ i × Iᴮ i → Iᴬ Iᴮ (AB), Iᴬ i (A), Iᴮ i (B), i i (O), each 1/4.
**The test cross**
  Problem: a black animal could be BB or Bb — they look the same.
  Cross it with a homozygous recessive (bb), whose gametes can only carry b, so each offspring's phenotype shows which allele came from the unknown parent.
  All offspring black → the unknown is (almost certainly) BB.
  About half black, half brown (1 : 1) → the unknown is Bb.
  Even one brown offspring proves the unknown carries b.
**Ratios are probabilities**
  A 3 : 1 ratio means each child has a 3/4 chance of the dominant phenotype. In a family of four, anything can happen; large numbers of offspring get close to the ratio.
**Worked example**
  In guinea pigs, black fur (B) is dominant to white (b). Two heterozygous black guinea pigs have 16 offspring. Predict the numbers of each phenotype.
    Bb × Bb → gametes B, b from each.
    Punnett: BB, Bb, Bb, bb → 3 black : 1 white.
    Expected: 16 × 3/4 = 12 black, 16 × 1/4 = 4 white.
**Going deeper**
  Mendel crossed pea plants in the 1850s and 1860s. In one F2 he counted 787 tall and 277 short plants — about 2.84 : 1, close to 3 : 1. His large samples let the ratio show through chance variation.
**Worked example 2**
  A tall pea plant crossed with a short plant (tt) produces 52 tall and 48 short offspring. What was the tall parent's genotype?
    This is a test cross (tt is homozygous recessive).
    52 : 48 is close to 1 : 1, so some offspring received t from the tall parent.
    The tall parent was Tt.
**Common mistakes**
  Writing a gamete with two alleles (Bb). Gametes are haploid: B or b.
  Treating a ratio as a guarantee for a small family.
  Forgetting to state both genotype and phenotype ratios when asked.
  In sex-linked crosses, forgetting that sons get their X from their mother.
**Exam tips**
  Always show the key, parental genotypes, gametes and Punnett square — markers award each step.
  Give probabilities as fractions or percentages exactly as asked.
**Connections**
  Writing genotypes.
  Dominance, codominance and incomplete dominance: why ratios differ.
  Pedigrees: probabilities for future children.
  Two genes (linked or independent): the next step up.
**Practice questions**
  Cystic fibrosis is autosomal recessive. Two carriers plan a child. What is the probability the child will be a carrier?
    Ff × Ff → 1 FF : 2 Ff : 1 ff. Probability of a carrier (Ff) = 2/4 = 1/2 (50%).
  Explain why a homozygous recessive individual is used in a test cross.
    Its gametes carry only the recessive allele, so it can't mask anything; each offspring's phenotype directly shows the allele it received from the unknown parent.
  A woman with blood group A and a man with blood group B have a child with blood group O. Give the parents' genotypes.
    The child is i i, so each parent must carry i: mother Iᴬ i, father Iᴮ i.
  A test cross of a black mouse gives 8 black and 0 white offspring. Can you be certain it is homozygous?
    Not certain — with only 8 offspring a heterozygote could, by chance, pass on B every time (probability (1/2)⁸, about 0.4%). It is very likely homozygous (BB); more offspring would increase confidence.
**Flashcards**
  Phenotype ratio for Aa × Aa (complete dominance)? :: 3 : 1.
  Genotype ratio for Aa × Aa? :: 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa.
  Phenotype ratio for Aa × aa? :: 1 : 1.
  A test cross crosses the unknown with a {{homozygous recessive}} individual.
  Test-cross offspring are about half dominant, half recessive. The unknown was… :: Heterozygous.
  How many alleles of a gene does a gamete carry? :: One.
  Xᴴ Xʰ × Xᴴ Y: chance a son has haemophilia? :: 1/2.

@ 2814a627 U2.1.3.3 | Two genes: independent assortment and linkage
**In a sentence**
  Two genes on different chromosomes assort independently, giving a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio from two heterozygotes and 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 from a test cross; genes close together on one chromosome are linked, so they're inherited together apart from a few recombinants made by crossing over.
**Key terms**
  **Dihybrid cross**: a cross following two genes at once.
  **Unlinked genes**: genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on one), which assort independently.
  **Linked genes**: genes close together on the same chromosome, which tend to be inherited together.
  **Parental type**: an offspring phenotype combination that matches a parent's chromosomes.
  **Recombinant**: an offspring with a new combination of alleles, produced by crossing over between linked genes.
  **Recombination frequency**: recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring × 100%.
**Unlinked genes — independent assortment**
  Example: in peas, yellow seeds (Y) are dominant to green (y) and round (R) to wrinkled (r). The genes are on different chromosomes.
  YyRr makes four kinds of gamete in equal numbers: YR, Yr, yR, yr (each 1/4) — because the two pairs of chromosomes line up independently in metaphase I.
  YyRr × YyRr → a 16-square Punnett square → 9 yellow round : 3 yellow wrinkled : 3 green round : 1 green wrinkled.
  **Dihybrid test cross**: YyRr × yyrr → 1 yellow round : 1 yellow wrinkled : 1 green round : 1 green wrinkled.
**Linked genes**
  Genes close together on one chromosome travel together into gametes, so they don't assort independently.
  Show which alleles are on the same chromosome by writing them together: AB/ab means one chromosome carries A and B, its homologue a and b.
  With **no crossing over**, AB/ab makes only AB and ab gametes. A test cross (× ab/ab) gives only the two parental types, 1 : 1 — it looks like a monohybrid cross.
  With **crossing over** between the two loci in prophase I, a few Ab and aB gametes (recombinants) also form. A test cross then gives:
    many of the two parental types (roughly equal),
    few of the two recombinant types (roughly equal).
**Recombination frequency**
  Recombination frequency = number of recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring × 100%.
  The further apart two genes are on a chromosome, the more often crossing over happens between them, so the higher the frequency.
  The maximum is 50%, which looks the same as independent assortment.
  Going deeper: 1% recombination is used as 1 map unit, which lets geneticists map the order of genes on a chromosome.
**Recognising linkage in data**
  Test cross of a double heterozygote:
    About 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 → genes assort independently (unlinked).
    Two large classes and two small classes → linked, with crossing over; the small classes are the recombinants.
    Only two classes → completely linked (no crossing over between them).
**Worked example**
  In fruit flies, grey body (G) is dominant to black (g) and normal wings (N) to vestigial (n). A GgNn fly (from a GGNN × ggnn cross) is test-crossed with ggnn. Offspring: 415 grey normal, 405 black vestigial, 92 grey vestigial, 88 black normal. Are the genes linked? Calculate the recombination frequency.
    If unlinked, expect about 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 (about 250 each). The results show two large and two small classes, so the genes are linked.
    The parent's chromosomes are GN/gn (from its GGNN and ggnn parents), so grey normal and black vestigial are parental types; grey vestigial and black normal are recombinants.
    Recombinants = 92 + 88 = 180. Total = 415 + 405 + 92 + 88 = 1000.
    Recombination frequency = 180 ÷ 1000 × 100 = 18%.
**Going deeper**
  Recombination frequencies can be used to order genes: if A–B is 10%, B–C is 5% and A–C is 15%, the genes must lie in the order A–B–C, with B in the middle.
**Common mistakes**
  Expecting 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 for linked genes.
  Labelling the most common classes as recombinants. Recombinants are the least common.
  Thinking linked genes can never be separated — crossing over can separate them.
  Writing gametes with two alleles of the same gene (Yy R).
**Exam tips**
  For a dihybrid cross, list the four gametes of each parent before drawing the Punnett square.
  To decide linkage, compare observed numbers with the numbers expected for independent assortment, and say which classes are recombinant.
**Connections**
  Meiosis: independent assortment in metaphase I, crossing over in prophase I.
  Monohybrid crosses and test crosses: the same method with one gene.
  Genetic diversity (Area of Study 2): recombination creates new allele combinations.
**Practice questions**
  Explain why a plant with genotype AaBb (genes on different chromosomes) makes four types of gamete in equal proportions.
    In metaphase I the two homologous pairs line up independently, so A is equally likely to go with B or b, and a likewise. This gives AB, Ab, aB and ab, each 1/4.
  A test cross of a double heterozygote gives 48 : 52 : 50 : 50 across the four phenotypes. Are the genes linked?
    No. The four classes are roughly equal (1 : 1 : 1 : 1), which is what independent assortment predicts.
  Two genes are linked. A test cross of AB/ab × ab/ab gives 460 AB, 440 ab, 55 Ab, 45 aB. Calculate the recombination frequency.
    Recombinants = 55 + 45 = 100; total = 1000; frequency = 100 ÷ 1000 × 100 = 10%.
  Why do genes further apart on a chromosome have a higher recombination frequency?
    There is more distance between them in which a crossover can happen, so they are separated by crossing over more often.
**Flashcards**
  Phenotype ratio from AaBb × AaBb (unlinked, complete dominance)? :: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
  Phenotype ratio from a dihybrid test cross AaBb × aabb (unlinked)? :: 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
  What are linked genes? :: Genes close together on the same chromosome that tend to be inherited together.
  In a test cross of linked genes, the {{least}} common classes are the recombinants.
  Formula for recombination frequency? :: Recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring × 100%.
  What process produces recombinants between linked genes? :: Crossing over in prophase I of meiosis.
  Gametes made by AaBb (unlinked)? :: AB, Ab, aB, ab — each 1/4.
