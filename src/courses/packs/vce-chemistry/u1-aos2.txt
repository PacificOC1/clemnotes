@ 5bac605e U1.2.1.1 | Relative isotopic mass
**In a sentence**
  Because atoms are far too small to weigh individually, their masses are compared on a scale where one carbon-12 atom is exactly 12.
**Key terms**
  **Relative isotopic mass (Ir)**: the mass of an atom of an isotope relative to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  **Carbon-12 standard**: the reference isotope, assigned a relative mass of exactly 12.
  **Atomic mass unit (u)**: 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, about 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g.
  **Relative atomic mass (Ar)**: the weighted mean of the relative isotopic masses of an element's isotopes.
**Key ideas**
  Atoms are far too light to weigh in grams usefully, so chemists compare their masses on a **relative** scale.
  The standard is the **carbon-12 isotope**, which is given a relative mass of **exactly 12**.
  The **relative isotopic mass (Ir)** of an isotope is the mass of one atom of that isotope compared with 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
    Example: a hydrogen-1 atom is about 1/12 of a C-12 atom, so its Ir is about 1.008.
  Relative masses have **no units** — they are ratios.
  Relative isotopic mass is close to, but not exactly equal to, the mass number (protons and neutrons don't weigh exactly 1, and electrons add a little). Only C-12 is exactly a whole number.
**Related terms**
  **Relative atomic mass (Ar)**: the weighted average of the relative isotopic masses of an element's naturally occurring isotopes (next dot point). This is the number on the periodic table.
  **Relative molecular mass (Mr)** or formula mass: the sum of the Ar values of the atoms in a formula.
**Going deeper**
  Why carbon-12? It is common, easy to handle and measure precisely by mass spectrometry, and it gives relative masses very close to whole numbers for most isotopes.
  Relative isotopic masses aren't exactly whole numbers because protons and neutrons don't each weigh exactly 1 u, and some mass is converted into the energy that binds the nucleus.
  The relative scale and the mole are connected: 12 g of carbon-12 contains exactly one mole of atoms — which is how Avogadro's constant is linked to the scale.
**Common mistakes**
  Giving units (g) to a relative mass.
  Mixing up relative isotopic mass (one isotope) with relative atomic mass (average of all isotopes).
**Connections**
  Isotopes (Unit 1, Area of Study 1) are what is being compared.
  Relative atomic mass from mass spectrometry averages these values.
  The mole: molar mass in g mol⁻¹ equals the relative mass.
**Practice questions**
  What does it mean that the relative isotopic mass of ²⁴Mg is 23.99?
    One ²⁴Mg atom has a mass 23.99 times 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  Why doesn't relative isotopic mass have units?
    It is a ratio of two masses (the isotope's mass divided by 1/12 of a C-12 atom's), so the units cancel.
  Why is ¹⁶O's relative isotopic mass (15.995) not exactly 16?
    Protons and neutrons don't each have a mass of exactly 1 u, and a little mass is converted into nuclear binding energy.
**Flashcards**
  What is the standard for the relative mass scale? :: The carbon-12 isotope, assigned a relative isotopic mass of exactly 12.
  Define relative isotopic mass. :: The mass of an atom of an isotope relative to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  What are the units of relative isotopic mass? :: None — it is a ratio.
  How does relative isotopic mass differ from relative atomic mass? :: Relative isotopic mass is for one isotope; relative atomic mass is the weighted average for all naturally occurring isotopes of the element.
  The only isotope whose relative isotopic mass is exactly a whole number is {{carbon-12}}.
  What is an atomic mass unit? :: 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom (about 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g).
  Why is carbon-12 used as the standard? :: It is common and can be measured very precisely, giving near-whole-number relative masses for other isotopes.

@ b736e1b4 U1.2.1.2 | Relative atomic mass and mass spectra
**In a sentence**
  A mass spectrum shows each isotope of an element and how common it is, and a weighted average of those gives the relative atomic mass on the periodic table.
**Key terms**
  **Mass spectrometer**: an instrument that separates particles by mass (mass-to-charge ratio) and measures their abundance.
  **Mass spectrum**: a graph of relative abundance against m/z.
  **Relative abundance**: how common each isotope is, as a percentage or relative to the largest peak.
  **Weighted mean**: an average in which each value counts in proportion to how common it is.
**Mass spectrometry (what it tells you)**
  A mass spectrometer separates the isotopes of an element by mass and measures how much of each is present.
  The output (mass spectrum) shows a peak for each isotope: its position gives the **relative isotopic mass** (m/z), its height gives the **relative abundance**.
  You don't need to know how the instrument works — only how to read a spectrum.
**Calculating relative atomic mass**
  Ar is the **weighted mean** of the isotopic masses:
  $A_r = \frac{\sum(\text{relative isotopic mass} \times \text{abundance})}{\text{total abundance}}$
  If abundances are percentages, the total is 100.
**Worked example**
  Chlorine: ³⁵Cl (Ir 34.97, 75.8%) and ³⁷Cl (Ir 36.97, 24.2%).
    Ar = (34.97 × 75.8 + 36.97 × 24.2) ÷ 100
    = (2650.7 + 894.7) ÷ 100 = 35.45
    The Ar is closer to 35 because ³⁵Cl is more abundant.
**Worked example — finding an abundance**
  Copper has isotopes ⁶³Cu (62.93) and ⁶⁵Cu (64.93) and Ar = 63.55. Find the % of ⁶³Cu.
    Let x = % of ⁶³Cu, so ⁶⁵Cu = 100 − x.
    63.55 × 100 = 62.93x + 64.93(100 − x)
    6355 = 6493 − 2.00x, so x = 69.0%
**Going deeper**
  The spectrometer works with positive ions, so peaks are at mass-to-charge ratios (m/z); for singly charged ions m/z equals the relative isotopic mass.
  Peak heights are sometimes given relative to the biggest peak (= 100) rather than as percentages; then divide by the sum of the heights, not by 100.
  Ar values vary slightly with where a sample came from (isotope ratios differ in nature), which is used in forensic and archaeological "fingerprinting".
**Worked example 2**
  Magnesium's spectrum has peaks at 24 (height 79), 25 (height 10) and 26 (height 11). Find Ar (use mass numbers).
    Sum of heights = 79 + 10 + 11 = 100
    Ar = (24 × 79 + 25 × 10 + 26 × 11) ÷ 100 = (1896 + 250 + 286) ÷ 100 = 24.32
**Common mistakes**
  Taking a simple average of the isotope masses instead of a weighted one.
  Forgetting to divide by the total abundance when peak heights aren't percentages.
  Expecting Ar to be a whole number.
**Exam tips**
  Show the formula with values substituted — method marks are available even if the final arithmetic slips. Give Ar to two decimal places unless told otherwise.
**Connections**
  Relative isotopic mass is the input; the mole and molar mass use the result.
  Isotopes (Area of Study 1).
**Practice questions**
  Gallium is 60.1% ⁶⁹Ga (68.93) and 39.9% ⁷¹Ga (70.92). Calculate its Ar.
    Ar = (68.93 × 60.1 + 70.92 × 39.9) ÷ 100 = (4142.7 + 2829.7) ÷ 100 = 69.72
  Silver's Ar is 107.87 and its isotopes are ¹⁰⁷Ag (106.91) and ¹⁰⁹Ag (108.90). Which is more abundant?
    ¹⁰⁷Ag — the average (107.87) is closer to 106.91 than to 108.90.
  What does the number of peaks in an element's mass spectrum tell you?
    How many isotopes the element has (for singly charged atomic ions).
**Flashcards**
  What does the position of a peak in a mass spectrum show? :: The relative isotopic mass (m/z) of that isotope.
  What does the height of a peak in a mass spectrum show? :: The relative abundance of that isotope.
  Formula for relative atomic mass from isotopes? :: Ar = Σ(relative isotopic mass × abundance) ÷ total abundance
  Boron is 19.9% ¹⁰B (10.01) and 80.1% ¹¹B (11.01). Find Ar. :: (10.01 × 19.9 + 11.01 × 80.1) ÷ 100 = 10.81
  Why is chlorine's Ar (35.45) closer to 35 than 37? :: ³⁵Cl is much more abundant (about 76%), so it weights the average towards 35.
  What is plotted on the x-axis of a mass spectrum? :: Mass-to-charge ratio (m/z).
  Peaks are given relative to the largest (100). What do you divide by to find Ar? :: The sum of all the peak heights.

@ f514a387 U1.2.1.3 | The mole and Avogadro's constant
**In a sentence**
  The mole is chemistry's counting unit: 6.02 × 10²³ particles, chosen so that one mole of a substance weighs its relative mass in grams.
**Key terms**
  **Mole (mol)**: the amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles.
  **Avogadro's constant (NA)**: 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
  **Amount (n)**: the quantity of a substance in moles.
  **Molar mass (M)**: the mass of one mole of a substance, in g mol⁻¹.
**The mole**
  A **mole** is the amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons). This number is **Avogadro's constant**, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
  The mole links the relative mass scale to grams: one mole of any substance has a mass in grams equal to its relative mass.
    1 mol of C-12 atoms = 12.00 g. 1 mol of H₂O (Mr 18.0) = 18.0 g.
  **Molar mass (M)**: the mass of one mole, in g mol⁻¹. Numerically equal to Ar or Mr.
**The key formulas**
  $n = \frac{m}{M}$ — amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g mol⁻¹)
  $N = n \times N_A$ — number of particles = amount × Avogadro's constant
**Counting atoms within molecules**
  1 mol of H₂O contains 1 mol of O atoms and 2 mol of H atoms (3 mol of atoms in total).
**Worked example**
  How many molecules are in 9.0 g of water?
    M(H₂O) = 2(1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0 g mol⁻¹
    n = 9.0 ÷ 18.0 = 0.50 mol
    N = 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.0 × 10²³ molecules
  How many H atoms is that? 2 × 3.0 × 10²³ = 6.0 × 10²³
**Worked example — mass of one atom**
  Mass of one carbon-12 atom = 12.00 g ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.99 × 10⁻²³ g
**Going deeper**
  Why we need moles: reactions happen particle by particle, so equations are ratios of particles. Weighing gives mass; the mole converts mass into a count of particles.
  Scale: 6.02 × 10²³ grains of sand would cover Australia many metres deep, yet it's only 18 g of water molecules.
  Always say what the particles are: "1 mol of oxygen" is ambiguous — 1 mol of O₂ molecules contains 2 mol of O atoms.
**Worked example 2**
  What is the mass of 3.01 × 10²² molecules of CO₂?
    n = N ÷ NA = 3.01 × 10²² ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 0.0500 mol
    M(CO₂) = 12.0 + 2(16.0) = 44.0 g mol⁻¹
    m = n × M = 0.0500 × 44.0 = 2.20 g
**Common mistakes**
  Multiplying by NA when you should divide (or the reverse). Check: more moles means more particles.
  Forgetting to count atoms per molecule when asked for atoms.
  Giving answers to the wrong number of significant figures (match the data).
**Exam tips**
  Write the formula first (n = m/M, N = n × NA), then substitute with units. Keep 3 significant figures unless the data says otherwise.
**Connections**
  Relative atomic mass gives molar masses.
  Empirical formulas, concentration (c = n/V) and gas volumes (n = V/Vm) all convert to moles first.
**Practice questions**
  How many moles of atoms are in 0.25 mol of CH₄?
    Each molecule has 5 atoms, so 0.25 × 5 = 1.25 mol of atoms.
  Calculate the number of chloride ions in 5.85 g of NaCl (M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹).
    n(NaCl) = 5.85 ÷ 58.5 = 0.100 mol; n(Cl⁻) = 0.100 mol; N = 0.100 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²² ions.
  What mass of water contains 1.20 × 10²⁴ molecules?
    n = 1.20 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.99 mol; m = 1.99 × 18.0 = 35.9 g
**Flashcards**
  What is a mole? :: The amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's constant).
  Value of Avogadro's constant? :: 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
  Formula linking amount, mass and molar mass? :: n = m ÷ M
  Formula linking number of particles and amount? :: N = n × NA
  How many moles are in 4.4 g of CO₂ (M = 44.0 g mol⁻¹)? :: 0.10 mol
  How many oxygen atoms are in 0.50 mol of CO₂? :: 0.50 × 2 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 6.0 × 10²³ atoms
  The molar mass of a substance in g mol⁻¹ equals its {{relative molecular (or atomic) mass}}.
  Mass of 0.0500 mol of CO₂? :: 2.20 g (0.0500 × 44.0)
  How many moles of O atoms are in 1 mol of O₂? :: Two.

@ 7bde0544 U1.2.1.4 | Empirical and molecular formulas
**In a sentence**
  From a compound's percentage composition you can find the simplest ratio of its atoms (the empirical formula), and its molar mass then tells you the actual molecule (the molecular formula).
**Key terms**
  **Percentage composition**: the percentage by mass of each element in a compound.
  **Empirical formula**: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
  **Molecular formula**: the actual number of each atom in one molecule.
**Molar mass of a compound**
  Add the molar masses of every atom in the formula. M(C₆H₁₂O₆) = 6(12.0) + 12(1.0) + 6(16.0) = 180.0 g mol⁻¹
**Percentage composition by mass**
  $\%\text{ element} = \frac{\text{mass of that element in 1 mol}}{M} \times 100$
  Example: % carbon in CO₂ = 12.0 ÷ 44.0 × 100 = 27.3%
**Empirical formula**
  The **simplest whole-number ratio** of atoms of each element in a compound.
  Steps (the "percent to mass, mass to mole, divide by small, multiply till whole" method):
    1. Assume 100 g, so each % becomes a mass in g.
    2. Convert each mass to moles (n = m ÷ M).
    3. Divide all by the smallest number of moles.
    4. If not close to whole numbers, multiply all by the same small factor (×2 for .5, ×3 for .33 or .67).
**Molecular formula**
  The **actual** number of atoms in one molecule. It's a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.
  Multiple = molar mass of the compound ÷ molar mass of the empirical formula.
**Worked example**
  A hydrocarbon is 85.7% C and 14.3% H by mass, with molar mass 56 g mol⁻¹.
    In 100 g: 85.7 g C and 14.3 g H.
    n(C) = 85.7 ÷ 12.0 = 7.14 mol; n(H) = 14.3 ÷ 1.0 = 14.3 mol
    Divide by 7.14: C 1, H 2.00. Empirical formula CH₂ (M = 14.0).
    Multiple = 56 ÷ 14.0 = 4. Molecular formula C₄H₈.
**Going deeper**
  Percentage composition can come from combustion analysis: burning an organic compound and weighing the CO₂ and H₂O formed tells you how much C and H it contained (all the C ends up in CO₂, all the H in H₂O).
  Different compounds can share an empirical formula: CH₂O is the empirical formula of methanal (CH₂O), ethanoic acid (C₂H₄O₂) and glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆).
  Ionic compounds are only ever written as empirical formulas (there are no molecules).
**Worked example 2**
  A compound is 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O, with molar mass 60 g mol⁻¹. Find its molecular formula.
    In 100 g: C 40.0 ÷ 12.0 = 3.33 mol; H 6.7 ÷ 1.0 = 6.7 mol; O 53.3 ÷ 16.0 = 3.33 mol
    Divide by 3.33: C 1, H 2.0, O 1 → empirical formula CH₂O (M = 30.0)
    Multiple = 60 ÷ 30.0 = 2 → molecular formula C₂H₄O₂ (ethanoic acid)
**Common mistakes**
  Rounding ratios like 1.5 to 2 instead of multiplying everything by 2.
  Dividing masses by atomic numbers instead of molar masses.
  Using the percentage of a diatomic element with M of the molecule (e.g. 32 for O) — use the atom's molar mass (16.0).
**Exam tips**
  Set out working in a table (element / mass / moles / ratio) — it keeps the steps clear and earns method marks. Don't round ratios more than about ±0.1.
**Connections**
  The mole and molar mass provide the conversions.
  Families of organic compounds: molecular formulas lead to structures and names.
  Water of hydration (Unit 2) uses the same mole-ratio method.
**Practice questions**
  Find the percentage of oxygen in H₂SO₄ (M = 98.1 g mol⁻¹).
    4 × 16.0 = 64.0; 64.0 ÷ 98.1 × 100 = 65.2%
  A hydrocarbon is 82.8% carbon. Find its empirical formula.
    C 82.8 ÷ 12.0 = 6.90; H 17.2 ÷ 1.0 = 17.2. Ratio 1 : 2.49 → ×2 → C₂H₅.
  The compound above has molar mass 58 g mol⁻¹. What is its molecular formula?
    Empirical mass = 29.0; 58 ÷ 29.0 = 2 → C₄H₁₀ (butane or 2-methylpropane).
**Flashcards**
  What is an empirical formula? :: The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
  What is a molecular formula? :: The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule — a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.
  Calculate the percentage of nitrogen in NH₃ (M = 17.0). :: 14.0 ÷ 17.0 × 100 = 82.4%
  Empirical formula CH₂O, molar mass 180 g mol⁻¹. Molecular formula? :: C₆H₁₂O₆ (180 ÷ 30 = 6)
  After dividing by the smallest, you get C 1 : H 2.5. What next? :: Multiply both by 2 → C₂H₅
  Molar mass of Ca(OH)₂? :: 40.1 + 2(16.0 + 1.0) = 74.1 g mol⁻¹
  Empirical formula of C₆H₁₂O₆? :: CH₂O
  Percentage of oxygen in H₂SO₄ (M = 98.1)? :: 65.2%

@ 57b53f5f U1.2.2.1 | Families of organic compounds
**In a sentence**
  Organic compounds come in families that share a functional group, a general formula and typical properties, and knowing the family tells you how a compound will behave and what it's used for.
**Key terms**
  **Hydrocarbon**: a compound of only carbon and hydrogen.
  **Saturated / unsaturated**: only single C–C bonds / at least one C=C (or C≡C) bond.
  **Functional group**: the atom or group of atoms that gives a family its characteristic reactions.
  **Homologous series**: a family with the same functional group, differing by CH₂ from one member to the next.
  **Hydroxyl group**: –OH (alcohols). **Carboxyl group**: –COOH (carboxylic acids).
**Organic compounds and homologous series**
  Organic compounds are based on chains of carbon atoms (carbon forms four bonds and bonds strongly to itself).
  A **homologous series** is a family with the same functional group and general formula; each member differs from the next by CH₂. Properties change gradually along the series (e.g. boiling point rises with chain length).
  A **functional group** is the atom or group of atoms that gives a family its characteristic reactions.
**Alkanes — CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**
  Only C–C single bonds (saturated). Non-polar: dispersion forces only, insoluble in water, low boiling points rising with chain length.
  Fairly unreactive: burn in oxygen (combustion) and react with halogens in UV light (substitution).
  Uses: fuels — methane (natural gas), propane and butane (LPG), octane (petrol); also lubricants, waxes.
**Alkenes — CₙH₂ₙ**
  Contain a C=C double bond (unsaturated). Functional group: C=C.
  More reactive than alkanes: **addition** reactions across the double bond (H₂, halogens, H₂O, HX).
    Test: alkenes quickly decolourise orange bromine water; alkanes don't.
  Uses: making polymers (ethene → polyethene), making other chemicals (ethene + water → ethanol); ethene ripens fruit.
**Haloalkanes — CₙH₂ₙ₊₁X (X = F, Cl, Br, I)**
  An alkane with a halogen replacing a hydrogen. The C–X bond is polar, so they have dipole–dipole forces and higher boiling points than similar alkanes.
  Uses: solvents (dichloromethane), refrigerants and propellants (CFCs — now banned because they destroy ozone), anaesthetics, making PVC (from chloroethene).
**Alcohols — CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH**
  Functional group: **hydroxyl** –OH.
  Hydrogen bonding between molecules → much higher boiling points than alkanes of similar size. Small alcohols dissolve in water.
  Uses: ethanol as a fuel (E10 petrol), solvent, in drinks and hand sanitiser; methanol as a fuel and feedstock.
**Carboxylic acids — CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH**
  Functional group: **carboxyl** –COOH.
  Weak acids (partially donate H⁺ in water). Strong hydrogen bonding → the highest boiling points of these families for their size; small ones dissolve in water.
  Uses: ethanoic acid in vinegar and food preservatives; citric acid in foods; making esters (flavours) and polymers.
**Comparing boiling points (similar size molecules)**
  alkane < alkene (about the same) < haloalkane < alcohol < carboxylic acid
**Going deeper**
  Physical properties change steadily along a series: each extra CH₂ adds electrons and surface area, so dispersion forces and boiling points rise.
  Solubility in water falls along a series: the polar group stays the same while the non-polar chain gets longer. Methanol to propanol and methanoic to butanoic acid are fully miscible; longer ones are not.
  Combustion: complete combustion of any hydrocarbon or alcohol gives CO₂ and H₂O, e.g. CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l). Limited oxygen gives carbon monoxide or soot (incomplete combustion).
  Alkenes react by addition because the second bond of C=C is weaker and exposed: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br.
**Worked example 2**
  Write the formula of the alkane, alkene and alcohol with 5 carbons.
    Alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: C₅H₁₂ (pentane)
    Alkene CₙH₂ₙ: C₅H₁₀ (pentene)
    Alcohol CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH: C₅H₁₁OH (pentanol)
**Common mistakes**
  Saying alkanes are polar because they have C–H bonds.
  Writing the alcohol general formula as CₙH₂ₙOH.
  Counting the COOH carbon separately in the name (ethanoic acid is CH₃COOH — two carbons).
**Exam tips**
  When explaining a property, name the functional group, the intermolecular force it causes, and the effect — e.g. "the –OH group allows hydrogen bonding, so…".
**Connections**
  Bonding and intermolecular forces explain the property trends.
  Naming organic compounds (IUPAC) comes next.
  Addition polymers are made from alkenes.
**Practice questions**
  Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of propane.
    C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l)
  Explain why ethanoic acid has a higher boiling point than ethanol.
    Both form hydrogen bonds, but ethanoic acid has more electrons and its –COOH group can form more (and pair-wise) hydrogen bonds, so its intermolecular forces are stronger.
  Write the equation for ethene reacting with bromine and name the product.
    CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br (1,2-dibromoethane).
  Which family does C₃H₇Cl belong to, and what is its general formula?
    Haloalkanes, CₙH₂ₙ₊₁X.
**Flashcards**
  General formula of alkanes? :: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
  General formula of alkenes? :: CₙH₂ₙ
  General formula of alcohols? :: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH
  General formula of carboxylic acids? :: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH
  What is a homologous series? :: A family of compounds with the same functional group and general formula, each member differing by CH₂.
  Why do alcohols have higher boiling points than alkanes of similar size? :: Alcohols have hydrogen bonding between molecules (–OH); alkanes have only dispersion forces.
  How can you tell an alkene from an alkane? :: An alkene quickly decolourises orange bromine water (addition reaction); an alkane doesn't.
  The functional group of a carboxylic acid is the {{carboxyl}} group, –COOH.
  Equation for the complete combustion of methane? :: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)
  Why does solubility in water fall along a homologous series? :: The polar group stays the same while the non-polar chain grows.

@ e641b9f7 U1.2.2.2 | Naming organic compounds (IUPAC)
**In a sentence**
  IUPAC names are built from the longest carbon chain, the family ending, and numbered positions for anything attached, so every name points to exactly one structure.
**Key terms**
  **Semi-structural (condensed) formula**: groups written in order, e.g. CH₃CH₂OH.
  **Structural isomers**: compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures.
  **Chain isomers**: differ in the carbon skeleton (butane vs 2-methylpropane).
  **Position isomers**: differ in the position of a functional group (propan-1-ol vs propan-2-ol).
  **Alkyl group**: a branch from an alkane (methyl –CH₃, ethyl –CH₂CH₃).
**Ways to represent a molecule**
  **Molecular formula**: C₃H₈O — atoms only.
  **Structural formula**: every atom and bond drawn.
  **Semi-structural (condensed) formula**: groups written in order, e.g. CH₃CH₂CH₂OH (propan-1-ol), CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃ (methylpropane).
**Naming: the parts**
  Prefix for the number of carbons in the **longest chain**: meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6), hept (7), oct (8).
  Suffix for the family: -ane (alkane), -ene (alkene), -ol (alcohol), -oic acid (carboxylic acid). Haloalkanes use a prefix: fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-.
  Branches (alkyl groups) are named as prefixes: methyl (–CH₃), ethyl (–C₂H₅).
**Naming: the steps**
  1. Find the longest carbon chain containing the functional group.
  2. Number from the end that gives the functional group (or C=C) the lowest number. For carboxylic acids, the COOH carbon is always C1.
  3. Name and number branches and halogens; list them alphabetically; use di-, tri- for repeats (2,2-dimethylpropane).
  4. Use commas between numbers and hyphens between numbers and words.
**Examples**
  CH₃CH=CHCH₃ → but-2-ene
  CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ → but-1-ene
  CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ → propan-2-ol
  CH₃CH₂COOH → propanoic acid
  CH₃CHClCH₃ → 2-chloropropane
  CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ → 2-methylbutane
**Structural isomers (up to C5)**
  Same molecular formula, different structural formula (different arrangement of atoms).
  C₄H₁₀: butane and 2-methylpropane.
  C₅H₁₂: pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane.
  C₄H₈ (alkenes): but-1-ene, but-2-ene and 2-methylpropene.
  C₃H₇OH: propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol (position isomers).
  Branched isomers have lower boiling points (less surface contact, weaker dispersion forces).
**Going deeper**

  **Condensation polymerisation**: monomers with two functional groups join, releasing a small molecule (usually water).
  **Ester link / amide link**: –COO– / –CONH– joining units in polyesters / polyamides.
**Polymers and monomers**
  A **polymer** is a very large molecule made of many repeating units joined by covalent bonds. The small molecules it is made from are **monomers**.
  Polymers can be **natural** (proteins, starch, cellulose, DNA, natural rubber) or **manufactured/synthetic** (polyethene, nylon, PET).
**Addition polymerisation**
  Monomers with a **C=C double bond** join as the double bond opens up; the monomers add onto each other.
  **No other product** is formed — all the atoms of the monomers end up in the polymer.
  Examples: polyethene (from ethene), polypropene, PVC, polystyrene, PTFE (Teflon).
**Condensation polymerisation**
  Monomers each have **two functional groups** (e.g. –OH, –COOH, –NH₂) that react with the groups on other monomers.
  Each new link releases a **small molecule**, usually water (hence "condensation").

**Comparing**
  Addition: monomer needs C=C; one product only; backbone of carbon atoms.
  Condensation: monomer needs two reactive functional groups; small molecule also formed; backbone contains other atoms (O, N) in the links.
  Condensation polymers can often be broken back into monomers by **hydrolysis** (adding water), which matters for recycling and biodegradation.
**Going deeper**
  Monomers joined by condensation must be bifunctional (two reactive groups); a monomer with one group ends the chain, which is sometimes used deliberately to control chain length.
  Natural condensation polymers: proteins (amino acids linked by amide/peptide links), starch and cellulose (glucose units linked with loss of water), DNA.
  Natural rubber is an addition polymer (of isoprene) — not all natural polymers are condensation polymers.

**Common mistakes**
  Saying addition polymers lose water.
  Forgetting that a condensation monomer needs a functional group at **each** end (one group only would stop the chain).
**Connections**
  Addition polymers from alkenes develops addition polymerisation.
  Hydrolysis and recycling polymers: condensation links can be broken by water.
  Families of organic compounds: the functional groups (–OH, –COOH) come from alcohols and carboxylic acids.

**In a sentence**

**Key terms**
  **Thermoplastic (thermosoftening) polymer**: linear or branched chains with no covalent cross-links; softens on heating.
  **Thermosetting polymer**: chains joined by many covalent cross-links; doesn't soften, decomposes on strong heating.
  **Cross-link**: a covalent bond between two polymer chains.
  **Elastomer**: a lightly cross-linked polymer that stretches and returns to shape.
**Thermoplastics (linear polymers)**
  Long chains with **no covalent bonds between chains** — held to each other only by intermolecular forces (dispersion, sometimes dipole–dipole or hydrogen bonds).
  On heating, the weak forces between chains are overcome, chains slide past each other and the plastic **softens and melts**. It hardens again on cooling.
  Can be **remoulded and mechanically recycled**.
  Examples: polyethene, polypropene, PVC, polystyrene, PET.
**Thermosetting polymers (cross-linked)**
  Chains are joined by **covalent cross-links** into a rigid 3D network.
  On heating they **don't soften or melt** — the chains can't slide past each other. Strong heating breaks covalent bonds and they **char/decompose**.
  Hard, rigid, heat resistant; **can't be remoulded** or melted to recycle.
  Examples: melamine (kitchenware), Bakelite (electrical fittings), epoxy resins, polyurethane foam in some forms.
**In between: elastomers**
  A few cross-links let chains stretch then pull back (vulcanised rubber in tyres).
**Linking structure to properties**
  More cross-links → more rigid, harder, more heat resistant, less flexible.
  Longer chains in a thermoplastic → stronger intermolecular forces → higher softening point and strength.
**Going deeper**
  Thermosets are usually made in two stages: a liquid resin is shaped, then "cured" (heat or a hardener) to form the cross-links — that's why an epoxy glue sets once mixed.
  Vulcanisation adds sulfur cross-links to natural rubber: a few links make it elastic and durable (tyres); many make hard ebonite.
  Recycling: thermoplastics can be remelted; thermosets can only be ground up as filler or burned for energy — a major challenge for circularity.
**Common mistakes**
  Saying thermoplastics soften because their covalent bonds break. Only the intermolecular forces between chains are overcome.
  Saying thermosets melt at a high temperature — they decompose instead.
**Exam tips**
  Link each property to structure: "Melamine doesn't soften when heated because covalent cross-links hold its chains in fixed positions."
**Connections**
  Designing addition polymers: branching and chain length affect thermoplastics.
  Plastics and recycling: why thermoplastics can be mechanically recycled and thermosets can't.
**Practice questions**
  Why is a thermoset used for saucepan handles?
    It doesn't soften when hot, because covalent cross-links hold the chains rigidly in place, and it is a poor heat conductor.
  What happens to a thermoplastic chain structure when it is heated and cooled?
    Heating overcomes the intermolecular forces so chains move past each other (it softens/melts); cooling re-forms the forces and it sets in a new shape. The covalent bonds are unchanged.
  Why does a rubber band stretch and return, but a thermoplastic bag stay stretched?
    Rubber is lightly cross-linked, so chains are pulled back into place; the bag's chains have no cross-links, so they slide to new positions and stay there.

  Addition polymers from alkenes: the side group comes from the monomer.
**Practice questions**
  Explain why increasing chain length increases the melting point of polyethene.
    Longer chains have more electrons and more contact with neighbouring chains, so dispersion forces are stronger, and chains are more tangled — more energy is needed to separate them.
  Why is HDPE more dense than LDPE?
    Its unbranched chains pack closely together in ordered (crystalline) regions, so more mass fits in the same volume.
  What is the effect of adding a plasticiser to PVC?
    It separates the chains and weakens the forces between them, so they slide more easily and the PVC becomes flexible.

  Why is LDPE less dense and softer than HDPE? :: LDPE's branched chains can't pack closely, so there are weaker dispersion forces between chains.
  How does increasing chain length affect a polymer? :: Stronger dispersion forces and more tangling → higher melting point and greater strength.
  Why is PVC more rigid than polyethene? :: The polar C–Cl bonds add dipole–dipole attractions between chains.
  What does a plasticiser do? :: It sits between polymer chains, pushing them apart so they slide more easily — making the plastic flexible.
  Give a use of LDPE and explain it. :: Plastic bags or cling wrap — it is flexible and soft because its branched chains are loosely packed.
  Fewer branches allow chains to pack more closely, increasing {{density and strength}}.
  What is a crystalline region in a polymer? :: An area where chains are closely packed in an ordered way.
  Why choose plasticised PVC for a hose? :: It is flexible (plasticiser between chains), tough and weather resistant.

@ c62951ad U1.2.3.5 | Plastics, bioplastics and recycling
**In a sentence**
  Plastics can come from oil or plants and can end their life by mechanical, chemical or organic recycling — and "bio", "biodegradable" and "compostable" mean different things.
**Key terms**
  **Fossil-fuel-based plastic**: made from crude oil or natural gas feedstocks.
  **Bioplastic**: a plastic that is bio-based, biodegradable, or both.
  **Mechanical recycling**: sorting, cleaning, shredding and remelting thermoplastics.
  **Chemical recycling**: breaking plastics into monomers or other chemicals to make new materials.
  **Organic recycling**: composting or anaerobic digestion of compostable plastics.
  **Downcycling**: recycling into a product of lower quality or value.
**Fossil-fuel-based plastics**
  Made from monomers derived from crude oil or natural gas.
  Recycling codes: 1 PET, 2 **HDPE**, 3 **PVC**, 4 **LDPE**, 5 **PP**, 6 **PS**, 7 other.
**Bioplastics**
  **Bio-based**: made from renewable biomass rather than fossil fuels.
  **PLA** (polylactic acid): a condensation polymer (polyester) of lactic acid, made by fermenting sugars from corn or sugarcane. Bio-based **and** industrially compostable.
  **Bio-PE** and **Bio-PP**: chemically identical to PE and PP, but the monomers come from bioethanol (sugarcane). Bio-based but **not** biodegradable — recycled like ordinary PE and PP.
**Key terms (often confused)**
  **Bio-based**: made from biomass. Says nothing about how it breaks down.
  **Biodegradable**: broken down by microorganisms into CO₂, water and biomass — eventually, under some conditions.
  **Compostable**: breaks down within a set time in composting conditions without toxic residue. Many need **industrial** composting (high temperature), not a home compost.
  **Renewable** raw ingredients: from sources that can be replenished (plants), rather than fossil fuels.
**Recycling methods**
  **Mechanical**: collect, sort by type, clean, shred, melt, remould. Only for thermoplastics. Chains shorten each time, so quality drops (often **downcycled**). Mixed or contaminated plastics are a problem.
  **Chemical**: break polymers back into monomers or small molecules (depolymerisation, hydrolysis of PET, pyrolysis to oils), then make new plastic. Can handle mixed plastics and give virgin-quality material, but uses more energy.
  **Organic**: composting or anaerobic digestion of compostable plastics (e.g. PLA) with food waste.
**Circularity**
  A circular plastic keeps its material in use: designed for recycling (single material, no hard-to-remove additives), collected, and made into new products — ideally from renewable raw ingredients.
**Going deeper**
  Why mechanical recycling degrades plastic: heat and shear break some chains, shortening them, and contamination and mixed additives creep in — so recycled plastic is often blended with new plastic or used for lower-grade products.
  Why sorting matters: different plastics have different melting points and don't blend well; a small amount of PVC can ruin a batch of PET.
  PLA composts in industrial conditions (about 58 °C, controlled moisture) over weeks; in a backyard compost or the ocean it breaks down very slowly.
  Circularity questions to ask: is the feedstock renewable? Is the product designed for one material and easy recycling? Is there collection infrastructure? Is it actually recycled in practice?
**Common mistakes**
  Assuming bioplastics are always biodegradable.
  Putting compostable plastics in the recycling bin — they contaminate PE/PET streams.
**Exam tips**
  Define terms precisely before evaluating — many marks are lost by using "biodegradable" when "bio-based" is meant.
**Connections**
  Thermoplastic vs thermosetting: only thermoplastics can be mechanically recycled.
  Hydrolysis and recycling polymers (chemical recycling of condensation polymers).
  Biomass as a source of chemicals.
**Practice questions**
  Why is recycled PET often mixed with new PET for food packaging?
    Mechanical recycling shortens polymer chains and may introduce contaminants, lowering strength and purity; blending restores quality.
  Classify each: PLA, Bio-PE, HDPE — bio-based? biodegradable?
    PLA: bio-based and (industrially) compostable. Bio-PE: bio-based, not biodegradable. HDPE: fossil-based, not biodegradable.
  Give one advantage and one disadvantage of chemical recycling compared with mechanical recycling.
    Advantage: can handle mixed or contaminated plastics and gives virgin-quality material. Disadvantage: uses more energy and is more expensive; not yet widely available.
**Flashcards**
  List the fossil-fuel plastics HDPE, PVC, LDPE, PP, PS in full. :: High-density polyethene, polyvinyl chloride, low-density polyethene, polypropene, polystyrene.
  What is PLA made from, and what type of polymer is it? :: Lactic acid from fermented plant sugars; a condensation polymer (polyester).
  Is Bio-PE biodegradable? :: No — it is chemically identical to fossil PE. It is bio-based, not biodegradable.

  Composting a compostable plastic like PLA is called {{organic}} recycling.

@ c6961506 U1.2.3.6 | Hydrolysis and recycling polymers
**In a sentence**
  Condensation polymers can be broken back into their monomers by hydrolysis, and new processes — including enzymes — use this to recycle plastics like PET in a closed loop.
**Key terms**
  **Hydrolysis**: breaking a bond by reaction with water.
  **Depolymerisation**: breaking a polymer back into monomers.
  **Enzyme**: a biological catalyst (a protein).
  **Closed-loop recycling**: turning a used product back into the same product.
**Hydrolysis — the reverse of condensation**
  **Hydrolysis** breaks a bond by reaction with water. In condensation polymers it splits ester or amide links, giving back the monomers.
    Polyester + water → acid + alcohol monomers.
  Usually needs an acid, base, high temperature or an **enzyme** as a catalyst.
  Addition polymers (C–C backbones) can't be hydrolysed — one reason they persist in the environment.
**Innovations in condensation polymers**
  **PLA**: bio-based polyester that can be composted industrially (hydrolysed and digested by microbes).
  **PET chemical recycling**: hydrolysis or glycolysis breaks PET back to its monomers, which are purified and repolymerised into food-grade PET — closed-loop recycling without quality loss.
  **Enzymatic recycling**: enzymes such as PETase (discovered in bacteria) hydrolyse PET at mild temperatures.
  **Biodegradable polyesters** (PHA, made by bacteria) and new polyamides designed to break down.
  Dissolvable stitches: polyesters that hydrolyse slowly in the body.
**Linear → circular**
  Being able to break polymers back into monomers means the same material can be made into new products repeatedly, rather than landfilled or burned.
  Limitations: collection and sorting; energy and chemical inputs; cost compared with new plastic; some processes are not yet at large scale.
**Going deeper**
  Hydrolysis of an ester link: –COO– + H₂O → –COOH + HO– (the reverse of the condensation that formed it). Acids, bases or enzymes speed it up.
  PET's monomers are a dicarboxylic acid (terephthalic acid) and a diol (ethane-1,2-diol); after hydrolysis they can be purified and repolymerised to food-grade PET.
  Enzymatic recycling (e.g. modified PETase and other cutinase-type enzymes) works at mild temperatures, saving energy, and is being scaled up commercially.
  Biodegradation of polyesters in the environment is really slow hydrolysis, often helped by microbial enzymes.
**Worked example 2**
  What are the products of hydrolysing a polyester made from butanedioic acid and ethane-1,2-diol?
    Hydrolysis reverses each ester link, adding water.
    Products: butanedioic acid (HOOC–CH₂CH₂–COOH) and ethane-1,2-diol (HO–CH₂CH₂–OH).
**Exam tips**
  Link hydrolysis to the circular economy explicitly: monomers recovered → new polymer of the same quality → no downcycling.
**Connections**
  Addition vs condensation polymerisation: only condensation polymers have hydrolysable links.
  Plastics and recycling: chemical recycling methods.
  Green chemistry: catalysis and design for degradation.
**Practice questions**
  Why can nylon be hydrolysed but polypropene can't?
    Nylon has amide links that react with water; polypropene has only C–C and C–H bonds in its chain.
  Explain how enzymatic hydrolysis of PET contributes to a circular economy.
    Enzymes break PET into its monomers at mild temperatures; the monomers are purified and made into new PET of the same quality, so the material stays in use instead of being downcycled or landfilled.
  Give one limitation of chemical recycling of PET.
    Collection and sorting are still needed; processes can be energy- or chemical-intensive and cost more than making new PET; some are not yet at large scale.
