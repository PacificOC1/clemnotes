@ 8ed00730 U2.2.1.1 | Units of concentration
**In a sentence**
  Concentration says how much solute is in a given amount of solution, and the same solution can be described in mol L⁻¹, g L⁻¹, percentages or ppm — converting between them is mostly multiplying by molar mass or powers of ten.
**Key terms**
  **Solute / solvent / solution**: what dissolves / what does the dissolving / the mixture.
  **Molarity (mol L⁻¹, M)**: moles of solute per litre of solution.
  **%(m/v)**: grams of solute per 100 mL of solution.
  **%(v/v)**: millilitres of solute per 100 mL of solution.
  **ppm**: parts per million — mg of solute per L of solution (dilute aqueous) or mg per kg.
  **ppb**: parts per billion — µg L⁻¹; used for very low concentrations (e.g. lead in drinking water).
**Concentration**
  Concentration is the quantity of **solute** dissolved in a given amount of **solution**.
**The units**
  **mol L⁻¹ (M, molarity)**: $c = \frac{n}{V}$ — moles of solute per litre of solution. The main unit in VCE calculations.
  **g L⁻¹**: grams of solute per litre of solution.
  **%(m/v)**: grams of solute per 100 mL of solution. 0.9%(m/v) saline = 0.9 g NaCl per 100 mL.
  **%(v/v)**: mL of solute per 100 mL of solution (liquids in liquids). 40%(v/v) spirits = 40 mL ethanol per 100 mL.
  **%(m/m)**: grams of solute per 100 g of solution (sometimes used for solids).
  **ppm (parts per million)**: mass of solute per million parts of solution. For dilute aqueous solutions, **1 ppm = 1 mg L⁻¹** (because 1 L of water has a mass of about 1 kg = 10⁶ mg). Also mg kg⁻¹ for soils.
**Conversions**
  mol L⁻¹ → g L⁻¹: multiply by molar mass M.
  g L⁻¹ → mol L⁻¹: divide by M.
  %(m/v) → g L⁻¹: multiply by 10 (per 100 mL → per 1000 mL).
  g L⁻¹ → ppm (mg L⁻¹): multiply by 1000.
  Volume: 1 L = 1000 mL; divide mL by 1000 to get L.
**Worked example**
  Express 0.250 M NaCl (M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹) in g L⁻¹, %(m/v) and ppm.
    g L⁻¹ = 0.250 × 58.5 = 14.6 g L⁻¹
    %(m/v) = 14.6 ÷ 10 = 1.46%
    ppm = 14.6 g L⁻¹ × 1000 = 14 600 mg L⁻¹ = 1.46 × 10⁴ ppm
**Worked example — making a solution**
  What mass of CuSO₄ (M = 159.6 g mol⁻¹) is needed for 250.0 mL of 0.100 M solution?
    n = c × V = 0.100 × 0.2500 = 0.0250 mol
    m = n × M = 0.0250 × 159.6 = 3.99 g
**Going deeper**
  A handy chain: mol L⁻¹ ×M→ g L⁻¹ ÷10→ %(m/v); g L⁻¹ ×1000→ mg L⁻¹ (ppm) ×1000→ µg L⁻¹ (ppb).
  Concentration is per **solution**, not per solvent: 1.0 M means 1.0 mol in a litre of final solution, which is why volumetric flasks are filled to the mark after dissolving.
  Australian drinking water guidelines use mg L⁻¹: e.g. lead limit 0.01 mg L⁻¹ (10 ppb), fluoride about 1 mg L⁻¹.
**Worked example 2**
  Seawater contains about 19 400 ppm chloride. Express this in mol L⁻¹.
    19 400 mg L⁻¹ = 19.4 g L⁻¹
    c = 19.4 ÷ 35.5 = 0.546 mol L⁻¹
**Common mistakes**
  Using mL instead of L in c = n/V.
  Treating %(m/v) as grams per litre.
  Forgetting ppm = mg L⁻¹ only works for dilute aqueous solutions.
**Exam tips**
  Write units at every step; most errors in concentration questions are unit errors (mL vs L, mg vs g).
**Connections**
  The mole (Unit 1): n = m/M.
  Titrations and dilution use c = n/V and c₁V₁ = c₂V₂.
  Salts in water and colorimetry report results in these units.
**Practice questions**
  Convert 0.150 M glucose (M = 180 g mol⁻¹) to %(m/v).
    0.150 × 180 = 27.0 g L⁻¹ → 2.70%(m/v)
  How many grams of NaCl are in 250 mL of 0.9%(m/v) saline?
    0.9 g per 100 mL → 2.25 g in 250 mL (2.3 g to 2 sig figs)
  A water sample has 0.20 mg of lead in 500 mL. Is it within the 0.01 mg L⁻¹ guideline?
    0.20 mg ÷ 0.500 L = 0.40 mg L⁻¹ — 40 times the guideline, so it is unsafe.
**Flashcards**
  Formula for molar concentration? :: c = n ÷ V (mol L⁻¹, with V in litres)
  What does 5%(m/v) mean? :: 5 g of solute per 100 mL of solution.
  What is 1 ppm in mg L⁻¹ for a dilute aqueous solution? :: 1 mg L⁻¹
  Convert 0.50 M NaOH (M = 40.0) to g L⁻¹. :: 0.50 × 40.0 = 20 g L⁻¹
  Convert 2.0 g L⁻¹ to ppm. :: 2000 ppm (2.0 g = 2000 mg per litre)
  How many moles are in 25.0 mL of 0.200 M HCl? :: 0.200 × 0.0250 = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol
  Convert 3.0%(m/v) to g L⁻¹. :: 30 g L⁻¹
  Convert 19 400 ppm chloride to mol L⁻¹. :: 0.546 mol L⁻¹ (19.4 g L⁻¹ ÷ 35.5)
  What is 1 ppb in a dilute aqueous solution? :: 1 µg L⁻¹

@ b3d59a35 U2.2.1.2 | Solubility curves and temperature
**In a sentence**
  Solubility tables say which ionic compounds dissolve, and solubility curves show how much dissolves at each temperature — more for most solids when hot, less for gases.
**Key terms**
  **Solubility**: the maximum mass of solute that dissolves in a set amount of solvent (usually g per 100 g water) at a given temperature.
  **Saturated / unsaturated / supersaturated**: at / below / above the solubility limit.
  **Crystallisation**: solid forming from a solution as solubility is exceeded.
**Solubility tables**
  Show which ionic compounds are soluble or insoluble in water — used to predict whether a precipitate forms when solutions are mixed (see Unit 1 precipitation notes).
**Solubility curves**
  Graphs of **solubility** (usually g of solute per 100 g of water) against temperature.
  A point **on** the curve is a **saturated** solution (no more dissolves at that temperature).
  **Below** the curve: **unsaturated** (more can dissolve). **Above**: **supersaturated** (unstable; excess tends to crystallise).
  Cooling a saturated solution: mass that crystallises = solubility at the higher temperature − solubility at the lower temperature (per 100 g water).
**Effect of temperature**
  **Most solids** become more soluble as temperature rises (e.g. KNO₃ rises steeply); NaCl barely changes; a few (e.g. Ce₂(SO₄)₃, Li₂CO₃) become less soluble.
  **Gases** become **less** soluble as temperature rises — gas particles gain kinetic energy and escape the solution. Warm water holds less dissolved O₂, which stresses fish (thermal pollution).
  **Liquids**: many miscible liquids (e.g. ethanol in water) mix in all proportions; for partially miscible liquids solubility usually increases with temperature.
**Worked example**
  KNO₃ solubility is 110 g/100 g water at 60 °C and 32 g/100 g at 20 °C. A saturated solution in 100 g water at 60 °C is cooled to 20 °C. What mass crystallises?
    110 − 32 = 78 g of KNO₃
  For 50 g of water the answer would be half: 39 g.
**Going deeper**
  Why gases dissolve less when warm: dissolving a gas releases energy (it's exothermic), and warmer gas molecules have more kinetic energy to escape the liquid.
  Why most solids dissolve more when warm: dissolving usually absorbs energy (endothermic), and more energetic water molecules break up the lattice more effectively.
  Recrystallisation purifies solids: dissolve in hot solvent, filter off insoluble impurities, cool so the product crystallises while soluble impurities stay dissolved.
  Pressure matters for gases too: fizzy drinks hold CO₂ under pressure; opening the bottle lowers pressure and gas comes out.
**Worked example 2**
  At 50 °C, 85 g of KNO₃ dissolves in 100 g water. A solution of 60 g KNO₃ in 50 g water at 50 °C — saturated, unsaturated or supersaturated?
    Scale to 100 g water: 120 g per 100 g.
    120 > 85, so more than can dissolve — supersaturated (or undissolved solid would remain).
**Common mistakes**
  Reading solubility per 100 g water as per 100 g of **solution**.
  Saying gases dissolve better in warm water.
**Exam tips**
  Read solubility curves carefully: the y-axis is usually "per 100 g of water". Scale your answer if the mass of water is different.
**Connections**
  Precipitation reactions (Unit 1) use solubility tables.
  Removing impurities from water by precipitation.
  Latent heat and water's properties: thermal pollution of rivers.
**Practice questions**
  Explain why fish may die in a river downstream of a power station that releases warm water.
    Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, so fish may not get enough oxygen.
  Using the data 110 g/100 g at 60 °C and 32 g/100 g at 20 °C for KNO₃, what mass crystallises when a saturated solution in 250 g of water is cooled from 60 °C to 20 °C?
    (110 − 32) × 2.5 = 195 g
  Why does a can of soft drink go flat faster when warm?
    CO₂ is less soluble at higher temperatures, so more gas escapes.
**Flashcards**
  What is a saturated solution? :: One in which no more solute can dissolve at that temperature.
  What does a point above a solubility curve represent? :: A supersaturated solution.
  How does temperature affect the solubility of most solids in water? :: Solubility increases as temperature increases.
  How does temperature affect the solubility of gases in water? :: Solubility decreases as temperature increases.
  Why does warm water harm fish? :: Less oxygen dissolves in warmer water.
  A solution at 40 °C has 30 g solute per 100 g water; solubility at 40 °C is 60 g. Saturated or unsaturated? :: Unsaturated — more can dissolve.
  Mass crystallising on cooling = solubility at the {{higher}} temperature minus solubility at the {{lower}} temperature.
  Why does gas solubility fall with temperature? :: Gas molecules gain kinetic energy and escape; dissolving gases is exothermic.
  What is recrystallisation used for? :: Purifying a solid by dissolving it hot and letting it crystallise on cooling.

@ 4ec338f8 U2.2.1.3 | Removing impurities by precipitation
**In a sentence**
  Unwanted dissolved ions can be pulled out of water by adding something that forms an insoluble solid with them, then settling and filtering the precipitate away.
**Key terms**
  **Coagulation / flocculation**: tiny particles clumping into larger ones (flocs) that settle.
  **Sedimentation**: letting solids settle to the bottom.
  **Hard water**: water containing dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions.
  **Heavy metals**: toxic metals such as lead, mercury, cadmium and arsenic.
**The idea**
  Dissolved ions that make water unsafe or unpleasant can be removed by adding a chemical whose ions form an **insoluble compound** with them. The precipitate is then removed by settling (sedimentation) and filtration.
**Examples**
  **Heavy metals** (Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺, Cu²⁺, Hg²⁺ in industrial and mine waste water): add hydroxide (e.g. lime, Ca(OH)₂) or sulfide to precipitate metal hydroxides or sulfides.
    Pb²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Pb(OH)₂(s)
  **Phosphate** (from fertiliser run-off and sewage — causes algal blooms): add Al³⁺ or Fe³⁺ salts.
    Fe³⁺(aq) + PO₄³⁻(aq) → FePO₄(s)
  **Hard water** (Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ — forms scale): add sodium carbonate to precipitate CaCO₃.
    Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s)
  **Flocculation/coagulation** in drinking water treatment: aluminium sulfate (alum) forms a gelatinous Al(OH)₃ precipitate that traps suspended particles, which then settle.
**Considerations**
  The added chemical must not leave harmful ions behind; excess reagent may need treatment; the sludge must be disposed of safely (especially if it contains heavy metals).
**Going deeper**
  A typical drinking-water treatment sequence: screening → coagulation/flocculation (alum or ferric chloride) → sedimentation → filtration (sand) → disinfection (chlorine or UV) → pH adjustment and fluoridation.
  Choosing the precipitating agent: it should form a very insoluble compound with the target ion, not add harmful ions, and be cheap. Lime (Ca(OH)₂) is common because Ca²⁺ is harmless and OH⁻ precipitates many metals.
  Removing phosphate prevents eutrophication: algae bloom when fertiliser phosphate reaches rivers, then die and decompose, using up oxygen.
**Worked example**
  What mass of Pb(OH)₂ precipitates from 1000 L of waste water containing 2.07 mg L⁻¹ of Pb²⁺, if all of it precipitates?
    Mass of Pb²⁺ = 2.07 mg L⁻¹ × 1000 L = 2070 mg = 2.07 g
    n(Pb²⁺) = 2.07 ÷ 207.2 = 9.99 × 10⁻³ mol = n(Pb(OH)₂)
    m = 9.99 × 10⁻³ × 241.2 = 2.41 g
**Connections**
  Precipitation reactions and solubility tables (Unit 1).
  Salts in water and soil: detecting the ions.
  Gravimetric analysis uses the same reactions to measure amounts.
**Practice questions**
  Write the ionic equation for removing cadmium ions with hydroxide.
    Cd²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cd(OH)₂(s)
  Why is it important to dispose of the sludge from heavy-metal precipitation carefully?
    It concentrates the toxic metals; if dumped, they could leach back into water and soil.
  Explain why removing phosphate from sewage protects rivers.
    Phosphate feeds algal blooms; when the algae die and decompose, bacteria use up dissolved oxygen, killing fish (eutrophication).
**Flashcards**
  How does precipitation remove dissolved impurities from water? :: A chemical is added whose ions form an insoluble compound with the impurity; the precipitate is settled and filtered out.
  Ionic equation for removing lead ions with hydroxide? :: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Pb(OH)₂(s)
  How can phosphate be removed from waste water? :: Add Fe³⁺ or Al³⁺ to precipitate insoluble FePO₄ or AlPO₄.
  How can water hardness (Ca²⁺) be removed by precipitation? :: Add sodium carbonate to precipitate CaCO₃.
  What does alum do in drinking water treatment? :: Forms a gelatinous Al(OH)₃ precipitate that traps suspended particles so they settle (flocculation).
  What is eutrophication? :: Excess nutrients (e.g. phosphate) cause algal blooms whose decay uses up oxygen in water.
  Why is lime used to precipitate metals from waste water? :: Its OH⁻ precipitates many metal ions and Ca²⁺ is harmless; it's cheap.

@ f0e9eb03 U2.2.2.1 | Titrations and standard solutions
**In a sentence**
  Volumetric analysis finds an unknown concentration by reacting a measured volume of it with a solution of known concentration until an indicator shows the reaction is exactly complete.
**Key terms**
  **Titration**: adding one solution from a burette to another until the reaction is complete.
  **Standard solution / primary standard**: a solution of accurately known concentration / a substance pure and stable enough to make one by weighing.
  **Aliquot / titre**: the measured volume from the pipette / the volume delivered from the burette.
  **Equivalence point / end point**: where reactants are in exact stoichiometric amounts / where the indicator changes colour.
  **Concordant titres**: titres within 0.10 mL of each other.
  **Dilution factor**: the ratio of final to initial volume.
**Key terms**
  **Standard solution**: a solution of accurately known concentration.
  **Primary standard**: a substance pure enough to weigh out directly to make a standard solution. It must be very pure, stable (not absorbing water or CO₂ from air), have a known formula and a high molar mass (reduces weighing error). Example: anhydrous sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃. NaOH is **not** a primary standard (absorbs water and CO₂).
  **Aliquot**: an accurately measured volume delivered by a **pipette**.
  **Titre**: the volume delivered from the **burette** to reach the end point.
  **Equivalence point**: where the reactants have reacted in exactly the mole ratio of the equation.
  **End point**: where the indicator changes colour. A good indicator makes the end point match the equivalence point.
  **Concordant titres**: titres that agree within 0.10 mL; average at least three.
**Preparing a standard solution**
  Weigh the primary standard accurately; dissolve in a little deionised water; transfer (with rinsings) to a **volumetric flask**; make up to the mark with deionised water; invert to mix.
**Dilution**
  Moles of solute stay the same, so $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$.
  Example: 20.00 mL of 1.00 M HCl diluted to 250.0 mL: c₂ = 1.00 × 20.00 ÷ 250.0 = 0.0800 M.
**Rinsing the glassware**
  Burette: rinse with the solution it will hold (water would dilute it).
  Pipette: rinse with the solution it will deliver.
  Volumetric flask and conical flask: rinse with **deionised water** (the moles added are fixed, so water doesn't matter; the solution would add extra moles).
**Choosing an indicator**
  Strong acid + strong base: phenolphthalein or methyl orange (both work).
  Weak acid + strong base (equivalence point basic): phenolphthalein.
  Strong acid + weak base (equivalence point acidic): methyl orange.
**Calculation steps**
  1. Write the balanced equation.
  2. n(known) = c × V (V in L), using the average concordant titre or the aliquot.
  3. Use the mole ratio to find n(unknown).
  4. c(unknown) = n ÷ V; account for any dilution.
**Worked example**
  A 20.00 mL aliquot of HCl is titrated with 0.1000 M NaOH. Concordant titres average 18.45 mL. Find c(HCl).
    HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (1 : 1)
    n(NaOH) = 0.1000 × 0.01845 = 1.845 × 10⁻³ mol
    n(HCl) = 1.845 × 10⁻³ mol
    c(HCl) = 1.845 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02000 = 0.09225 M
**Worked example — diprotic acid**
  25.00 mL of H₂SO₄ needs 21.60 mL of 0.1500 M NaOH.
    H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
    n(NaOH) = 0.1500 × 0.02160 = 3.240 × 10⁻³; n(H₂SO₄) = 1.620 × 10⁻³
    c(H₂SO₄) = 1.620 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02500 = 0.06480 M
**Effect of errors (explain the direction)**
  Burette rinsed with water: titrant diluted → larger titre → calculated unknown too high (when the burette holds the known solution).
  Pipette rinsed with water: aliquot diluted → fewer moles → smaller titre → unknown too low.
  Overshooting the end point → titre too large.
**Going deeper**
  Accuracy of glassware: a 20.00 mL pipette and 250.0 mL volumetric flask are accurate to about ±0.03 mL and ±0.3 mL; a measuring cylinder is not accurate enough for standard solutions.
  Reading the burette: eye level with the bottom of the meniscus, to two decimal places (the second always 0 or 5).
  Rough titration first: find the approximate end point quickly, then do careful titrations adding drop by drop near the end point.
  If the unknown was diluted before titrating, multiply back: c(original) = c(diluted) × dilution factor.
**Worked example 2 (with dilution)**
  25.00 mL of vinegar is diluted to 250.0 mL. A 20.00 mL aliquot of the diluted solution needs 17.60 mL of 0.1000 M NaOH. Find the ethanoic acid concentration in the vinegar.
    n(NaOH) = 0.1000 × 0.01760 = 1.760 × 10⁻³ mol
    CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O (1 : 1): n(CH₃COOH) = 1.760 × 10⁻³ mol in 20.00 mL
    c(diluted) = 1.760 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02000 = 0.08800 M
    Dilution factor = 250.0 ÷ 25.00 = 10 → c(vinegar) = 0.8800 M
**Worked example 3 (standard solution)**
  What mass of anhydrous Na₂CO₃ (M = 106.0 g mol⁻¹) is needed for 250.0 mL of 0.0500 M solution?
    n = 0.0500 × 0.2500 = 0.01250 mol; m = 0.01250 × 106.0 = 1.325 g
**Exam tips**
  Lay out titration calculations in numbered steps: equation → n(known) → mole ratio → n(unknown) → c(unknown) → dilution. Each step is usually a mark.
**Connections**
  Neutralisation reactions (Area of Study 1) are what's being measured.
  Units of concentration and the mole.
  Indicators: choosing one whose colour change matches the equivalence point.
**Practice questions**
  A 20.00 mL aliquot of NaOH is titrated with 0.0950 M HCl; the average titre is 22.40 mL. Find c(NaOH).
    n(HCl) = 0.0950 × 0.02240 = 2.128 × 10⁻³ mol = n(NaOH); c = 2.128 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02000 = 0.106 M
  Titres: 21.45, 21.10, 21.15, 21.05 mL. Which are concordant, and what is the average?
    21.10, 21.15, 21.05 (within 0.10 mL); average 21.10 mL. The first (rough) titre is excluded.
  A student rinses the pipette with water before taking the aliquot of acid. Effect on the calculated acid concentration?
    The aliquot is diluted, so fewer moles of acid are present, the titre is smaller, and the calculated concentration is too low.
  Why is the conical flask rinsed with water, not with the solution it will hold?
    The number of moles in the aliquot is fixed by the pipette; extra water doesn't change it, but rinsing with solution would add extra moles.
**Flashcards**
  What is a primary standard? :: A substance pure and stable enough to be weighed directly to make a solution of accurately known concentration (e.g. anhydrous Na₂CO₃).
  Why isn't NaOH a primary standard? :: It absorbs water and CO₂ from the air, so its mass doesn't give an accurate amount.
  Difference between the end point and the equivalence point? :: Equivalence point: reactants in the exact mole ratio. End point: where the indicator changes colour.
  What are concordant titres? :: Titres that agree within 0.10 mL.
  What should a burette be rinsed with? :: The solution it will contain.
  What should the conical flask be rinsed with? :: Deionised water.
  Dilution formula? :: c₁V₁ = c₂V₂
  Which indicator for titrating ethanoic acid with NaOH? :: Phenolphthalein (the equivalence point is basic).
  10.00 mL of 2.00 M HCl is diluted to 100.0 mL. New concentration? :: 0.200 M
  c(vinegar) if the diluted (×10) solution is 0.08800 M? :: 0.8800 M
  Mass of Na₂CO₃ for 250.0 mL of 0.0500 M? :: 1.325 g

@ 49fc202a U2.2.3.1 | Greenhouse gases
**In a sentence**
  CO₂, CH₄ and water vapour absorb the infrared radiation Earth gives off and send some back down, keeping the planet warm — and extra CO₂ and CH₄ from human activity are strengthening that effect.
**Key terms**
  **Infrared (IR) radiation**: longer-wavelength radiation emitted by warm objects like Earth's surface.
  **Greenhouse gas**: a gas that absorbs and re-emits IR radiation.
  **Enhanced greenhouse effect**: extra warming from human-added greenhouse gases.
  **Global warming potential (GWP)**: warming effect of a gas relative to the same mass of CO₂ over a time period.
  **Carbon sink**: a reservoir that absorbs CO₂ (oceans, forests, soils).
**The greenhouse effect**
  The Sun's energy reaches Earth mainly as visible and UV light; the surface absorbs it and re-emits energy as **infrared (IR) radiation**.
  **Greenhouse gases** absorb some of this IR and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the surface, keeping the lower atmosphere warmer.
  The **natural** greenhouse effect keeps Earth's average temperature about 33 °C warmer than it would otherwise be (about 15 °C instead of −18 °C).
**Why these molecules absorb IR**
  Molecules with bonds that can bend or stretch in a way that changes the molecule's dipole absorb IR. CO₂, CH₄ and H₂O do; N₂ and O₂ (the main gases in air) do not.
**The three main gases**
  **Water vapour (H₂O)**: the most abundant greenhouse gas. Its amount depends on temperature (warmer air holds more), so it acts as a feedback that amplifies warming rather than a direct driver.
  **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**: from burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement making; also respiration and volcanoes. Long-lasting in the atmosphere. Concentration has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 420 ppm.
  **Methane (CH₄)**: from livestock digestion, rice paddies, landfill, natural gas leaks and wetlands. Much more potent per molecule than CO₂ (roughly 28 times over 100 years) but shorter-lived.
**The enhanced greenhouse effect**
  Human activities increase the concentrations of CO₂ and CH₄, so more IR is absorbed and re-emitted → global warming and climate change.
**Going deeper**
  Why these molecules absorb IR: IR makes bonds vibrate; only vibrations that change the molecule's dipole absorb IR. CO₂'s bending and asymmetric stretching do; N₂ and O₂'s single symmetric stretch doesn't.
  The atmosphere is transparent to most incoming visible light but absorbs outgoing IR — like a blanket rather than a glass roof.
  Methane oxidises in the atmosphere over about a decade, but it's much more potent per molecule while it lasts; CO₂ persists for centuries.
  Feedbacks: warming → more water vapour (amplifies); warming → melting ice → less reflection (amplifies); warming → more plant growth (small dampening).
**Exam tips**
  Explain the mechanism in steps: Sun's radiation absorbed by surface → surface emits IR → greenhouse gases absorb IR → re-emit in all directions, some back to the surface → lower atmosphere warms.
**Connections**
  Ocean acidification (Area of Study 1): CO₂'s other effect.
  Gas calculations: measuring gases produced by reactions.
  Biomass and renewable feedstocks: reducing CO₂ emissions.
**Practice questions**
  Explain why N₂ is not a greenhouse gas even though it is 78% of the atmosphere.
    Its only vibration (a symmetric stretch) doesn't change its dipole, so it doesn't absorb IR radiation.
  Give two human activities that increase methane levels and one that increases CO₂.
    Methane: livestock, rice paddies, landfill, natural gas leaks. CO₂: burning fossil fuels (or deforestation, cement making).
  Why is water vapour's effect called a feedback?
    Its concentration depends on temperature; warming increases evaporation, and the extra water vapour traps more heat, amplifying the warming.
**Flashcards**
  How do greenhouse gases warm the atmosphere? :: They absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth's surface and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the surface.
  Name the three major greenhouse gases in the study design. :: Carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄) and water vapour (H₂O).
  Why don't N₂ and O₂ act as greenhouse gases? :: Their vibrations don't change the molecule's dipole, so they don't absorb IR.
  Difference between the natural and enhanced greenhouse effect? :: Natural: warming from naturally present greenhouse gases. Enhanced: extra warming from greenhouse gases added by human activity.
  Give two human sources of methane. :: Livestock, rice paddies, landfill, natural gas leaks (any two).
  Why is water vapour described as a feedback rather than a driver? :: Its amount depends on air temperature; warming increases water vapour, which amplifies the warming.
  Greenhouse gases absorb {{infrared}} radiation.
  Why does CO₂ absorb IR but N₂ doesn't? :: CO₂ has vibrations that change its dipole; N₂'s only vibration doesn't.
  What is a carbon sink? :: A reservoir that absorbs CO₂, such as oceans, forests or soils.

@ 954c6263 U2.2.3.2 | Gas pressure and SLC
**In a sentence**
  Gas pressure comes from particles hitting the container walls, and chemists compare gases at standard laboratory conditions — 25 °C and 100 kPa — where a mole of any gas takes up 24.8 L.
**Key terms**
  **Pressure**: force per unit area (p = F ÷ A), in pascals (Pa).
  **Kinetic molecular theory**: a model of gases as fast, randomly moving particles with elastic collisions.
  **Absolute zero**: 0 K (−273 °C), the lowest possible temperature.
  **SLC**: standard laboratory conditions, 25 °C and 100 kPa.
  **Molar volume (Vm)**: volume of one mole of gas; 24.8 L mol⁻¹ at SLC.
**Gas pressure**
  **Pressure** is the force exerted per unit area. Gas pressure comes from gas particles colliding with the walls of their container.
  More collisions (more particles, smaller volume) or harder collisions (higher temperature) → higher pressure.
**Units**
  Pascal (Pa) = 1 N m⁻². 1 kPa = 1000 Pa.
  1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 101 325 Pa = 760 mmHg.
**Standard laboratory conditions (SLC)**
  **25 °C (298 K) and 100 kPa**.

**In a sentence**

**Key terms**
  **Ideal gas**: a model gas with no attractions between particles and negligible particle volume.
  **Universal gas constant (R)**: 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  **Kelvin temperature**: T(K) = T(°C) + 273.
**The equation**
  $pV = nRT$
  **R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹**.
  With p in **kPa** and V in **L**, the units work (kPa × L = J), so use: p (kPa), V (L), n (mol), T (K).
**Unit conversions**
  Pressure: Pa ÷ 1000 → kPa; atm × 101.3 → kPa.
  Volume: mL ÷ 1000 → L. (m³ × 1000 → L)
  Temperature: °C + 273 → K. Never use °C in pV = nRT.
**Worked example — find volume**
  Volume of 0.200 mol of O₂ at 35 °C and 150 kPa?
    T = 35 + 273 = 308 K
    V = nRT ÷ p = 0.200 × 8.31 × 308 ÷ 150 = 3.41 L
**Worked example — find amount**
  A 500 mL flask holds a gas at 98.0 kPa and 20 °C. How many moles?
    n = pV ÷ RT = 98.0 × 0.500 ÷ (8.31 × 293) = 0.0201 mol
**Worked example — combined gas law**
  With n constant: $\frac{p_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2V_2}{T_2}$

**Going deeper**

  Molar mass from pV = nRT: M = mRT/(pV), useful for identifying a gas.

    M = 4.40 ÷ 0.100 = 44.0 g mol⁻¹ (consistent with CO₂)

  Using °C, mL or Pa in pV = nRT with R = 8.31.
  Using 24.8 L mol⁻¹ when the conditions aren't SLC.
**Exam tips**

**Connections**

  1. Write the balanced equation.

  For gases at the same temperature and pressure, volumes are in the same ratio as moles (Avogadro's law).

**In a sentence**

**Gravimetric analysis**
  A method of finding the amount of a substance by **precipitating** it from solution as an insoluble compound of known formula, and weighing the precipitate.
**Steps**
  1. Dissolve an accurately weighed sample in water.
  2. Add excess precipitating agent so all of the ion of interest precipitates.
  3. Filter, **wash** the precipitate with deionised water (removes soluble impurities and spectator ions).
  4. Dry in an oven and weigh; repeat drying and weighing **to constant mass**.
  5. Calculate using mass–mass stoichiometry.

  n(precipitate) = m ÷ M → mole ratio → n(ion or compound of interest) → mass, then % in the sample.

    Percentage of SO₄²⁻ = 0.192 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = 9.60%

  Precipitate not dried to constant mass (still wet) → mass too high → result too **high**.
  Not washing the precipitate (soluble impurities left) → too **high**.
  Precipitate passing through the filter paper or lost in transfer → too **low**.
  Not enough precipitating agent (incomplete precipitation) → too **low**.
  Another ion also precipitating (co-precipitation) → too **high**.

    % Cl = 0.710 ÷ 1.50 × 100 = 47.3%
**Exam tips**

@ 55b326f2 U2.2.4.4 | Colorimetry and calibration curves
**In a sentence**
  Coloured solutions absorb light in proportion to their concentration, so measuring the absorbance of standards and an unknown at the right wavelength lets you read the unknown's concentration off a calibration curve.
**Key terms**
  **Absorbance (A)**: how much light a sample absorbs (no units).
  **Colorimeter / UV-visible spectrophotometer**: instruments measuring absorbance of visible / UV and visible light.
  **λmax**: the wavelength of maximum absorbance.
  **Calibration curve**: a graph of absorbance against concentration for standards.
  **Blank**: a sample with everything except the analyte, used to zero the instrument.
  **Cuvette**: the small transparent container holding the sample.
**The principle**
  Coloured solutions absorb some wavelengths of light. The amount of light absorbed (**absorbance, A**) is proportional to the concentration of the absorbing species (for a set path length and wavelength).
  A **colorimeter** uses visible light; a **UV-visible spectrophotometer** uses UV and visible light and can select a precise wavelength.
**Choosing the wavelength**
  Use the wavelength the substance absorbs most strongly (**λmax**) — gives the greatest sensitivity. It's the **complementary** colour to the solution's colour (a blue solution absorbs orange/red light).
**Making ions visible**
  Colourless or weakly coloured ions can be reacted with a complexing agent to form a strongly coloured complex (e.g. Fe³⁺ + thiocyanate → deep red complex; phosphate with molybdate → blue complex).
**Calibration curve**
  1. Prepare a series of **standard solutions** of known concentration (by dilution of a stock solution) that cover the expected range.
  2. Zero the instrument with a **blank** (the solvent and any reagents, without the analyte).
  3. Measure the absorbance of each standard at λmax and plot absorbance (y) against concentration (x). It should be a straight line through the origin.
  4. Measure the unknown under the same conditions and read its concentration from the graph (or use the line's equation).
  5. The unknown must fall **within** the range of the standards (don't extrapolate); dilute it if it's too concentrated, then multiply back by the dilution factor.
**Worked example**
  The calibration line is A = 0.125 × c (c in mg L⁻¹). A diluted sample (diluted 10 times) reads A = 0.500.
    c(diluted) = 0.500 ÷ 0.125 = 4.00 mg L⁻¹
    c(original) = 4.00 × 10 = 40.0 mg L⁻¹
**Limitations**
  Other substances absorbing at the same wavelength interfere; very concentrated solutions don't give a linear response; bubbles, fingerprints or scratched cuvettes cause errors.
**Going deeper**
  Beer–Lambert relationship: A = εcl — absorbance is proportional to concentration (c) and path length (l); ε depends on the substance and wavelength. You only need the proportionality.
  Complementary colours: a solution looks the colour it doesn't absorb. Blue copper(II) solutions absorb orange-red light (around 600–800 nm), so a red/orange filter is used.
  Standards should bracket the unknown and be made by accurate dilution of a stock solution with volumetric glassware.
**Worked example 2**
  Standards of Cu²⁺ give: 0.00 M → A 0.00; 0.020 M → 0.24; 0.040 M → 0.48; 0.060 M → 0.72. An unknown reads 0.36. Find its concentration.
    The line is A = 12 × c (0.24 ÷ 0.020 = 12).
    c = 0.36 ÷ 12 = 0.030 M
**Exam tips**
  Draw the line of best fit through the standards (and origin), read the unknown from the line, and state that it lies within the calibration range.
**Connections**
  Units of concentration and dilution.
  Salts in water and soil: measuring specific ions.
  Presenting data: calibration curves (key science skills).
**Practice questions**
  Why is a red filter used to analyse a blue-green copper solution?
    The solution absorbs red/orange light most strongly (its complementary colour), giving the greatest change in absorbance with concentration.
  An unknown reads A = 0.95, but the highest standard is 0.72. What should you do?
    Dilute the unknown accurately (e.g. ×2) so it falls within the range, measure again, then multiply the result by the dilution factor.
  Why is the cuvette handled only on its frosted sides?
    Fingerprints on the clear faces absorb or scatter light, giving an absorbance that's too high.
**Flashcards**
  What is the relationship between absorbance and concentration? :: Absorbance is directly proportional to concentration (at a fixed wavelength and path length).
  Why is λmax used? :: The substance absorbs most strongly there, giving the greatest sensitivity.
  What is a blank used for? :: To zero the instrument, so only absorbance by the analyte is measured.
  What is a calibration curve? :: A graph of absorbance against concentration for standards of known concentration, used to find an unknown's concentration.
  Why should the unknown's absorbance fall within the range of the standards? :: The relationship is only known (and linear) over that range; extrapolation may be inaccurate.
  A red solution absorbs mainly which colour? :: Green (the complementary colour).
  How can a colourless ion be measured by colorimetry? :: React it with a complexing agent to form a coloured complex.
  Absorbance has {{no}} units.
  What does the Beer–Lambert relationship say? :: Absorbance is proportional to concentration (and path length).
  Concentration if A = 0.36 on a line A = 12c? :: 0.030 M
