@ 7a1c500c U2.3.1.1 | Key concepts for your investigation
**In a sentence**
  Your investigation stands on its chemistry: know the reaction, define the terms, and explain how the concepts connect to your question.
**Key terms**
  **Research question**: the specific, testable question the investigation answers.
  **Background theory**: the chemistry that explains and predicts the results.
**What this means**
  Your investigation (on gases, acid–base or redox reactions, or analysing substances in water) must rest on the right chemistry — explained accurately and with key terms defined.
**Do this**
  Identify the core concepts your question depends on (e.g. for "How does temperature affect the rate of CO₂ production from an antacid?": neutralisation, the carbonate–acid reaction, gas volumes, the ideal gas equation).
  Define each key term precisely (e.g. "titre", "limiting reactant", "molar volume").
  Write the balanced equation(s) with states for the reaction you studied.
  Explain why the concepts matter to your question — how they let you predict or interpret results.
**Going deeper**
  A strong introduction moves from general to specific: the chemistry concept → why it matters in the real world → the gap your question addresses → your hypothesis.
  Suitable Unit 2 topics: gas production (e.g. CO₂ from antacids or carbonates), acid–base reactions (e.g. ethanoic acid in vinegar brands), redox (e.g. metal reactivity, simple cells), analysis of water (e.g. salinity, hardness).
**Worked example**
  Question: "How does the concentration of hydrochloric acid affect the volume of CO₂ produced per minute from marble chips?"
    Key concepts: acid–carbonate neutralisation, rate of reaction and collision theory, measuring gas volumes.
    Equation: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
    Key terms to define: concentration (mol L⁻¹), rate of reaction, limiting reactant.
**Connections**
  Every Area of Study 1 and 2 learning point can be the chemistry behind an investigation.
  Writing a scientific report: where the concepts go.
**Practice questions**
  Why should the balanced equation for your reaction appear in your report?
    It shows the chemistry being studied and is needed for any stoichiometry (predicted amounts, yield).
  Suggest the key concepts behind an investigation of the ethanoic acid content of different vinegar brands.
    Acid–base neutralisation, titration (standard solutions, indicators, concordant titres), concentration units and dilution.
**Flashcards**
  Why must you define key terms in an investigation report? :: So your chemistry is precise and your reader understands exactly what was measured and concluded.
  What should accompany the chemistry of a reaction studied in an investigation? :: A balanced equation with states, and an explanation of how the concepts relate to the research question.
  Which concepts underpin an investigation into the volume of CO₂ from an antacid? :: Acid–carbonate reactions, stoichiometry and gas volumes (molar volume or pV = nRT).
  Why should the chemistry in your introduction be linked to your question? :: It shows how the concepts let you predict and explain your results.
  Define "titre". :: The volume of solution delivered from the burette to reach the end point.

@ f21735a8 U2.3.1.2 | Choosing a scientific methodology
**In a sentence**
  The methodology is the overall approach — experiment, fieldwork, modelling and so on — and it has to match what your question is asking.
**Key terms**
  **Methodology**: the overall approach used to answer a question.
  **Method**: the specific steps and equipment used.
  **Fieldwork**: gathering data in the natural environment.
  **Simulation**: imitating a real process with a model to study it.
**The methodologies (choose the one that fits the question)**
  **Classification and identification**: sorting or identifying substances by their properties (e.g. identifying unknown salts by precipitation tests).
  **Controlled experiment**: changing one **independent variable**, measuring the **dependent variable**, keeping everything else **controlled** (e.g. effect of acid concentration on the volume of gas produced).
  **Fieldwork**: observing or sampling in the natural environment (e.g. measuring the salinity of a local creek at several sites).
  **Modelling**: building a physical, conceptual or mathematical model to represent and predict (e.g. modelling ocean acidification with CO₂ bubbled through water).
  **Product, process or system development**: designing or improving something to meet a need (e.g. designing a cheap water-filtration method and testing its effectiveness).
  **Simulation**: using a model to mimic a real process that's too big, slow, dangerous or expensive to study directly (e.g. a computer simulation of titration curves).
**How to choose**
  Match the methodology to what the question asks: "what effect does X have on Y" → controlled experiment; "what is present" → classification and identification; "how much is in the local environment" → fieldwork.
  Justify the choice in your report.
**Going deeper**
  Controlled experiments give the strongest evidence of cause and effect because only one variable changes.
  Fieldwork is realistic but has many uncontrolled variables (weather, time, location), so conclusions are about patterns, not causes.
  Product or process development is judged against design criteria (cost, effectiveness, safety) rather than a hypothesis alone.
**Connections**
  Choosing an investigation methodology (key science skills).
  Variables: independent, dependent and controlled.
**Practice questions**
  Which methodology fits: "Which of three commercial antacids neutralises the most acid per tablet?"
    Controlled experiment (or classification/comparison using titration) — the antacid is the independent variable, the acid neutralised is the dependent variable.
  Give one strength and one limitation of fieldwork.
    Strength: real-world data. Limitation: many variables can't be controlled, so it's hard to show cause and effect.
**Flashcards**
  What is a controlled experiment? :: An experiment where one independent variable is changed, the dependent variable is measured, and all other variables are kept constant.
  Which methodology suits "How salty is the creek at different points"? :: Fieldwork.
  What is the difference between modelling and simulation? :: Modelling builds a representation to explain or predict; simulation uses a model to imitate a real process that's hard to study directly.
  Give an example of product or process development in chemistry. :: Designing and testing a low-cost water filter, or improving a method to make a bioplastic.
  Identifying unknown salts by their reactions uses the {{classification and identification}} methodology.

@ ca2e5f4d U2.3.1.3 | Generating primary data
**In a sentence**
  Plan exactly what you'll observe and measure, with equipment fine enough to detect the changes you expect, and record it all as it happens.
**Key terms**
  **Qualitative data**: descriptions (colour, fizzing, precipitate).
  **Quantitative data**: numerical measurements with units.
  **Data logger**: an electronic device that records measurements (temperature, pH, pressure) over time.
**Kinds of data**
  **Qualitative**: descriptions without numbers — colour changes, precipitate formed, fizzing, smell. Useful for identification and for noticing problems.
  **Quantitative**: numerical measurements with units — mass, volume, temperature, pH, time, absorbance, conductivity.
**Techniques relevant to Unit 2**
  Titration (burette, pipette, volumetric flask) for concentrations.
  Gas collection (gas syringe, displacement of water) or mass loss for gas volumes.
  Gravimetric analysis (precipitate, filter, dry to constant mass).
  pH meters and indicators; conductivity meters; colorimetry with a calibration curve; data loggers and probes (temperature, pressure).
**Good practice**
  Choose equipment with suitable resolution (a burette reads to 0.05 mL; a measuring cylinder is much coarser).
  Calibrate instruments; repeat measurements (at least three); record raw data immediately in your logbook with units and uncertainties.
**Going deeper**
  Match resolution to the size of change: if the temperature only rises 2 °C, a thermometer marked in 1 °C steps gives large relative uncertainty; a 0.1 °C probe is better.
  Record uncertainty with measurements where possible (e.g. 25.0 ± 0.1 mL for a burette reading).
  Plan the range and interval of the independent variable: at least five values across a sensible range, with repeats of each.
**Connections**
  Accuracy, precision and validity.
  Techniques from Area of Study 2: titration, gas collection, gravimetric analysis, colorimetry.
**Practice questions**
  Which is more suitable for measuring 20 mL of acid for a titration: a 25 mL measuring cylinder or a 20.00 mL pipette? Why?
    The pipette — it delivers a fixed volume far more accurately (about ±0.03 mL).
  Give two qualitative observations you might record when magnesium reacts with acid.
    Fizzing (bubbles of gas); the magnesium gets smaller and disappears; the flask gets warm.
**Flashcards**
  Difference between qualitative and quantitative data? :: Qualitative: descriptive, non-numerical (e.g. colour). Quantitative: numerical measurements with units.
  Give three techniques for generating quantitative data in Unit 2 chemistry. :: Titration, gas-volume collection, gravimetric analysis, pH or conductivity measurement, colorimetry (any three).
  Why choose a burette rather than a measuring cylinder for a titration? :: It has a much finer resolution and is more accurate for delivering variable volumes.
  Give an example of qualitative data in a precipitation experiment. :: The colour of the precipitate, or that a precipitate formed at all.
  Give an example of a data logger measurement. :: Temperature, pH or gas pressure recorded automatically over time.

@ 0ca503f9 U2.3.1.4 | Accuracy, precision and validity
**In a sentence**
  Good measurements are accurate (near the true value), precise (consistent), repeatable, reproducible, fine enough (resolution) and valid (actually measuring what you intend).
**Key terms**
  **Uncertainty**: the range within which the true value is likely to lie.
  **True value**: the value that would be obtained in an ideal measurement.
  **Measurement error**: the difference between a measured value and the true value.
**The terms (learn these word-perfect)**
  **Accuracy**: how close a measurement is to the true value.
  **Precision**: how close repeated measurements are to each other (little scatter).
  **Repeatability**: how close results are when the same person repeats the measurement with the same method, equipment and conditions.
  **Reproducibility**: how close results are when the measurement is repeated by different people, equipment or labs.
  **Resolution**: the smallest change in a quantity that an instrument can detect (e.g. a balance reading to 0.01 g).
  **Validity**: whether the method actually investigates what it claims to (fair test, controlled variables, measuring the right thing).
**Errors**
  **Random errors** → scatter → reduce precision. Reduce by repeating and averaging, and using higher-resolution equipment.
  **Systematic errors** → consistent offset → reduce accuracy. Caused by uncalibrated equipment or a flawed method; not fixed by repeating; fix by calibrating or changing the method.
  **Mistakes** (personal errors, e.g. misreading or spilling) aren't errors to analyse — discard the result and repeat.
**Example**
  Titres of 21.30, 21.32 and 21.31 mL are precise. If the NaOH had absorbed CO₂ (lowering its real concentration), every titre would be too high — precise but inaccurate (systematic error).
**Going deeper**
  A dartboard picture: tight cluster on the bullseye = accurate and precise; tight cluster off-centre = precise, not accurate (systematic error); scattered around the centre = accurate on average, not precise (random error).
  Resolution isn't accuracy: a digital balance reading to 0.001 g can still be wrong if it hasn't been calibrated.
  Validity is threatened by uncontrolled variables and by measuring the wrong thing (e.g. measuring gas volume over water for a soluble gas).
**Connections**
  Accuracy, precision, repeatability and errors (key science skills).
  Indicators and pH meters: a worked comparison of these ideas.
**Practice questions**
  A student's titres are 18.20, 18.25, 18.20 mL, but the true value is 19.50 mL. Describe the data and suggest a cause.
    Precise but inaccurate — a systematic error, e.g. the NaOH solution being more concentrated than labelled or the burette not rinsed correctly.
  Why does repeating an experiment improve precision but not accuracy?
    Averaging reduces the effect of random scatter, but a systematic error shifts every result the same way.
  What is the difference between repeatability and reproducibility?
    Repeatability: same person, method and conditions. Reproducibility: different people, equipment or labs.
**Flashcards**
  Define repeatability. :: The closeness of results when the same person repeats a measurement with the same method, equipment and conditions.
  Define reproducibility. :: The closeness of results when the measurement is repeated by different people, with different equipment or in different labs.
  Define resolution. :: The smallest change in a quantity that an instrument can detect.
  What type of error affects precision, and how is it reduced? :: Random error; reduce by repeating and averaging (and higher-resolution equipment).
  What type of error affects accuracy, and how is it reduced? :: Systematic error; reduce by calibrating equipment or improving the method (repeating won't help).
  What makes an experiment valid? :: It measures what it's meant to, with the independent variable changed and all other variables controlled.
  Results clustered tightly but far from the true value are {{precise but not accurate}}.
  Precise but inaccurate results suggest what kind of error? :: Systematic error.

@ d15d26dc U2.3.1.5 | Health, safety and ethics
**In a sentence**
  Before any practical, identify the hazards, assess the risks, plan how to control them, and conduct and report your work ethically.
**Key terms**
  **Hazard**: something that could cause harm (a corrosive acid, a flame).
  **Risk**: the likelihood and severity of harm from a hazard.
  **Control measure**: an action that reduces risk.
  **PPE**: personal protective equipment (safety glasses, lab coat, gloves).
**Risk assessment**
  Before practical work, identify **hazards** (e.g. corrosive acids, flammable solvents, toxic heavy-metal salts, hot equipment, glassware), assess the **risk** (how likely and how serious), and plan **control measures**.
  Use **safety data sheets (SDS)** for each chemical: hazards, safe handling, PPE, first aid, spills, disposal.
**Common control measures**
  Safety glasses, lab coat, gloves; work in a fume hood for volatile or toxic substances; use the lowest concentration and smallest quantities that work; know where eyewash, shower and extinguisher are; dispose of heavy metals and solvents in labelled waste containers, not the sink.
**Ethics**
  Report data honestly (no fabricating or selectively deleting results); acknowledge sources and help; respect people and the environment (e.g. permission and minimal disturbance in fieldwork; informed consent if surveying people).
**Going deeper**
  The hierarchy of controls: eliminate the hazard → substitute something safer → engineering controls (fume hood) → administrative controls (procedures, signage) → PPE (the last line of defence).
  Common Unit 2 hazards: concentrated acids and bases (corrosive), hydrogen gas (flammable), heavy-metal salts (toxic), hot equipment, broken glass.
**Connections**
  Risk assessments and SDS; lab safety; ethical conduct (key science skills).
**Practice questions**
  Complete a risk assessment line for 2 M hydrochloric acid.
    Hazard: corrosive, irritant. Risk: burns to skin and eyes if spilled or splashed. Controls: safety glasses and lab coat, use lowest concentration that works, small volumes, rinse spills with water, eyewash nearby.
  Why is PPE the last line of defence?
    It only protects the wearer after exposure happens; eliminating or reducing the hazard stops the harm at its source.
**Flashcards**
  What is a safety data sheet (SDS)? :: A document for a chemical giving its hazards, safe handling and storage, PPE, first aid, spill and disposal information.
  Difference between a hazard and a risk? :: A hazard is something that can cause harm; risk is the likelihood and severity of harm occurring.
  How should heavy-metal waste be disposed of? :: In a labelled waste container for proper treatment, never down the sink.
  Give an example of ethical conduct in reporting investigations. :: Recording and reporting all data honestly, including unexpected results, and acknowledging sources.
  What is the hierarchy of controls (in order)? :: Eliminate, substitute, engineering, administrative, PPE.

@ a2a45fe1 U2.3.2.1 | Aim, hypothesis, model, theory, law
**In a sentence**
  An aim says what you'll find out, a hypothesis predicts it, a model represents it, a theory explains it broadly, and a law describes a consistent pattern.
**Key terms**
  **Aim**: the purpose of an investigation.
  **Hypothesis**: a testable prediction with a reason.
  **Model**: a simplified representation.
  **Theory**: a well-supported explanation of many observations.
  **Law**: a description (often mathematical) of a consistent relationship.
**Definitions**
  **Aim**: a statement of what the investigation sets out to do or find. "To determine how temperature affects the solubility of KNO₃."
  **Hypothesis**: a testable prediction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variables, with a reason. "If temperature increases, then the solubility of KNO₃ will increase, because…"
  **Model**: a representation (physical, visual, mathematical or conceptual) used to explain or predict phenomena.
  **Theory**: a well-tested, evidence-supported **explanation** of a broad range of observations (e.g. kinetic molecular theory). Explains why.
  **Law**: a statement or equation describing **what happens** consistently under set conditions, usually without explaining why (e.g. the law of conservation of mass; pV = nRT describes ideal gas behaviour).
**Key distinctions**
  A hypothesis is tested by one investigation; a theory is supported by many.
  A law describes; a theory explains. A theory never "becomes" a law.
**Going deeper**
  Examples from Unit 2: the ideal gas law (pV = nRT) describes how gases behave; kinetic molecular theory explains why; the "particles as tiny billiard balls" picture is a model.
  Theories are never "proven" but can be extremely well supported; a single reliable contradictory result can force them to be revised.
**Connections**
  Formulating hypotheses (key science skills).
  Results that fit or challenge models and theories.
**Practice questions**
  Classify: "pV = nRT", "Gas particles move randomly and collide elastically", "To determine the molar volume of hydrogen".
    Law; theory (kinetic molecular theory); aim.
  Rewrite as a proper hypothesis: "Temperature will affect solubility."
    "If the temperature of the water increases, then more KNO₃ will dissolve per 100 g of water, because dissolving KNO₃ absorbs energy and higher temperatures provide it."
**Flashcards**
  What is a hypothesis? :: A testable prediction of how the independent variable will affect the dependent variable, usually with a reason.
  Difference between a theory and a law? :: A law describes what happens (often as an equation); a theory explains why it happens.
  Write a hypothesis for how acid concentration affects the rate of gas production with magnesium. :: If the acid concentration increases, then the rate of H₂ production will increase, because more frequent collisions occur between acid particles and magnesium.
  What is an aim? :: A statement of what the investigation is trying to find out or do.
  Kinetic molecular theory is a {{theory}} because it explains the behaviour of gases.

@ 21f9a8b9 U2.3.2.2 | Results vs models and theories
**In a sentence**
  When results agree with accepted theory they support it; when they don't, check your method first — and if the disagreement is real and repeatable, the model may need refining.
**Key terms**
  **Consistent with**: in agreement with, within experimental uncertainty.
  **Anomalous result**: a result that doesn't fit the pattern or prediction.
**How science responds to evidence**
  Results that agree with a model or theory **support** it (but never prove it absolutely).
  Results that disagree may mean: an error in the investigation, conditions where the model doesn't apply, or that the model is incomplete and needs changing.
  First check the method and errors; if the result is repeatable and reproducible, it may lead to a refined or new model.
**Examples**
  Real gases deviate from the ideal gas equation at high pressure and low temperature (particles do have volume and attract each other) — the model is useful within limits.
  Water's anomalously high boiling point challenged simple "size decides boiling point" reasoning, and is explained by hydrogen bonding.
  Measured molar volume of H₂ close to 24.8 L mol⁻¹ supports the model.
**In your report**
  Compare your results with the accepted value or prediction; discuss whether they support the theory and explain any disagreement.
**Going deeper**
  Historical example: the shell model of the atom was refined into subshells because some ionisation energy data didn't fit it.
  "Within uncertainty" matters: 24.5 L mol⁻¹ vs 24.8 L mol⁻¹ may be consistent if your uncertainty is ±0.4 L mol⁻¹.
**Connections**
  Models, theories and their limits.
  Analysing primary data and errors.
**Practice questions**
  A student's molar volume at SLC is 22.1 L mol⁻¹. Does this challenge the ideal gas model? Explain.
    Probably not — an 11% difference points to an error in the method (gas lost, gas dissolved, oxide on the metal). It would only challenge the model if careful, repeated measurements consistently disagreed.
  How can a model be useful even though real systems don't follow it exactly?
    It explains and predicts behaviour well within certain conditions, which is enough for most purposes.
**Flashcards**
  What should you check first if results disagree with an accepted theory? :: Sources of error and the method; whether the results are repeatable.
  Why can't results prove a theory true? :: New evidence could always contradict it; results can only support it.
  When does the ideal gas model not fit real gases well? :: At high pressure and low temperature, where particle volume and attractions matter.
  Measured molar volume of H₂ is 24.6 L mol⁻¹. Does this support the model? :: Yes — it is close to the predicted 24.8 L mol⁻¹ at SLC (within about 1%).

@ dd997af6 U2.3.2.4 | Analysing primary data and errors
**In a sentence**
  Organise data in clear tables and graphs, describe the trend and relationship, calculate what's needed, and explain which errors affected the results and how.
**Key terms**
  **Line of best fit**: a straight line or smooth curve showing the overall trend.
  **Gradient**: the slope of a line (rise ÷ run), often a rate.
  **Random error / systematic error**: unpredictable scatter / consistent bias in one direction.
**Organising**
  Tables: independent variable in the first column; headings with quantities and units; consistent decimal places matching the equipment's resolution.
  Graphs: independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis; labelled axes with units; a suitable scale; a line of best fit (straight or smooth curve), not dot-to-dot.
**Analysing**
  Calculate means (excluding outliers, with a reason), percentages, percentage error, rates, gradients.
  Describe the **trend** (as x increases, y increases …) and the **relationship** (linear, directly proportional if through the origin, inverse, non-linear).
  Percentage error = |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100.
**Identifying sources of error**
  Relate each error to your actual method and say which direction it would push the result.
  Distinguish random (scatter) from systematic (consistent offset — e.g. uncalibrated balance, heat loss in every trial).
  Suggest specific improvements (not just "be more careful").
**Worked example**
  Measured molar volume 23.9 L mol⁻¹; accepted 24.8. Percentage error = (24.8 − 23.9) ÷ 24.8 × 100 = 3.6%. Consistently low values suggest a systematic error, such as gas escaping before the syringe was attached.
**Going deeper**
  Describe relationships precisely: "directly proportional" only if the line is straight **and** through the origin; otherwise "linear" or "positive correlation".
  When error questions ask for improvements, pair each error with a specific fix (e.g. "heat loss to the surroundings → insulate the beaker and use a lid").
**Worked example**
  Gas volume data: 0 s 0 mL, 30 s 18 mL, 60 s 34 mL, 90 s 46 mL, 120 s 54 mL. Describe and interpret.
    Volume increases with time, but the rate slows (the curve flattens).
    Initial rate ≈ 18 ÷ 30 = 0.6 mL s⁻¹; between 90 and 120 s ≈ 8 ÷ 30 = 0.27 mL s⁻¹.
    The rate falls because the acid is used up, so its concentration and collision frequency decrease.
**Connections**
  Graphs and relationships; processing data (key science skills).
  Accuracy, precision and validity.
**Practice questions**
  What's the difference between "linear" and "directly proportional"?
    Linear: a straight line. Directly proportional: a straight line that also passes through the origin (doubling x doubles y).
  A calorimetry experiment consistently gives values 20% lower than the data book. Identify the likely error and an improvement.
    Heat loss to the surroundings (systematic). Improve with insulation (polystyrene cup, lid), a draught shield, and less distance between flame and container.
**Flashcards**
  Which variable goes on the x-axis of a graph? :: The independent variable.
  Formula for percentage error? :: |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100
  What does a straight line through the origin show? :: The dependent variable is directly proportional to the independent variable.
  Why is "human error" not an acceptable source of error in a report? :: It's vague; you should name the specific error, whether it's random or systematic, and its effect.
  How should a line of best fit be drawn? :: A smooth line or curve that follows the trend with points balanced either side — not joining the dots.
  When is a relationship "directly proportional"? :: When the graph is a straight line through the origin.

@ 8dd8e210 U2.3.2.6 | Limitations of your investigation
**In a sentence**
  Every investigation has limits — in its methodology, method, data and analysis — and saying honestly what they are shows how far your conclusion can be trusted.
**Key terms**
  **Limitation**: a factor that reduces the reliability, validity or scope of findings.
  **Assumption**: something taken to be true without testing it (e.g. complete reaction, ideal gas behaviour).
**Kinds of limitation**
  **Methodology**: a controlled experiment can't control every variable; fieldwork has uncontrolled environmental variables; a simulation is only as good as its model.
  **Method**: equipment resolution; heat loss; gas escaping or dissolving; incomplete reactions; indicator end-point judgement.
  **Data generation**: small number of trials; narrow range of the independent variable; few data points to define a trend.
  **Analysis**: assumptions (ideal gas behaviour, complete reaction, 100% purity), rounding, outliers, choice of line of best fit.
**Why it matters**
  Limitations determine how confidently a conclusion can be drawn and how widely it can be applied.
**Writing it**
  State the limitation, explain how it affected the results (direction and size if possible), and suggest a specific improvement.
**Going deeper**
  Separate limitations of the method (fixable next time) from limitations of the methodology (built into the approach — e.g. a lab model of the ocean can't include every factor).
  Rank limitations: mention the one with the biggest effect first.
**Connections**
  Limitations of conclusions (key science skills).
  Evaluating and improving methods.
**Practice questions**
  State a limitation of using a school lab to model ocean acidification by bubbling CO₂ through seawater.
    The lab setup can't reproduce ocean conditions (volume, mixing, temperature, living organisms, time scale), so results may not apply directly to real oceans.
  Why is "the reaction goes to completion" a limitation to state?
    If it doesn't, the measured amounts will be lower than calculated, affecting accuracy.
**Flashcards**
  Give a limitation of using a small number of trials. :: Random errors and outliers have a larger effect, and precision can't be assessed well.
  Why is "assuming ideal gas behaviour" a limitation? :: Real gases deviate, especially at high pressure and low temperature, so calculated values may be slightly wrong.
  What three things should a good limitation statement include? :: The limitation, its effect on the results, and a specific improvement.
  Give a limitation of assuming a reaction goes to completion. :: If it doesn't, the amount of product is less than calculated, so results are too low.

@ 6614d11b U2.3.3.1 | Writing a scientific report
**In a sentence**
  A scientific report follows a standard structure and conventions so that any reader can understand, check and repeat your work.
**Key terms**
  **Abstract / summary**: a short overview of the whole report.
  **Discussion**: the section interpreting results, errors and limitations.
  **Conclusion**: a direct answer to the research question.
**Structure of a scientific report (or poster)**
  Title; aim and hypothesis; introduction/background with key chemistry; method (enough to replicate); risk assessment; results (tables and graphs); discussion (analysis, errors, limitations, evaluation against the hypothesis); conclusion (answers the aim); references.
**Conventions**
  Formal, objective language; usually third person and past tense for the method ("the solution was titrated…").
  Correct chemical terminology, formulas, IUPAC names and balanced equations with states.
  SI units and standard abbreviations: g, kg, mL, L, mol, mol L⁻¹, °C, K, kPa, s. A space between number and unit (25 °C, 10.0 mL).
  Significant figures consistent with the data; scientific notation for very large or small numbers.
  Tables and figures numbered and captioned; graphs with labelled axes and units.
**Going deeper**
  Past, passive voice is traditional for methods ("25.00 mL was pipetted"), though some teachers accept active first person — check your school's expectations.
  Tables: title above, units in column headings (not beside each number). Graphs: title, labelled axes with units, sensible scale, line of best fit.
**Connections**
  Chemical terminology and conventions.
  Presenting findings and implications.
**Practice questions**
  Where should units go in a results table?
    In the column headings (e.g. "Volume of CO₂ (mL)"), not after every number.
  What's the difference between the discussion and the conclusion?
    The discussion interprets and evaluates the results (trends, errors, limitations, comparison with theory); the conclusion briefly answers the research question with reference to the evidence.
**Flashcards**
  Name the main sections of a scientific report. :: Title, aim, hypothesis, introduction, method, results, discussion, conclusion, references.
  Why is the method written so that it could be repeated? :: So others can replicate the investigation and check the results (reproducibility).
  What should a conclusion do? :: Answer the aim, state whether the hypothesis was supported, with reference to the evidence.
  Write "twenty-five degrees Celsius" and "ten millilitres" in conventional form. :: 25 °C and 10 mL

@ 91e7eed5 U2.3.3.2 | Presenting findings and implications
**In a sentence**
  Lead with your key finding, back it with your best evidence, and explain what it means and what should come next.
**Key terms**
  **Key finding**: the main result that answers the research question.
  **Implication**: what the finding means for the chemistry, for people or for further research.
**Key findings**
  State the main result clearly with the supporting numbers (e.g. "the concentration of ethanoic acid in the vinegar was 0.82 M, within 3% of the label value").
  Use the best representation: a well-labelled graph showing the trend, or a summary table.
**Implications**
  Explain what the findings mean: for the hypothesis, for the wider chemistry, and in a real-world context (e.g. whether the local creek's salinity is safe for stock).
  Note the confidence you have in the findings, given the limitations.
  Suggest further investigation.
**For a poster**
  Short, clear headings; the key finding stands out; visuals over text; logical flow; concise wording (VCE posters for Unit 4 have a word limit — practise being concise now).
**Connections**
  Effective science communication (Unit 1).
  Implications of findings (key science skills).
**Practice questions**
  Write a one-sentence key finding for: vinegar labelled 4% ethanoic acid measured as 4.1 ± 0.1% by titration.
    "Titration showed the vinegar contained 4.1 ± 0.1% ethanoic acid, consistent with the 4% on its label."
  What might be an implication of finding high nitrate levels in a local creek?
    Fertiliser run-off may be entering the creek, risking algal blooms; further sampling and land-management changes may be needed.
**Flashcards**
  What makes a key finding clearly presented? :: A direct statement of the main result with supporting data, shown in the clearest representation.
  What does "implications" mean in a report? :: What the findings mean for the hypothesis, the chemistry and the real world, and what should be investigated next.
  Give two features of an effective scientific poster. :: Clear headings and logical flow; key finding prominent; visuals rather than text; concise wording.
  Why state your confidence in a finding? :: Limitations and errors affect how far the result can be trusted and applied.
