@ d3bc4dd5 S.1.1 | Constructing aims and questions
**In a sentence**
  A good investigation starts with a specific, testable question that names what you'll change and what you'll measure.
**Key ideas**
  A good investigation question is specific, testable, and names what will be changed and what will be measured.
    Weak: "Do antacids work?" Better: "How does the mass of CaCO₃ tablet affect the volume of CO₂ produced with 50 mL of 1.0 M HCl?"
  An aim states what the investigation will do: "To determine…", "To investigate the effect of … on …".
  Research background chemistry first so the question is meaningful and achievable with school equipment.
**Practice questions**
  Improve this question: "Does temperature affect reactions?"
    "How does the temperature of 1.0 M HCl (20–60 °C) affect the time for 0.10 g of magnesium ribbon to react completely?"
  Why should a question be achievable with school equipment?
    Otherwise you can't generate enough reliable data to answer it within the time and resources available.
**Flashcards**
  What makes a research question good? :: It is specific and testable, identifying the variable changed and the variable measured, and is achievable.
  Turn "Is vinegar acidic?" into a better investigation question. :: e.g. "What is the concentration of ethanoic acid in brand X vinegar, determined by titration with standardised NaOH?"

@ 313b7051 S.1.2 | Variables in an experiment
**In a sentence**
  Change one thing (independent), measure one thing (dependent), and keep everything else the same (controlled).
**Key ideas**
  **Independent variable**: the one you deliberately change.
  **Dependent variable**: the one you measure (it depends on the independent variable).
  **Controlled variables**: everything else that could affect the result, kept the same so the test is fair.
  Example: effect of temperature on the rate of Mg reacting with HCl. Independent: temperature. Dependent: rate (e.g. volume of H₂ per minute). Controlled: acid concentration and volume, mass and surface area of Mg.
  A **control** (control group) is a separate trial for comparison (e.g. no catalyst) — different from controlled variables.
**Practice questions**
  An investigation tests how the mass of antacid affects the volume of CO₂ produced. Identify the variables.
    Independent: mass of antacid. Dependent: volume of CO₂. Controlled: type of antacid, volume, concentration and temperature of acid, reaction time, collection method.
  What is the difference between a controlled variable and a control?
    A controlled variable is kept constant in every trial; a control is a separate comparison trial (e.g. with no antacid).
**Flashcards**
  Define the independent variable. :: The variable deliberately changed by the investigator.
  Define the dependent variable. :: The variable measured, which responds to changes in the independent variable.
  Why must controlled variables be kept constant? :: So that any change in the dependent variable can be attributed to the independent variable (a fair test).
  In testing how acid concentration affects CO₂ volume from marble chips, name two controlled variables. :: Mass and size of chips, volume and temperature of acid, type of acid.

@ 51e4a548 S.1.3 | Formulating hypotheses
**In a sentence**
  A hypothesis is a testable prediction about how the independent variable affects the dependent variable, with a chemical reason.
**Key ideas**
  A hypothesis is a **testable** prediction about the relationship between the independent and dependent variables, based on chemistry.
  Common form: "If [independent variable changes], then [dependent variable will change in this way], because [chemical reason]."
  It must be possible for the results to **refute** it.
**Practice questions**
  Write a hypothesis for: "How does acid concentration affect the rate of reaction with marble chips?"
    "If the concentration of HCl increases, then the rate of CO₂ production will increase, because there are more acid particles per volume, so more frequent collisions with the marble surface."
  Can a hypothesis be useful even if the results don't support it?
    Yes — refuting a hypothesis is still new knowledge and helps refine understanding.
**Flashcards**
  What is the "If … then … because" format for? :: Writing a hypothesis that links the independent and dependent variables and gives a reason.
  What makes a hypothesis scientific? :: It is testable and could be shown to be false by the results.

@ 38ac95d6 S.1.4 | Predicting possible outcomes
**In a sentence**
  Use theory and calculations to predict what should happen, so you can check your method is feasible and have something to compare results against.
**Key ideas**
  Use theory and calculations to predict results before doing the experiment: e.g. calculate the expected volume of CO₂ at SLC, or the expected titre.
  Predicting helps check the method is feasible (volumes fit the equipment) and gives a benchmark to compare results with (percentage error).
**Practice questions**
  Predict the mass of CO₂ lost when 2.00 g of CaCO₃ reacts completely with excess acid.
    n(CaCO₃) = 2.00 ÷ 100.1 = 0.0200 mol = n(CO₂); m = 0.0200 × 44.0 = 0.880 g
  How can a prediction show that a method won't work before you start?
    E.g. if the predicted gas volume is 500 mL but the syringe holds only 100 mL, the amounts must be changed.
**Flashcards**
  Why calculate a predicted result before an experiment? :: To check the method is feasible and to have an expected value to compare results against.
  Predict the volume of H₂ at SLC from 0.0243 g of Mg with excess acid. :: n(Mg) = 0.00100 mol → n(H₂) = 0.00100 mol → 0.0248 L (24.8 mL).

@ 60d2a538 S.2.1 | Choosing an investigation methodology
**In a sentence**
  Choose the methodology that fits the question: experiment for cause and effect, fieldwork for real-world patterns, literature review for what's already known.
**The methodologies**
  Case study; classification and identification; controlled experiment; fieldwork; literature review; modelling; product, process or system development; simulation.
  **Case study**: an in-depth look at a particular event, situation or example (e.g. a chemical spill and its clean-up).
  **Literature review**: collating and evaluating published research on a question.
  (The others are described under Unit 2, Area of Study 3.)
**Choose to fit the question**
  Cause-and-effect questions → controlled experiment. What's there / what is it → classification and identification. What's happening in the environment → fieldwork. What is already known → literature review.
**Practice questions**
  Which methodology suits "What is known about the health effects of microplastics?"
    A literature review.
  Which methodology suits "Is the pH of rainwater lower near a busy road?"
    Fieldwork (sampling at sites near and far from the road).
**Flashcards**
  Which methodology collates and evaluates published research? :: Literature review.
  What is a case study? :: An in-depth investigation of a particular event, situation or example.
  Which methodology suits testing whether X causes a change in Y? :: A controlled experiment.

@ 22818340 S.2.2 | Designing an investigation
**In a sentence**
  A good design chooses the right equipment, sample and amount of data, and plans ahead for the errors that could creep in.
**Key ideas**
  Choose methods, equipment and procedures that fit the methodology and give data of the right type and amount.
  **Sampling**: take representative samples; enough of them (sample size) to see trends and assess precision; random or systematic sampling in fieldwork.
  **Equipment**: choose appropriate resolution (burette 0.05 mL, electronic balance 0.01 or 0.001 g, volumetric glassware for accurate volumes).
  Plan for **error and uncertainty**: repeats (at least three), controls, calibration, minimising heat or gas loss.
  Decide what qualitative and quantitative data will be recorded, and how many data points (e.g. at least five values of the independent variable for a trend).
**Practice questions**
  Why should you choose at least five values for the independent variable?
    To show a clear trend or relationship; fewer points can hide the shape of the relationship.
  Suggest how to take a representative water sample from a creek.
    Sample at several points (and depths), at the same time of day, in clean containers rinsed with creek water, and label each sample.
**Flashcards**
  Why use at least five values of the independent variable? :: To establish a clear trend or relationship on a graph.
  Why do a trial more than once? :: To check repeatability, identify outliers and reduce random error by averaging.
  What makes a sample representative? :: It reflects the whole population being studied (e.g. taken from several sites or times, not one convenient spot).

@ 768f8adc S.3.1 | Risk assessments and safety data sheets
**In a sentence**
  Use safety data sheets to identify each chemical's hazards and write a risk assessment that controls them before you start.
**Key ideas**
  A **risk assessment** lists each hazard, the risk (likelihood and severity), and control measures to reduce it.
  **Safety data sheets (SDS)** give each chemical's hazards (e.g. corrosive, flammable, toxic), handling, PPE, first aid, spill and disposal procedures — use them to inform the assessment.
  Hierarchy of control: eliminate or substitute the hazard (use a safer chemical or lower concentration) before relying on PPE.
**Practice questions**
  Name three pieces of information found on an SDS.
    Hazards, safe handling and storage, PPE, first aid, spill procedures, disposal (any three).
  Why assess risk rather than just list hazards?
    Risk considers how likely and how serious harm is in your specific procedure, so you can choose appropriate controls.
**Flashcards**
  What does a risk assessment include? :: Hazards, the risk each poses, and the control measures used to reduce the risk.
  Where do you find a chemical's hazards and first aid information? :: Its safety data sheet (SDS).
  What's a better control than PPE, where possible? :: Eliminating or substituting the hazard, e.g. using a less concentrated or less toxic chemical.

@ 07d2f53d S.3.2 | Lab health and safety
**In a sentence**
  Follow lab safety rules every time: PPE, careful handling, knowing the emergency equipment, and correct disposal.
**Key ideas**
  Follow lab rules: safety glasses, enclosed shoes, lab coat, tie back hair; no food or drink.
  Handle chemicals correctly: add acid to water (never water to concentrated acid); use fume hoods for volatile or toxic substances; heat test tubes pointing away from people.
  Know emergency equipment (eyewash, safety shower, fire blanket, extinguisher) and report accidents and spills straight away.
  Dispose of waste as directed (heavy metals and organic solvents in labelled containers).
**Practice questions**
  Why should long hair be tied back in the lab?
    It could catch fire near a Bunsen burner or dip into chemicals.
  What should you do if you spill a small amount of dilute acid on the bench?
    Tell the teacher, neutralise or dilute it as directed (e.g. with sodium hydrogen carbonate or water) and wipe it up with PPE on.
**Flashcards**
  Why add acid to water rather than water to concentrated acid? :: Dilution releases a lot of heat; adding acid to water keeps the mixture dilute so it doesn't boil and splash.
  When should a fume hood be used? :: When working with volatile, toxic or strong-smelling substances.
  What should you do immediately after a chemical splash to the eye? :: Rinse with the eyewash station for at least 15 minutes and report it.

@ a2cd6a79 S.3.3 | Ethical conduct
**In a sentence**
  Be honest with data, credit others, and minimise harm to people, animals and the environment.
**Key ideas**
  Report all data honestly — including unexpected results and outliers (explain, don't hide them). Never fabricate or falsify.
  Acknowledge others' work and assistance; don't plagiarise.
  Respect people (consent and privacy in surveys), animals and the environment (minimal harm in fieldwork, correct disposal).
**Practice questions**
  A student leaves out one titre that doesn't match. Is this ethical?
    Only if the reason is stated (e.g. overshot end point) — silently removing data is unethical.
  Why is it unethical to copy another group's results?
    It misrepresents the work as your own and the data wasn't generated by your method.
**Flashcards**
  Give two examples of ethical conduct in science. :: Reporting data honestly (no fabrication or selective deletion); acknowledging sources; minimising environmental harm.
  What should you do with an unexpected result? :: Record and report it, and discuss possible reasons — don't delete it.

@ ddf8d56b S.4.3 | Presenting data
**In a sentence**
  Pick the presentation that makes the pattern obvious: tables for raw data, bar charts for categories, line graphs for continuous variables, diagrams for set-ups.
**Choose the right form**
  **Schematic diagrams**: simple labelled drawings of apparatus (e.g. gas syringe set-up, galvanic cell).
  **Flow charts**: steps in a process (e.g. water treatment, recycling).
  **Tables**: raw and processed data with headings and units.
  **Bar charts**: categories (e.g. conductivity of different water samples).
  **Line graphs**: continuous independent variables (e.g. solubility vs temperature).
  **Calibration curves**: absorbance (or another signal) against known concentrations, used to find unknowns.
**Graph conventions**
  Title; independent variable on x-axis; labelled axes with units; even scales; line or curve of best fit.
**Practice questions**
  Which type of graph suits pH of five different brands of soft drink?
    A bar chart (brand is categorical).
  Which type of graph suits the solubility of KNO₃ from 0 °C to 100 °C?
    A line graph with a smooth curve of best fit.
**Flashcards**
  When should a bar chart be used rather than a line graph? :: When the independent variable is categorical (e.g. different brands), not continuous.
  What is a calibration curve used for? :: Finding the concentration of an unknown from its measured signal (e.g. absorbance), using standards of known concentration.
  What should every graph have? :: A title, labelled axes with units, even scales, and a line of best fit where appropriate.

@ 363e0850 S.5.1 | Processing data
**In a sentence**
  Turn raw measurements into meaning with the right calculation — means, percentages, percentage change and ratios — carrying units throughout.
**Key calculations**
  **Mean**: sum of values ÷ number of values (exclude clear outliers, and say why).
  **Percentage**: part ÷ whole × 100 (e.g. percentage composition, percentage yield).
  **Percentage change**: (new − original) ÷ original × 100.
  **Ratios**: e.g. mole ratios from equations; ratio of water to salt in a hydrate.
  Always carry units through and convert to consistent units first.
**Practice questions**
  Calculate the mean of 4.52, 4.48, 4.55 and 4.49 g.
    (4.52 + 4.48 + 4.55 + 4.49) ÷ 4 = 4.51 g
  A sample's mass falls from 12.0 g to 9.0 g. Percentage change?
    (9.0 − 12.0) ÷ 12.0 × 100 = −25%
**Flashcards**
  Formula for percentage change? :: (new − original) ÷ original × 100
  Mean of titres 20.10, 20.15 and 20.05 mL? :: 20.10 mL
  Mass of a sample drops from 5.00 g to 4.20 g. Percentage change? :: (4.20 − 5.00) ÷ 5.00 × 100 = −16.0%

@ 0af77f13 S.5.2 | Significant figures
**In a sentence**
  Your answer can only be as precise as your least precise measurement, so match significant figures to the data.
**Rules for counting**
  All non-zero digits count. Zeros between non-zero digits count (1.05 → 3). Leading zeros don't count (0.0045 → 2). Trailing zeros after a decimal point count (2.50 → 3).
**In calculations**
  **Multiplying/dividing**: give the answer to the **fewest** significant figures of the data used.
  Keep extra figures in intermediate steps; round only the final answer.
  Use scientific notation to show significant figures clearly (1.20 × 10³).
**Practice questions**
  How many significant figures in 3.050 × 10⁻³?
    Four.
  Give 0.0045678 to three significant figures.
    0.00457
**Flashcards**
  How many significant figures in 0.02050? :: Four (2, 0, 5, 0)
  Rule for significant figures when multiplying or dividing? :: Give the answer to the least number of significant figures in the data.
  Why round only at the end of a multi-step calculation? :: Rounding intermediate values adds rounding error to the final answer.
  0.0250 mol × 58.44 g mol⁻¹ = ? (correct sig figs) :: 1.46 g (3 significant figures)

@ d6096133 S.5.3 | Graphs and relationships
**In a sentence**
  Graphs reveal relationships — straight lines for linear ones, curves for non-linear — and gradients give rates.
**Key ideas**
  Plot the independent variable on x and the dependent on y.
  **Linear**: points lie on a straight line (y = mx + c). **Directly proportional**: straight line through the origin (e.g. absorbance vs concentration).
  **Non-linear**: a curve (e.g. solubility vs temperature for KNO₃, pH vs volume in a titration).
  Draw a line or smooth curve of best fit; find the gradient for rates (rise ÷ run, with units); interpolate within the data range, be cautious extrapolating.
**Practice questions**
  What does a curve that levels off in a gas volume vs time graph show?
    The reaction is slowing down and stopping as a reactant is used up.
  How do you find a rate from a straight-line graph of volume against time?
    Calculate the gradient: change in volume ÷ change in time, with units (e.g. mL s⁻¹).
**Flashcards**
  What does a straight line through the origin tell you? :: The two variables are directly proportional.
  How is a gradient calculated, and what does it represent in a volume–time graph? :: Rise ÷ run; the rate of gas production (e.g. mL s⁻¹).
  Difference between interpolation and extrapolation? :: Interpolation reads values within the range of data; extrapolation extends beyond it (less reliable).

@ e9e5861f S.5.4 | Accuracy, precision and errors
**In a sentence**
  Know the measurement words exactly — accuracy, precision, repeatability, reproducibility, resolution, validity — and tell random errors from systematic ones.
**The definitions**
  **Accuracy**: closeness to the true value. **Precision**: closeness of repeated measurements to each other.
  **Repeatability**: same person, method, equipment and conditions. **Reproducibility**: different people, equipment or labs.
  **Resolution**: smallest change an instrument can detect. **Validity**: the method measures what it intends to, fairly.
**Errors**
  **Random**: unpredictable scatter; affects precision; reduced by repeats and averaging.
  **Systematic**: consistent offset in one direction; affects accuracy; reduced by calibration or improved method.
**Practice questions**
  A balance reads 0.02 g too high for every measurement. What kind of error, and how is it fixed?
    Systematic; zero (tare) or calibrate the balance.
  Why does a thermometer with 0.1 °C resolution help in calorimetry?
    Temperature changes can be small, and finer resolution reduces the relative uncertainty.
**Flashcards**
  Titres of 23.40, 23.45 and 23.40 mL when the true value is 21.00 mL are … :: Precise but not accurate (a systematic error).
  Which error type does calibrating an instrument reduce? :: Systematic error.
  What does high reproducibility tell you? :: Different people or labs using the method get similar results, so it is reliable.

@ 1a11b3b3 S.5.5 | Outliers and incomplete data
**In a sentence**
  Spot outliers and gaps in data, investigate them, and be clear about what they mean for your conclusion.
**Key ideas**
  An **outlier** is a value that differs markedly from the others or from the trend. Check for a mistake; if there's a clear reason, exclude it (and say so); if not, repeat the measurement.
  **Contradictory** data: results that disagree with each other or with other sources — investigate method and conditions.
  **Provisional** data: preliminary, not yet confirmed (e.g. a single trial, or new research not yet replicated).
  **Incomplete** data: gaps that limit what can be concluded (too few points, missing range).
**Practice questions**
  Should you always delete an outlier? Explain.
    No — check for a known mistake first. Exclude it only with a stated reason; otherwise repeat the measurement.
  What is incomplete data, and why does it matter?
    Data with gaps (e.g. missing values or too few points), which limits what conclusions can be drawn.
**Flashcards**
  What is an outlier? :: A data point that lies well away from the other values or the trend.
  What should you do with an outlier? :: Check for a mistake; exclude it with a stated reason if justified, or repeat the measurement.
  What is provisional data? :: Preliminary data that hasn't yet been confirmed or replicated.

@ a4878ed8 S.5.6 | Repeats and precision
**In a sentence**
  Repeating measurements shows how precise they are and reduces the effect of random errors on your average.
**Key ideas**
  Repeat each measurement (at least three times). The spread (range) of repeats shows the precision; a small spread means high precision.
  In titrations, repeat until you have three **concordant** titres (within 0.10 mL).
  Repeating improves precision of the mean and helps identify outliers, but can't remove systematic error.
**Practice questions**
  Titres 22.10, 22.15, 22.40, 22.05 mL — which would you average?
    22.10, 22.15 and 22.05 (within 0.10 mL); average 22.10 mL.
  What does a large spread between repeats tell you?
    Precision is low — random errors are large.
**Flashcards**
  How do repeated trials show precision? :: A small spread (range) between repeated results indicates high precision.
  Can repeating an experiment reduce systematic error? :: No — it only reduces the effect of random error.

@ 509d39f3 S.5.7 | Evaluating and improving methods
**In a sentence**
  Evaluate your method honestly and suggest specific improvements that target the errors that mattered most.
**Key ideas**
  Identify the main sources of error in **your** method and their effect on the result.
  Suggest **specific** improvements: e.g. use a gas syringe instead of collecting over water (CO₂ dissolves); use a burette with finer resolution; calibrate the pH meter; insulate the calorimeter; increase the number of trials; control temperature with a water bath.
  Avoid vague suggestions ("be more careful", "use better equipment").
**Practice questions**
  Suggest an improvement to a titration where the end point was hard to see.
    Use a white tile under the flask, add titrant drop by drop near the end point, or use a pH meter to find the equivalence point.
  Why is "use better equipment" a weak improvement?
    It's vague — name the equipment and the error it reduces (e.g. "a burette instead of a measuring cylinder, for finer resolution").
**Flashcards**
  Suggest an improvement when collecting CO₂ over water gives low volumes. :: Use a gas syringe (CO₂ dissolves in water), or collect over warm or saturated salt water.
  Why is "be more careful" a poor improvement? :: It's vague and doesn't address a specific source of error.
  How can temperature be controlled as a variable? :: Use a thermostatted water bath and monitor with a thermometer.

@ 57a1f5a8 S.6.1 | Evidence vs opinion and anecdote
**In a sentence**
  Evidence is systematic and checkable; opinions and anecdotes aren't; and scientific ideas are the ones that can be tested.
**Key ideas**
  **Evidence**: systematically gathered, checkable data. **Opinion**: a personal view. **Anecdote**: a personal story or single case.

  "My grandad drank tank water his whole life and was fine, so it's safe." — What kind of claim is this? :: An anecdote — a single uncontrolled case, not evidence.
